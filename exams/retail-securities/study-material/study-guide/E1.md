# Element 1: Know-Your-Client (KYC) & suitability

This element covers the whole relationship between a Registered Representative (RR) and a retail client. It has four stages:
- **Open the account:** KYC, identity, the trusted contact person, account appropriateness, relationship disclosure and the welcome package.
- **Recommend:** product due diligence, know-your-product and suitability.
- **Maintain the account:** updates, trigger events and supervision.
- **Conduct:** conflicts, outside activities, personal financial dealings and the standards of conduct.

It is the largest element, with about **27 of the 120 questions**.

The content is rule-based, and the syllabus asks for no calculations. Expect short client scenarios that ask what the RR or the dealer must do, may do or must not do. This guide cites current IDPC numbers (August 2026), CIRO guidance notes and, where needed, NI 31-103.

**Terminology.**
- The syllabus says "Investment Dealer". The IDPC says "Dealer Member" and defines it so that "Mutual Fund Dealer Members are to be excluded" (IDPC 1201). This guide uses **the dealer** for both.
- An **Approved Person** is any individual that CIRO approves "to carry out a function for a Dealer Member".
- The **Registered Representative (RR)** is one type of Approved Person. CIRO approves an RR "to trade, or advise on trades, in securities or derivatives with the public in Canada, on the Dealer Member's behalf" (IDPC 1201).
- The **client** is the person who holds the account.

## 1.1 The firm–client relationship

### The client relationship model

The client relationship model (CRM) is CIRO's set of client-facing obligations. CIRO guidance (GN-3100-21-001) says that the CRM project addressed four elements:
- relationship disclosure (1.12);
- conflicts of interest management and disclosure (1.23);
- suitability assessment (1.17);
- account performance reporting (8.6, 9.2).

**Why the CRM exists.** CIRO says that each element is part of the broader obligation to deal fairly, honestly and in good faith with clients.

### Trust, agency and conflicts of interest

These are concepts, not terms that a rule defines.
- **Agent.** For listed equities, the dealer usually acts as **agent**. It executes the client's order for a commission. An agent follows lawful instructions, acts loyally and accounts for the client's property.
- **Principal.** For bonds and other over-the-counter products, the dealer usually acts as **principal**. It sells from its own inventory at a markup or spread, so it is on the other side of the client's trade.
- **Trust.** The client relies on the RR's competence and honesty, and usually cannot check the advice. This reliance explains many client duties.
- **Conflicts of interest.** The dealer and the RR earn their pay from the client's business. IDPC 3110 requires them to identify material conflicts (1.23).

### Clients in the United States and other foreign jurisdictions

> **Key rule:** The dealer must be "registered (or exempt from such registration) in the appropriate category under securities laws in each jurisdiction in which clients of the Dealer Member reside" (IDPC 2551(1)(i)). The same applies to the individual (2551(1)(ii)).

IDPC "securities laws" are Canadian laws (IDPC 1201). So the rule covers the provinces and territories where clients reside. It sets no foreign registration requirement.

**Snowbirds** spend the winter in the United States or elsewhere, but usually stay resident in Canada (concept). If a client becomes resident abroad, that country's rules can apply. Those rules are outside this exam's sources, so the RR involves compliance.

### Considerations for changes in residence

**The client moves to another province or territory.** IDPC 2551(1) now applies to the new province. The NI 31-103 client mobility exemptions let the dealer and the RR keep the client without registration there. In each other jurisdiction:
- an individual may serve **5 or fewer** eligible clients;
- a dealer may serve **10 or fewer** eligible clients.

An **eligible client** is an individual who was a client immediately before becoming resident in the other jurisdiction. That client's spouse or child is also an eligible client.

For the individual exemption, all of these conditions also apply:
1. the individual and the sponsoring firm are each registered in their principal jurisdiction;
2. the individual acts only as that registration permits;
3. the individual complies with Part 13 (dealing with clients);
4. the individual acts fairly, honestly and in good faith with the eligible client;
5. before the individual first acts, the dealer tells the client that the individual is exempt and "not subject to requirements otherwise applicable under local securities legislation".

The sponsoring firm files Form 31-103F3 as soon as possible after the individual first relies on the exemption. It supervises these individuals and keeps records of compliance. A dealer with more than 10 eligible clients in a jurisdiction must register there.

**The client leaves Canada.** CRA rules for emigrants apply:
- **Departure tax.** CRA treats the client as if they sold certain property at fair market value. This rule excludes registered plans and pensions.
- **TFSA.** The client may keep it, and withdrawals stay tax-free. A new contribution while non-resident can have a monthly tax.
- **RRSP.** Unless a tax treaty reduces it, the withholding on a withdrawal is 25%.

Either move can change tax, income or liquidity. So it can be a significant KYC change (1.7) and a suitability trigger (3402(2)(iii)) (1.21).

> **Exam trap:** The mobility exemption starts when the **client** moves, not when the RR moves.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Astrid Lunde, a client of an Ontario RR, retires to British Columbia. The RR already serves three eligible clients in BC and is not registered there. The dealer is registered in BC.
>
> **Question.** Can the RR keep Astrid?
>
> **Resolution.** Yes, under the individual mobility exemption. Astrid was a client just before the move, so she is an eligible client. She is the RR's fourth in BC, within the limit of five.
>
> The dealer meets 2551(1) through its BC registration. Before the RR first acts, the dealer discloses the exemption, and it files Form 31-103F3 as soon as possible. Her retirement is also a KYC change.

*Sources: GN-3100-21-001; IDPC 1201, 2551(1), 3110, 3402(2); NI 31-103 ss. 1.1, 2.2, 8.30; 31-103CP ss. 2.2, 8.30; CRA emigrant and departure-tax pages; CRA TFSA non-resident pages; CRA RRSP withholding page; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.2 KYC information required

### Why KYC matters

IDPC 3202(1) requires the dealer to "learn and remain informed of the essential facts relative to every order, account and client it accepts." KYC has two purposes:
- **Suitability.** KYC is the input to every suitability determination (1.17). Without adequate and timely KYC, the dealer cannot meet that obligation.
- **Gatekeeping.** The dealer also collects KYC as a gatekeeper of the markets, for example for anti-money laundering.

KYC is specific to each client. For example, do not give a client the investment knowledge of the client's spouse.

### What the dealer must establish

1. **Identity.** If the dealer has cause for concern, it also makes reasonable inquiries into the client's reputation.
2. **Insider status.** Is the client an insider of a publicly traded issuer? Guidance says to explain what an insider is, and then to ask.
3. **Suitability information.** Sufficient information on six factors: personal circumstances, financial circumstances, investment needs and objectives, investment knowledge, risk profile and time horizon.
4. **Creditworthiness.** This applies if the dealer finances the client's purchase.

### Financial circumstances

This factor covers these items:
- annual income;
- **liquidity needs**;
- financial assets;
- **net worth** (assets and liabilities);
- **borrowing or leverage to invest**.

The RR may need to ask about investments, other assets and debts that the client holds outside the dealer.

**Liquidity needs.** Ask three questions:
- Does the client have other means to cover expenses?
- Are the needs expected or unexpected?
- When the need comes, will the client withdraw money regularly?

**Borrowing to invest.** Ask whether the client uses borrowed money to invest. If so, gather information on the client's ability to meet the debt obligations. Leverage magnifies losses (1.10).

### Personal circumstances

For an individual, this covers these items:
- age and address;
- family situation and dependants;
- employment.

It also covers anyone else who can instruct on the account or has a financial interest in it. For an entity, it covers the type of entity, its constating documents and its business, and the persons who may instruct (1.4).

### Investment knowledge

Knowledge is the client's understanding of markets, of the risks and limits of investment types, and of the risk–return link. If the answers do not agree, ask more questions. For example, a client may claim little knowledge but want a high level of risk.

### Risk profile

The risk profile combines risk tolerance and risk capacity. Section 1.3 explains how to assess it.

### Investment objectives and needs

Objectives include capital preservation, income, growth, or investing by personal values. Let the client describe goals in their own terms. Examples are a retirement lifestyle or a home purchase.

### Investment time horizon

The dealer checks that the stated horizon is feasible, given the client's liquidity needs, age, objectives and risk profile. A short horizon may mean that only conservative investments are suitable.

### Investment constraints and restrictions

- **ESG.** Guidance recognizes investing "in accordance with environmental, social and governance criteria or other personal preferences."
- **EDI.** The syllabus adds equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI) considerations, such as a preference for issuers with diverse boards. No rule defines EDI, so record it as a client preference or restriction.
- **Other personal preferences.** Record each restriction, such as an excluded sector, so that every recommendation respects it.

> **Exam trap:** Never manipulate KYC to match a product or to justify a pre-determined recommendation. Do not pre-fill KYC questions before you talk to the client. Do not ask about two KYC factors in one question.

*Sources: IDPC 3202(1); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.1, 2.2.3–2.2.5, 2.3.1–2.3.6, 2.12*

## 1.3 Assessing the client's risk profile

Guidance splits the risk profile into "willingness to accept risk, sometimes referred to as risk tolerance, and ability to endure financial loss, sometimes referred to as risk capacity."

> **Key rule:** "The client's risk profile should reflect the lower of the client's assessed risk tolerance and risk capacity." (GN-3400-21-004, s. 2.3.5)

**Why the lower of the two.** A client who panics can sell at the bottom, which is a tolerance failure. A loss can also damage an essential goal, which is a capacity failure. The profile must respect both limits (concept).

### Willingness to accept risk (risk tolerance)

Tolerance asks how much loss and volatility the client will accept. Preference, knowledge and experience drive it. Behavioural factors also influence it, such as loss aversion, overconfidence, recent markets and the framing of questions (concept; biases: 7.3). So check stated tolerance against the client's knowledge and past behaviour.

### Ability to endure financial loss (risk capacity)

Capacity asks how much loss the client can absorb. It depends on these facts:
- the financial situation;
- how much of the client's total investments the account or position represents;
- current investments;
- age and life stage;
- the investment horizon and the need for liquidity.

### How much risk is needed (risk need)

Risk need is a planning concept, not a rule. It depends on the required rate of return, the market risk environment and the consequence of failure. Guidance says that an RR should consider a statement of the investment return assumptions that the client's objectives require. Risk need can show that a goal is unrealistic, but it never lifts the profile above the lower of tolerance and capacity.

### A thorough process

A thorough process does four things:
- assesses both tolerance and capacity;
- interprets answers without too much weight on any single answer;
- checks that the products and services in the account fit the profile;
- identifies clients better suited to cash deposits or guaranteed products.

Questions must be "fair, clear and not misleading". Document the questions and the answers. The RR must not influence the client's answers.

### Resolving conflicts between expectations and the risk profile

When the client expects more return than the risk profile supports, the guidance expects this response (GN-3400-21-004; same steps in 7.5):

1. Consider "a detailed discussion with the client on the relationship between risk and return".
2. Do not override the risk profile or other KYC information "to meet a client’s expectations for returns".
3. Identify mismatches between needs and objectives, risk tolerance and risk capacity, and revisit them with the client.
4. If the goals need more risk than the client can take, "clearly explain the alternatives available". Examples are to save more, wait longer or lower the target.
5. Ask whether the client has the capacity and tolerance for the losses and volatility of a higher-risk portfolio. If not, explain that the higher return "cannot realistically be met". In that case, the higher-risk portfolio is unsuitable.
6. Document the interaction and the end result.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Tomasz Wierzbicki, 61, plans to retire at 64 on modest savings and no pension. He wants a 9% annual return and says he is "fine with risk". But he sold all his equities in the last market fall.
>
> **Question.** What profile should the RR record?
>
> **Resolution.** His past behaviour suggests lower tolerance than he states. His capacity is low, because a loss would harm his retirement. The profile is the lower of the two, so it is low. The 9% target is his risk need, and it does not raise the profile. The RR explains risk and return, offers alternatives such as a later retirement, and documents the discussion.

*Sources: GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.3–2.3.5; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.4 Business structures and investment opportunities

**Why the structure matters.** The structure tells the dealer whose money it is, who may give instructions and who controls the entity. These answers decide whose KYC applies and whom the dealer must identify.

### Sole proprietorship

CRA defines it as "An unincorporated business entirely owned by one person." The owner has unlimited liability (concept). Business debts and cash needs are the owner's, so they shape the owner's capacity and liquidity. The business also ends when the owner dies.

### Partnership

CRA describes two or more persons who carry on a business together and share profits or losses, usually under a partnership agreement. General partners have unlimited liability, and limited partners risk only their investment (concept). The dealer establishes the partnership's existence and business, and identifies the individuals who control it (IDPC 3203). The agreement sets who may instruct.

### Corporation (private vs public)

A corporation is a separate legal entity with limited liability: "Shareholders are not responsible for a corporation's debts". It has continuous existence, and it pays tax separately from its owners. This structure creates investment opportunities:
- it can raise money by issuing shares or bonds;
- corporate tax rates are generally lower than personal rates, so more profit can stay invested (concept).

A **public** corporation is generally a Canadian-resident corporation with shares listed on a designated Canadian exchange. A **private** corporation, for example a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC), has no listed shares. Owners of a public company may be insiders, with trading restrictions.

For a corporate account, the dealer obtains the directors' names within 30 days. It identifies holders of **25% or more** of the voting rights (IDPC 3204). The account needs a separate application with the corporation's own KYC.

### Co-operative

A co-operative is a member-based business. It can incorporate in one of two ways (concept):
- federally, under the Canada Cooperatives Act, with a filing at Corporations Canada;
- provincially, under provincial co-operatives law.

It is democratically controlled, typically one member one vote, and returns surpluses to members (concept). Member shares are usually illiquid. Investment authority comes from the by-laws and the board.

CIPF generally treats a corporation or partnership as separate from its owners, with exceptions for certain personal holding entities (1.14).

*Sources: CRA business pages ("Sole proprietorships and partnerships", definitions, "Type of corporation"); ISED "Benefits of incorporating", "Co-operatives"; IDPC 3203, 3204; GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.1, 2.7.2; CIPF coverage page; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.5 Documenting discussions and client confirmation

**Why it matters.** Records let the client check that the dealer understood them. They also let the dealer show that it met its obligations (concept).

Within a reasonable time after it collects KYC, the dealer "must take reasonable steps to have a client confirm the accuracy of such information" (IDPC 3202(3)). The confirmation covers the information collected at opening and any significant change to it later.

> **Remember:**
> - Evidence of confirmation is the client's signature (handwritten, electronic or digital) or file notes of the client's instructions.
> - Verify changes in writing and give the client a chance to correct them.
> - Use extra controls for changes to name, address or banking information, where fraud risk is higher.
> - Record the date of collection and keep evidence of the confirmation.
> - Document the risk-profile questions and answers, and any discussion of mismatched expectations.
> - Records must document correspondence with clients and demonstrate KYC and suitability compliance (IDPC 3804(2)(xii), (xiv)).

### Why confirmation and dating matter

- **Accuracy.** Every suitability determination depends on the KYC (1.17). Without confirmation, a client can later deny the KYC (concept).
- **Currency.** As a best practice, the dealer records the date of collection. The date shows that the KYC was current, and when the 3209(4) review is due (1.7).
- **Meaningful interaction.** Guidance says that KYC is not a "tick the box" exercise. The obligation is the same for a meeting, a phone call or an online form.
- **Tools.** The dealer may use questionnaires or other tools, but it stays responsible for the KYC process. The tools must accurately reflect the client's information (1.2).

Documentation continues after opening. For example, the RR documents the disclosure of a recommendation's material positive and negative factors (1.17).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Bianca Russo phones her RR to say that she lost her job. The RR changes her income and objectives in the dealer's system.
>
> **Question.** What else must the dealer do?
>
> **Resolution.** The dealer confirms the changes with Bianca in writing and gives her a chance to correct them. It keeps dated evidence of her confirmation, such as her signature or a file note. The change is significant, so the dealer approves it like a new account application (1.7). If Bianca later disputes a trade, the record shows what the dealer knew.

*Sources: IDPC 3202(3), 3209(4), 3215(1), 3804(2); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.5, 2.6.1, 2.6.2, 2.11.2, 3.2.1; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.6 Trusted contact person and temporary holds

### Definitions (IDPC 3201(3))

- A **trusted contact person (TCP)** is an individual named by the client, whom the dealer may contact in accordance with the client's written consent.
- A **vulnerable client** "might have an illness, impairment, disability or aging-process limitation that places the client at risk of financial exploitation."
- **Financial exploitation** is the use or control of a person's financial asset, or the denial of it. It happens through undue influence, unlawful conduct or another wrongful act.

**Why the TCP exists.** RRs often see the first signs of exploitation. CSA guidance calls the TCP "a resource for a registrant to assist in protecting a client's financial interests or assets".

### How to establish a TCP

When it collects KYC from an individual client, the dealer takes reasonable steps to get a TCP's name and contact information (IDPC 3202(4)). It also gets the client's written consent to contact the TCP about:
1. possible financial exploitation;
2. the client's mental capacity to make financial decisions;
3. the client's legal representative;
4. the client's contact information.

CSA guidance adds these points:
- a client may name more than one TCP;
- the TCP should not be the client's RR on the account;
- the client may name the attorney under their power of attorney (POA). But the dealer should encourage a TCP who is not involved in account decisions.

### What a TCP permits and does not permit

The dealer may contact the TCP about the four matters, within the client's consent. CIRO's investor brochure says that a TCP "acts like an emergency contact for your account, although they cannot make financial decisions or account changes." The Relationship Disclosure explains when the dealer might disclose information to the TCP (1.12).

### Actions if the client refuses to provide one

The dealer can still open the account. But it must take reasonable steps, such as to explain the purpose and ask. It may ask why the client refuses. It keeps records that show compliance (NI 31-103 s. 11.5(2)(l)). At each KYC update, it asks the client again.

### When to contact a TCP

Speak to the client first. If there are warning signs of exploitation or lost capacity, contact the TCP within the client's consent. If repeated attempts to reach the client fail, and this is unusual, the dealer may ask the TCP to confirm the contact information. Never contact a TCP who may be involved in the exploitation. Consider the police or the public guardian and trustee instead.

### How to recognize capacity and exploitation concerns

- **Exploitation:** sudden withdrawals, an unexplained jump to high risk, new "helpers", sudden POA or will changes, isolation.
- **Lost capacity:** memory loss, trouble with forms, confusion over basic terms, missed appointments.

One sign alone may not show a problem. The CSA does not expect the RR or the dealer to be the final arbiter of capacity. A hold needs only a reasonable belief (below).

### Conditions for using temporary holds

A temporary hold is a hold on a purchase or sale, or on a withdrawal or transfer of cash or securities (IDPC 1201). The dealer may place it only if it reasonably believes **either**:
- the client is vulnerable **and** financial exploitation "has occurred, is occurring, has been attempted or will be attempted"; **or**
- the client lacks the mental capacity to make financial decisions (IDPC 3222(1)–(2)).

The dealer must then (3222(3)):
1. document the facts and reasons;
2. notify the client, with reasons, as soon as possible;
3. review the relevant facts as soon as possible after it places the hold, and then on a reasonably frequent basis;
4. **within 30 days**, and every 30 days after, either revoke it or notify the client that it continues, with reasons.

Records must demonstrate compliance (3804(2)(xix)). CSA guidance says that the hold should cover the specific transaction, not the whole account. If the transaction involves all the assets in the account, a hold on the whole account may be reasonable. Even then, the dealer does not stop the payment of regular expenses. When the reasonable belief ends, the hold must end.

### Documenting the discussion

Record each discussion with the client or the TCP about these concerns: the concern, the date and the result. CSA expects TCP policies to cover "how to document discussions with a TCP" (IDPC 3804(2)(xiv); NI 31-103 s. 11.5(2)(n)).

> **Exam trap:** A hold is not the tool for an unsuitable instruction; that is IDPC 3402(5) (1.18). A poor decision alone is not exploitation. Never use a hold to delay a withdrawal because the dealer fears that it will lose the client.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Odette Lamarche, 84 and recently widowed, has named her daughter as TCP. A new "friend" now attends every meeting and answers for her. Odette asks to transfer $150,000 to the friend, but cannot say why.
>
> **Question.** What should the RR and the dealer do?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. The RR speaks with Odette alone and escalates the concern.
> 2. The dealer may contact her daughter, who is not suspected, within Odette's consent.
> 3. If the dealer reasonably believes that Odette is vulnerable and exploitation is attempted, it may hold this transfer.
> 4. It then meets 3222(3): notice with reasons, frequent review, a decision every 30 days, and records.

*Sources: IDPC 1201, 3201(3), 3202(4)–(5), 3216(5)(ii)(n)–(o), 3222, 3804(2)(xiv), (xix); NI 31-103 s. 11.5(2)(l), (n); 31-103CP Appendix G; CIRO brochure "Opening an Investment Account"*

## 1.7 Primary responsibility, no delegation, keeping KYC current

### The primary responsibility

> **Key rule:** KYC compliance "is primarily the responsibility of the Registered Representative, Portfolio Manager or Associate Portfolio Manager assigned to the client account" and "must not be delegated to any other person" (IDPC 3209(1)–(2)).

The dealer remains ultimately responsible for KYC for all its clients.

### Prohibition on KYC delegation

KYC is an input to the suitability determination. For this reason, the suitability KYC may not come from an affiliate or an unregistered assistant. Identification information may come from others. In the same way, an RR must not delegate the suitability determination itself (IDPC 3406(2)).

**The team approach.** A dealer may assign several RRs to the same account. Then any RR on the team may collect the suitability KYC.

**Why no delegation.** Information from a third party can be incomplete, out of date or collected for another purpose (concept).

### Obligation to keep KYC current

Guidance considers KYC current if it is up to date enough to support a suitability determination and the dealer's other regulatory obligations.

- **Significant change.** If the dealer learns of a significant change, it updates the KYC within a reasonable time (3209(3)). Guidance says that a significant change includes a change to:
  - the risk profile;
  - the investment time horizon;
  - the investment needs and objectives;
  - anything else that could reasonably have a significant impact on net worth or income.
- **Effect of the change.** A significant change can make the KYC insufficient for suitability. An example is a job loss that changes the risk profile and objectives. The dealer should then consider a restriction to liquidating trades, transfers or disbursements until it re-evaluates the KYC (1.21).
- **Finding changes.** The dealer makes reasonable enquiries to find significant changes. The RR can ask at a portfolio review, or when the RR discusses other account matters with the client.
- **Approval of changes.** The dealer approves significant changes to client information in the same manner as a new account application (IDPC 3215(1)).
- **Review cycle (3209(4)).** Review at least every **36 months**, and every **12 months** for managed and discretionary accounts. If nothing changes, keep evidence of the review.
- **More frequent updates.** The dealer should consider them if it distributes exempt market securities. It should also consider them for accounts with a significant position in high-risk, complex or illiquid products.
- **Independent check.** The dealer's procedures for verifying significant changes must be independent of the RR (IDPC 3213(2)(iv)).

**What goes wrong.** Stale KYC makes the suitability obligation impossible to meet. A recommendation can seem suitable in the records when it no longer fits the client.

*Sources: IDPC 3209, 3213(2)(iv), 3215(1), 3406(2); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.5, 2.11, 3.9*

## 1.8 Client account records

IDPC 3210 defines **client account records** as information, disclosures and agreements that the dealer must give to the client or get from the client. They include identity verification, the account appropriateness assessment, KYC information and the account application.

**Why they matter.** Together, the records prove that the dealer opened the account correctly. They give the designated Supervisor a complete file to approve (concept).

### Verification of identity

The dealer keeps documentation that it established the client's identity (IDPC 3202(1)(i), 3210(1)(i)). Guidance calls identification the first step in knowing the client. For beneficial owners and controlling individuals of entity accounts, the dealer uses methods that let it "form a reasonable belief it knows the identity" of each individual. It keeps a record of how it confirmed the identity (IDPC 3206(2)).

The dealer must establish the identity of each such individual as soon as practicable, and within 30 days of opening (3206(3)). If it cannot, it restricts the account to liquidating trades, transfers, paying out funds or delivering securities. The restriction stays until the dealer establishes the individual's identity (3206(4)).

### Account appropriateness assessment

The dealer keeps documentation that supports its 3211 determination (1.9).

### KYC information

The dealer keeps the 3202(1) information, confirmed by the client (1.5).

### Acknowledgement of disclosures

Records must show that the dealer provided the required account documents (3804(2)(xi)). Two examples need proof of receipt:
- **Leverage risk disclosure.** It warns that "Using borrowed money to finance the purchase of securities involves greater risk than using cash resources only." The dealer gets the client's positive acknowledgement of receipt (3217(1)(ii)).
- **Derivatives risk disclosure statement.** The dealer gets the client's acknowledgement of receipt (3254(1)(ii)).

### Record of trusted contact person refusal

No IDPC section names this record. NI 31-103 requires records that demonstrate compliance with the TCP section (s. 11.5(2)(l)).

### Approval and missing records

- A designated Supervisor must not approve a new account until the dealer has all client account records (3214(2)).
- Approval is due no later than one business day after the initial trade (1.11).
- If the designated Supervisor does not approve the account after the initial trade, the dealer restricts it "to only liquidating trades, transfers out, paying out funds or delivering securities to the client". The restriction stays until final approval (3214(5)).
- The dealer takes specific action on supporting documents still missing **25 business days** after opening (3213(2)(iii)). That is a follow-up deadline, not a window for approval.

*Sources: IDPC 3202(1)(i), 3206, 3210, 3213(2), 3214(2), (5), 3217, 3254, 3804(2)(xi); NI 31-103 s. 11.5(2)(l); 31-103CP Appendix G; GN-3400-21-004 s. 2.2.2*

## 1.9 Account appropriateness

> **Key rule:** Before opening an account, the dealer must determine, "on a reasonable basis and putting the person's interest first", that opening it is appropriate. It must also determine that "the scope of products, services and account relationships which the person would have access to within the account are appropriate for the person" (IDPC 3211(1)).

**Why it exists.** The account decides what the client can do and how the client pays. A wrong account can harm a client before any trade, such as margin access for a novice (concept).

### Applying the obligation

The assessment covers:
- the type of service, such as order-execution-only (OEO), full-service or managed;
- account relationships, such as margin, options, futures, fee-based or commission-based;
- the compensation option;
- strategies such as leverage.

The dealer explains the features and costs of its account types and recommends the one that puts the client first. It need not consider other dealers' accounts. When it chooses the compensation option, it must also address any conflict in the client's best interest (1.23).

If nothing that the dealer offers is appropriate, it should not open the account. If only some products are appropriate, it restricts access.

### Exemptions

- OEO and direct electronic access accounts: only the question "may this person open this account here" applies.
- Carried accounts: the introducing broker, not the carrying broker, meets the obligation.
- Accounts of dealers, banks and insurers: exempt, because these clients are sophisticated.

### Examples from guidance

- A client who wants to make every decision should not have a managed account.
- For a client who will rarely trade, consider whether a commission-based account is more appropriate.
- A novice with low capacity who wants margin and options needs those relationships restricted.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Aurelie Gagnon, 72, holds bank shares and GICs and trades about twice a year. Her RR suggests a fee-based account, which pays the RR more.
>
> **Question.** Is the fee-based account appropriate?
>
> **Resolution.** Probably not. She would pay an ongoing fee for little activity, so the RR should consider whether a commission-based account is more appropriate. The RR's higher pay is a conflict to address in her best interest (1.23). The dealer explains both options and their costs, and recommends the one that puts Aurelie first.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Lev Antonov, 24, has $8,000 of savings and no investment experience. He asks for a margin account with options trading.
>
> **Question.** What should the dealer do?
>
> **Resolution.** His knowledge and capacity are low, so margin and options are not appropriate for him. Other services may still fit, so the dealer need not refuse him. It can open an advisory cash account without margin or options access, and explain why. If his situation changes, the dealer reassesses later (1.20).

*Sources: IDPC 3211; GN-3400-21-004 ss. 3.3.1, 3.3.3, 3.3.4; IIROC Notice 17-0093*

## 1.10 Account types

### Definitions (IDPC 1201)

- **Advisory account:** "the client is responsible for all investment decisions but is able to rely on advice given by a Registered Representative."
- **Managed account:** a Portfolio Manager or Associate Portfolio Manager makes decisions on a continuing basis.
- **Discretionary account:** the client grants authority that the dealer did not solicit. The dealer accepts it "to accommodate a client who is frequently or temporarily unavailable to authorize trades." It lasts no more than 12 months and is not renewable.

### Advisory (non-managed) account: commission-based and fee-based

The client makes each decision with the RR's advice, and every action needs a suitability determination (1.17).
- **Commission-based.** The client pays per trade. It is cheap for a client who trades rarely, but the cost rises with activity. Per-trade pay can tempt an RR to trade too much (churning: 1.17).
- **Fee-based (% of assets).** The cost is predictable, and the RR gains when the assets grow. But a buy-and-hold client pays ongoing fees without matching service. Payout grids often favour fee-based revenue. So the fee or commission choice must be appropriate at opening and on an ongoing basis.

### Managed account

The client gets continuous professional management, but gives up control and pays fees. The main rules (IDPC 3277–3281) are:
- a written agreement that describes the client's KYC and restrictions;
- written approval of the account by the designated Supervisor;
- the dealer's fair-allocation policy, given to the client;
- a KYC review at least every 12 months;
- a written agreement for fees based on volume or performance.

### Discretionary account

It lets trading continue while the client is unavailable, but it is temporary only. The rules control the risk of trades that the client did not approve:
- the agreement lasts at most 12 months and is not renewable;
- the RR needs at least 2 years' active experience with all product types traded on discretion (3275(1));
- the designated Supervisor approves the account and the signed agreement (3273(1)(v));
- the account must not hold publicly traded securities of the dealer or its affiliates.

Nobody may use discretion in other accounts, except time and price discretion for best execution (3221) (8.5).

### Cash account vs margin account

| | Cash account | Margin account |
|---|---|---|
| Payment | Full payment or delivery by settlement date (Form 1, Sch. 4) (8.4) | Borrows against the loan value of the securities |
| Advantages | Simple; no borrowing | Leverage; short selling |
| Disadvantages | No leverage or shorting | Magnified losses, interest, margin calls |
| Key rules | Settle on settlement date | Signed margin agreement before opening; client made aware of risks and benefits (3246–3247); restrictions on an undermargined account (Form 1, Sch. 4, Note 5) |

Loan values and rates are in 6.5, and margin account operation is in 8.5.

> **Exam trap:** Long-term portfolio management needs a **managed** account. Discretionary authority is a short-term, non-renewable accommodation.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Gustavo Pereira, a ship's engineer, is at sea for months at a time and cannot authorize trades. He wants trades in his account to continue while he is away.
>
> **Question.** Which arrangement fits?
>
> **Resolution.** He is frequently unavailable, so a discretionary account can accommodate him, under all the rules above. The agreement lasts at most 12 months and is not renewable. If Gustavo wants continuous management for many years, a managed account fits better.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Farida Rahimi, an experienced investor with high capacity, wants to borrow to buy shares and to sell a stock short.
>
> **Question.** What does she need?
>
> **Resolution.** Borrowing and short selling need a margin account.
> 1. The dealer gets a signed margin agreement before opening, and makes her aware of the risks and benefits.
> 2. She will borrow to invest, so the dealer gives her the leverage risk disclosure statement. It gets her positive acknowledgement of receipt (3217) (1.8).
> 3. Each trade still needs a suitability determination.

*Sources: IDPC 1201, 3217, 3221, 3246, 3247, 3273–3281; IDPC Form 1 Schedule 4 Notes 5 and 7; IIROC Notice 17-0093; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.11 Account-opening documentation

### The dealer's business model, client relationships and products

Guidance says that the depth of KYC enquiries can vary with three things. KYC must support the suitability obligation, and that need differs from dealer to dealer.

**Business model.** A dealer that offers only OEO services makes no recommendations, so it needs no suitability KYC (see below). A full-service advisory dealer needs the full suitability KYC.

**Relationship with clients.** A fully customized service or complex finances call for more detail. Simple model portfolios for clients with straightforward finances call for less.

**Products and services offered.** Illiquid or high-risk products may require more information on the client's finances, including holdings at other financial institutions.

OEO, direct electronic access and institutional accounts need no suitability KYC. But they still need the essential facts, anti-money-laundering information and creditworthiness where the dealer gives credit.

**Typical advisory package (concept).** It contains the application with confirmed KYC, identity verification, TCP details and consent, and the Relationship Disclosure. It also contains conflicts and fee disclosure, the complaint summary and the brochure. Where applicable, it adds a margin agreement, risk disclosures, or a managed or discretionary agreement.

### Timing

- The dealer assigns an account number only when it knows the client's full name and address.
- The complete application is due by the next business day.
- The designated Supervisor approves the account no later than one business day after the first trade (IDPC 3214).

### Conditions for one set of KYC for multiple accounts

One set of KYC can serve several accounts (for example cash, margin and RRSP) if all these conditions apply:
1. the beneficial owner is identical;
2. the same RR or team handles all the accounts;
3. the dealer can supervise them together;
4. the client acknowledges that the dealer may assess suitability across them.

**Joint, corporate and trust accounts** need separate applications:
- a joint account has different beneficial owners;
- a corporation owns its account, not its beneficial owner;
- a trust is separate from its beneficiaries, and its trustee usually sets its objectives.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Zainab Yusuf has a cash account and an RRSP with one RR. She wants to add a margin account, a joint account with her husband and an account for her holding company.
>
> **Question.** How many applications does the dealer need?
>
> **Resolution.** The cash, RRSP and margin accounts have one owner and one RR. One application can cover them if two more conditions apply:
> - the dealer can supervise them together;
> - Zainab acknowledges that the dealer may assess suitability across all three accounts.
>
> The joint account and the holding company account each need a separate application.

*Sources: IDPC 3208, 3214; GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.7, 2.4, 2.7; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.12 Relationship Disclosure

### Objective

Relationship disclosure information (RDI) is "a written communication from the Dealer Member to the client describing the products and services offered by the Dealer Member, the nature of the account and the manner in which the account will operate and the responsibilities of the Dealer Member to the client." The dealer must give it to **retail** clients. It need not give it to institutional clients.

**Why it exists.** CIRO's intent is that all clients have answers to basic questions about the account relationship, even if they do not ask. A client who knows what to expect can see when the dealer does not meet its obligations (concept).

### Frequency of provision

The dealer provides the RDI at account opening. It provides it again when there is a significant change to the information.

### Form and format

- **Form.** The dealer can customize the RDI for each client, or standardize it by class of client. A standardized RDI must still fit the client. Several accounts may share a combined RDI if appropriate.
- **Format.** It must be in writing and in plain language. It contains all required content, or refers to the other documents that contain it. It may be a separate document or part of the account-opening package.

### Content

> **Remember:** RDI content (IDPC 3216(5)) is titled "Relationship Disclosure" and covers:
> - products and services, including resale restrictions and fund ongoing fees, and any limits (such as proprietary products);
> - the account type and who decides;
> - the suitability process and its triggers, and whether market fluctuations trigger reviews;
> - account reporting (8.6, 9.2);
> - the conflicts statement;
> - third-party benefits;
> - account and transaction charges and their compounding impact;
> - account documents;
> - complaint procedures and brochure (7.12);
> - benchmarks (9.2);
> - when the dealer might disclose client or account information to the TCP;
> - temporary holds.

An OEO account may omit the suitability description, if the dealer makes the OEO disclosure that section 3241 requires (3216(5)(iii)).

### Review

A partner, Director, officer or designated Supervisor approves the RDI. For standardized documents, the designated Supervisor ensures that each client gets the right version. If the dealer customizes the RDI for each client, the designated Supervisor must approve each document (3216(6)(i)).

*Sources: IDPC 3216; GN-3100-21-001; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.13 Containment of confidential information

### Why containment matters

Dealers receive **material non-public information (MNPI)** from these sources (concept):
- investment banking mandates;
- board seats;
- research contacts;
- insider clients.

CIRO says that containment matters for every dealer, not only for dealers that do investment banking. The aim is to prevent illegal insider trading. Trading on MNPI, or tipping it, damages market integrity (insider trading prohibition: 8.2).

> **Key rule:** Some people must not disclose MNPI "to anyone including any Directors, Executives, employees or clients, or research or trading departments of the Dealer Member unless in the necessary course of business" (IDPC 3508(2)–(4)). This applies to a representative of the dealer who is an issuer's director, or who acts in an underwriting or advisory role. It also applies to colleagues whom they properly tell.

### Information barriers and firewalls

**Information barriers (firewalls)** are "physical and procedural means of restricting access to confidential information to those needing to know it and of recording who gets access to it and when." They include:
- an accountable senior officer;
- staff undertakings;
- physical and electronic barriers;
- logged "wall-crossings".

### Grey and restricted lists

| | Grey (watch) list | Restricted list |
|---|---|---|
| Contents | Issuers on which the dealer holds confidential information | Issuers with which the dealer has a **current, publicly disclosed** involvement |
| Circulation | Highly confidential, limited | Distributed to staff who take orders |
| Effect | Silent monitoring of trading for leaks | Restrictions on trading and advising |

**Why the difference.** The grey list stays secret because the deal is not public, and a list would itself leak it. The restricted list can circulate because the involvement is already public (concept).

### The role of investment banking, the research department and corporate finance

- **Investment banking and corporate finance** raise capital and advise issuers. They are "private side" (concept).
- **Research** must stay independent. Analysts may not get pay that is directly based on a specific investment banking transaction (IDPC 3620(2)). Investment banking may not approve reports, may correct only factual errors, and gets no advance notice of rating changes (3621).
- **Retail RRs** are "public side" (concept).

### Cybersecurity

A cybersecurity incident is unauthorized access to, or disruption or misuse of, the dealer's information system. It counts if it causes, or is reasonably likely to cause, substantial harm or other listed effects. The dealer reports it to CIRO in writing:
- **within three calendar days** of discovering the incident;
- with a follow-up report **within 30 calendar days** of discovery, unless CIRO agrees otherwise (IDPC 3703(2)(vii)).

### Privacy

PIPEDA governs private-sector collection, use and disclosure of personal information. Its ten principles are:
1. accountability;
2. identifying purposes;
3. consent;
4. limiting collection;
5. limiting use, disclosure and retention;
6. accuracy;
7. safeguards;
8. openness;
9. individual access;
10. challenging compliance.

If a breach poses a real risk of significant harm, the dealer reports it to the Privacy Commissioner and notifies the affected individuals.

### How to analyze a containment problem

1. What is the information: MNPI, or personal client data?
2. Who has it, and who should have it?
3. Which control applies: a barrier, a list or privacy safeguards?
4. What must follow: no trade or tip, escalation to compliance, or a report?

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Theo Brannigan, a retail RR, hears two bankers in the elevator discuss the dealer's work on a takeover of Delta Mining. An hour later, a client asks him whether Delta Mining is a good buy.
>
> **Question.** What should Theo do?
>
> **Resolution.** The information is MNPI, and Theo did not receive it in the necessary course of business. He must not trade, recommend or hint, to this client or anyone else. He reports what he heard to compliance immediately. The bankers' careless talk is itself a containment failure that the dealer must address.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A phishing email gives an outsider access to client names and account numbers for two days.
>
> **Question.** What must the dealer report?
>
> **Resolution.** This is a cybersecurity incident with a reasonable likelihood of substantial harm. The dealer reports it to CIRO in writing within three calendar days, then within 30 calendar days. The breach also creates a real risk of significant harm. So the dealer reports to the Privacy Commissioner and notifies the affected clients.

*Sources: GN-3500-21-001; IDPC 3508, 3620, 3621, 3703; PIPEDA s. 3, s. 10.1, Schedule 1; OPC fair information principles; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.14 The welcome package

**Why the package exists.** It gives the client the facts needed to use the account safely: costs, protections, risks, conflicts and how to complain (concept).

### Fee schedule

The RDI describes all account fees and transaction charges (IDPC 3216(5)(ii)(h)–(i)). Except in a managed account, the dealer discloses these items before it accepts a retail client's instruction to buy or sell (3218(1)):
- the charges;
- any deferred sales charge;
- trailing commissions;
- fund ongoing fees.

### CIRO brochures

- **CIRO Complaints Brochure (mandatory).** It explains how to complain to the dealer and to CIRO. The dealer gives a copy at account opening (3724).
- **How CIRO protects investors (mandatory).** It explains that CIRO "regulates the activities of Canadian investment dealers and mutual fund dealers and the advisors they employ".
- **Opening an Investment Account (optional).** This investor guide covers KYC questions, naming a TCP and questions to ask the RR.

### Canadian Investor Protection Fund

The dealer discloses its membership, the fund's name and the coverage (IDPC 2284).
- **Covers:** client property (cash, securities and similar) held by a member firm (the dealer) that is missing when the dealer becomes insolvent.
- **Does not cover:** market losses, unsuitable investments, misrepresentation, poor advice or issuer default.
- **Limits for an individual** (generally, per the CIPF coverage page as at October 2026):
  - **$1 million** for general accounts combined (cash, margin, TFSA, FHSA);
  - **plus $1 million** for registered retirement accounts (RRSP, RRIF, LIF);
  - **plus $1 million** for RESPs.
- **Corporations and partnerships:** generally $1 million.
- **Claims:** the client must file a claim within 180 days of the insolvency.

For example, a client is missing $1.2 million across margin and TFSA accounts. CIPF covers $1 million of that general-account property, and an RRSP has its own separate limit. CIPF protects against the failure of the dealer, not against investment losses (concept).

### Derivative Risk Disclosure

It warns that "you may lose more than the amount of funds deposited". The dealer gives it to each derivatives retail client before it accepts the first derivatives order. It gets the client's acknowledgement of receipt (3254(1)(i)–(ii)).

### Investment Dealer conflict of interest disclosures

The disclosure is written, prominent, specific and in plain language. The dealer gives it before opening if it has already identified the conflicts (3113) (1.23).

### Investment Dealer complaint handling procedures

The dealer gives a written summary at opening, and the summary stays available on an ongoing basis (3724) (7.12).

*Sources: IDPC 2284, 3113, 3216(5), 3218, 3254, 3724; CIRO Official Brochures page and brochures; GN-3200-24-001 Attachment A; CIPF coverage page; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.15 Product due diligence (PDD)

> **Key rule:** The dealer must not make securities available to clients unless it has taken reasonable steps to "assess the relevant aspects of the securities or derivatives, including the securities' or derivatives' structure, features, risks, initial and ongoing costs and the impact of those costs". It must also approve them and monitor them for significant changes (IDPC 3301(1)).

**Why PDD exists.** PDD makes the dealer a filter before products reach clients. Through PDD, the dealer can find securities that it should offer only to certain classes of clients, or to no client at all.

### On the Investment Dealer

- **Making available.** A dealer makes a security "available" when it puts it on the shelf, buys it for a client, recommends it or advertises it.
- **A risk-based approach.** PDD can be light for listed common shares. It is deeper for complex, novel, leveraged or prospectus-exempt products. Do not assume that a product needs little review because it looks like one already on the shelf.
- **Restrictions.** The dealer may restrict complex products, such as leveraged or inverse ETFs and principal-protected notes, to certain clients. It may also keep them off the shelf.
- **Own analysis.** The dealer should do its own analysis rather than rely on reports that the issuer paid for.
- **A formal process.** For complex or new products, guidance describes a documented review by staff from compliance, legal, finance, marketing, sales and operations. A new product committee with senior staff then makes a formal decision.
- **Monitoring.** A change to the security itself is significant. A general market move usually is not, but a change specific to one security or sector may be.

### On the Approved Person

The Approved Person "must not purchase securities or derivatives for, or recommend securities or derivatives to, a client unless the securities or derivatives have been approved by the Dealer Member" (3301(2)).

**Transfers-in and client-directed trades** generally need no shelf approval if the dealer does not otherwise make the security available. But the dealer must still take reasonable steps to assess them. PDD does not apply to carrying-broker arrangements.

> **Exam trap:** PDD is not underwriting due diligence. Underwriting due diligence concerns prospectus **disclosure**; PDD concerns whether a product fits the **client base**.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A supplier asks a dealer to add a new 3x leveraged inverse equity ETF to its shelf. It provides a glossy report that it paid for.
>
> **Question.** How should the dealer handle the request?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. The ETF uses leverage and derivatives and is new to the dealer, so it needs the deeper review.
> 2. The dealer does its own analysis and does not rely on the supplier's report.
> 3. A committee reviews it, and senior staff make a formal decision.
> 4. If approved, the dealer can restrict it to clients with the knowledge and capacity for it.
> 5. The dealer trains its RRs on the ETF and monitors it for significant changes.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A client asks RR Callum Whitlock about a private bond that a friend bought. The bond is not on the dealer's shelf.
>
> **Question.** May Callum recommend it?
>
> **Resolution.** No. An Approved Person must not recommend a security that the dealer has not approved. If the client later directs the purchase, approval is generally not needed. But the dealer must still assess the bond, and the instruction still needs a suitability determination (1.18).

*Sources: IDPC 3301, 3303(1); GN-3300-21-001*

## 1.16 Know-your-product (KYP)

> **Key rule:** An Approved Person "must not purchase or sell securities or transact in derivatives for, or recommend securities or derivatives to, a client unless the Approved Person takes steps to understand the securities or derivatives, including the securities' or derivatives' structure, features, risks, initial and ongoing costs and the impact of those costs" (IDPC 3302(1)).

**Why KYP matters.** An RR must understand a product before the RR can judge whether it fits a client. CIRO calls KYP an extension of the duty to deal fairly, honestly and in good faith with clients.

The steps required are those reasonable for suitability (3302(2)). **KYP is separate from PDD:** shelf approval does not discharge it. KYP covers everything in the account, including transfers-in and client-directed trades.

### Structure and features

The RR should be able to explain the features: returns, leverage, time horizon, complexity and conflicts. The RR should also understand how the product can help the client reach the objectives.

### Risks

The RR should be able to explain the risks: loss of principal, liquidity and redemption risk, and embedded derivatives. The RR should also understand how market volatility could affect returns.

### Initial and ongoing costs, and the impact of costs

**Costs** include these items:
- commissions and sales charges;
- trailing commissions;
- management, incentive and referral fees.

**Impact of costs (concept).** Costs compound. Take $100,000 invested for 20 years at 6% before costs. Assume annual compounding. The net return is the gross return minus the annual cost. It grows to about $265,330 at a 1% cost, but only to about $219,112 at 2%.

### What not to do

Never recommend a product **solely** on these grounds:
- information from the issuer or other third parties;
- similarity to other products;
- recommendations of others.

**Exemptions.** KYP does not apply to OEO and direct electronic access accounts, or to carrying-broker arrangements.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A client wants "a safe way to own equities". RR Ewan MacLeod's dealer has an index-linked principal-protected note (PPN) on its shelf.
>
> **Question.** What must Ewan understand before he recommends it?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. **Structure:** the protection usually applies only at maturity and depends on the issuer's credit (concept).
> 2. **Features:** the return may be capped, and the term is long (concept).
> 3. **Risks:** a sale before maturity may return less than the principal, and liquidity may be limited (concept).
> 4. **Costs:** embedded fees reduce the return, and Ewan must explain their impact.
> 5. Shelf approval does not replace this work.

*Sources: IDPC 3302, 3303(2); GN-3300-21-001; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.17 Retail suitability determination

> **Key rule:** Before an investment action, "the Dealer Member must determine, on a reasonable basis, that the action satisfies the following criteria": the action is suitable, and "the action puts the retail client's interest first" (IDPC 3402(1)).

**Why it matters.** CIRO calls suitability a fundamental obligation that is critical to investor protection. It does not guarantee a result. It requires a reasonable decision on the facts at the time.

### The factors (3402(1)(i))

- **(a)** KYC;
- **(b)** the dealer's PDD and the Approved Person's KYP;
- **(c)** the impact on the account, including **concentration** and **liquidity**;
- **(d)** the potential and actual **impact of costs**;
- **(e)** a **reasonable range of alternatives** available at the dealer.

The factors do not always have equal weight. The RR uses professional judgement to weigh them.

### The relationship of KYC to recommendations

- KYC, PDD and KYP come first, and a product's risk rating alone is not enough.
- The investment must meet the client's objectives and stay within the client's risk profile.
- The dealer judges suitability on the resulting **account portfolio**, not trade by trade (3402(4)).
- Higher concentration in one security or sector calls for more steps, and more documentation, to show suitability.
- Several options may be suitable. The RR picks the one that puts the client first, ahead of higher pay. The RR discloses material positive and negative factors.
- CIRO judges the determination by what a reasonable RR would have done at the time, not with hindsight.

### The need to avoid excessive switches (churning)

Churning is trading or switching mainly to generate commissions. The IDPC does not use the word, but retail supervision must detect "excessive trading" (IDPC 3945(2)(iii)). It harms the client because each trade adds costs, and costs compound (concept).
- **Switches.** CSA guidance says that, before a switch, the dealer tells the client the charges, the dealer's incentives and the tax consequences. An example of an incentive is a higher trailing commission. The dealer also explains why the switch is appropriate.
- **Red flags.** CIRO's mutual fund dealer rules give examples, such as redemptions within 3 months of purchase or more than 5 trades a month.
- **Consent.** Mutual fund dealer staff guidance says that "disclosure or client consent cannot be viewed as a means to justify excessive trading."

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Piotr Nowak holds a balanced mutual fund that suits him. His RR suggests a switch to a similar fund with the same mandate. The new fund pays the dealer a higher trailing commission. The old fund also charges a redemption fee on the switch.
>
> **Question.** Is the switch suitable?
>
> **Resolution.** The RR must disclose the charges, the higher trailing commission and the tax consequences, and explain why the switch is appropriate. Here the funds are alike, so the main effect is more cost for Piotr and more pay for the dealer. The switch does not put Piotr first, so it is not suitable. His consent does not change this.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ximena Vanier has a growth profile, and 30% of her account is in energy stocks. Her RR wants to sell other holdings and put 20% of the account into a new oil producer.
>
> **Question.** What should the RR consider?
>
> **Resolution.** The stock may suit her profile on its own. But suitability depends on the resulting portfolio, and half of it would be in one sector. The RR should consider a smaller position or alternatives that put Ximena first. Any recommendation needs more documented steps to show suitability.

*Sources: IDPC 3402(1), (4), 3945(2)(iii); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 3, 3.1–3.2, 3.12; 31-103CP "Switch or change transactions"; Mutual Fund Dealer Rule 200 (VI); MFDA MSN-0065; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.18 Unsolicited or unsuitable client instructions

### Obligations of the Registered Representative

In an advisory account, suitability applies to client instructions as well as to recommendations. Marking an order "unsolicited" is not enough. The RR need not accept an unsuitable order.

If the RR decides that a client instruction is not suitable, guidance says that the RR does these things (GN-3400-21-004 s. 3.10):
1. tell the client why the action is not suitable;
2. advise the client against proceeding;
3. recommend an alternative action that is suitable.

| Situation | Obligation |
|---|---|
| Advisory, client's idea, suitable | Proceed; the determination is still made and documented |
| Advisory, client's idea, unsuitable | Explain, advise against, offer an alternative, get recorded confirmation, or decline |
| OEO account | No recommendations and no trade suitability; only continued account suitability (3402(3)(i), 3404(1)) |
| Signs of exploitation or incapacity | TCP and temporary hold (1.6), not 3402(5) |

### Obligations of the Investment Dealer

> **Key rule:** The dealer may carry out an unsuitable retail instruction only if it has (IDPC 3402(5)):
> 1. "informed the retail client of the basis for the determination that the action will not satisfy subsection 3402(1) and advised the client against proceeding";
> 2. recommended a suitable alternative;
> 3. "received recorded confirmation of the retail client's instruction to proceed".

**Why this balance.** The rule respects the client's right to decide. It also makes sure that the client decides with full information, and it records the warning (concept).

The dealer also has these obligations:
- **Records.** Its records must demonstrate compliance with the suitability requirements, including the recorded confirmation (3804(2)(xii)).
- **Supervision.** Its daily and monthly supervision must detect unsuitable trading and excessive trading (3945(2)(i), (iii)). Its policies must also state the actions that deal with problems that the review finds (3945(1)).
- **Responsibility.** The dealer answers for the acts of its RRs (1.27).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Nadia Haddad, 66, has a conservative income profile. She tells her RR to put $60,000 into one speculative junior mining stock that a neighbour mentioned.
>
> **Question.** What must the RR do before he enters the order?
>
> **Resolution.** He tells her why the trade is not suitable and advises her not to proceed. He recommends a suitable alternative. If she still insists, the dealer needs her recorded confirmation before it acts. He may also decline the order. If the dealer accepts it, trade supervision reviews the order (3945(2)(i)).

*Sources: IDPC 3402(3), (5), 3404(1), 3804(2)(xii), 3945(1)–(2); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 3.4.3, 3.10; GN-3200-26-001 s. 2.1; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.19 Types of investment action

Suitability applies before the dealer "purchases, sells, withdraws, exchanges or transfers-out securities" or "takes any other investment action" (IDPC 3402(1)). Guidance adds **depositing** and a **decision to hold**.

### Purchasing

A purchase adds a holding, so the RR checks the resulting portfolio, not only the new security (3402(4)) (1.17). The 3402(1) factors apply in this way:
- **KYC fit:** the security matches the client's objectives, time horizon and risk profile.
- **Concentration:** the higher the concentration in one security, sector or industry, the more steps the RR takes and documents.
- **Liquidity:** an illiquid security, such as an exempt market product, can stop the client from getting cash when needed (concept).
- **Costs and alternatives:** the RR compares the costs of a reasonable range of alternatives at the dealer.

### Selling

The RR uses professional judgement and puts the client's interest first, "being mindful of any tax or other consequences". Check these points:
- which position to sell, given the capital gain or loss on each (concept; 7.10);
- costs, such as commissions or redemption fees;
- the risk and concentration of the holdings that remain.

This also covers the liquidation of transferred-in holdings.

### Holding a position

A decision to keep a position after a review is an investment action. So is the decision to leave cash uninvested.

### Depositing and transferring in

A deposit or transfer-in of securities is a trigger (3402(2)(i)). Guidance also lists depositing as an investment action. The dealer may not be able to complete the suitability determination before it opens the account. If so, it completes the determination within a reasonable time. Until then, it restricts investment actions, for example to liquidating trades, transfers or disbursements.

### Exchanging

An exchange, such as a fund switch, sells one security and buys another. So the points for selling and purchasing both apply. Before a switch, the dealer tells the client the charges, the dealer's incentives and the tax consequences (1.17). The RR compares the old and new holdings. If they are alike, the switch mainly adds costs and does not put the client first (concept).

### Transferring out and withdrawals

Consider the effect on what remains. For example, a withdrawal from the safe part of a portfolio leaves a riskier mix (concept).

**Pre-authorized plans.** The dealer assesses a plan at set-up and on triggers, not before each instalment.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ravi Subramanian moves his account from another dealer. It holds listed shares, a bond fund and a large exempt real estate fund that cannot be redeemed.
>
> **Question.** How does suitability apply?
>
> **Resolution.** The transfer-in is a trigger. Until the review is done, the dealer restricts the account to liquidating trades, transfers or disbursements. The RR understands each holding and decides whether to hold or sell it, mindful of tax. Ravi cannot redeem the real estate fund, so the review of it is limited. The RR still considers it in future recommendations.

*Sources: IDPC 3402(1)–(2), (4); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 3.1.2, 3.2.3, 3.4.1–3.4.4; 31-103CP "Switch or change transactions"; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.20 Account appropriateness vs suitability determination

| | Account appropriateness (IDPC 3211) | Suitability (IDPC 3402) |
|---|---|---|
| When | **Once, before** opening | Before each investment action, on triggers and on periodic review |
| Question | Is this account, and the access it gives, appropriate for this person? | Is this action, and the resulting portfolio, suitable, and does it put the client first? |
| Alternatives | Account types at the dealer | Reasonable range of actions at the dealer |
| OEO | Only whether opening is appropriate | Only whether keeping the account remains suitable |

### The link between the two

Guidance calls appropriateness "a one-time obligation" that the suitability obligation "tracks" after opening. Under 3402(3), the dealer must continue to determine that the account and the scope of access remain suitable. This applies when the client's circumstances change, or when the dealer offers new products or account types. The dealer reassesses the relationship when a trigger warrants it, not at every review.

**Why two obligations.** Appropriateness is a check at opening, and suitability is the ongoing check. A client can pass the appropriateness check and later need a different account. An action can also be unsuitable inside a suitable account (concept).

### Analyzing scenarios

Ask first: is the issue about the **account and its access**, or about an **action and the resulting portfolio**?
- A retiree in a fee-based account who now trades once a year: **account** question.
- A request to buy a speculative stock: **action** question.
- An OEO client who asks what to buy: **account** red flag, because the client expects advice.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Maren Lindqvist, 79, has an OEO account. She now phones the dealer often to ask what to buy, and she cannot place trades online without her grandson's help.
>
> **Question.** Which obligation applies?
>
> **Resolution.** This is an account question under 3402(3)(i). She expects recommendations and has persistent trouble with the online platform. Guidance treats both as red flags, so the OEO account may no longer be suitable. The dealer should discuss an advisory account with her and must not give advice within the OEO account.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Thaddeus Quill opened a fee-based account when he traded actively. He is now retired and trades once a year. He also asks to buy shares of a large bank.
>
> **Question.** Which obligations apply?
>
> **Resolution.** There are two separate questions.
> 1. **Account question (3402(3)).** His circumstances changed, so the dealer determines whether the fee-based account is still suitable. He pays an ongoing fee for little activity (1.9).
> 2. **Action question (3402(1), (4)).** The dealer determines whether the purchase, and his resulting portfolio, are suitable and put his interest first.
>
> The dealer answers each question on its own. A suitable trade does not make the account suitable.

*Sources: IDPC 3211, 3402(1), (3)–(4); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 3.3.1–3.3.3, 3.4.4; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.21 Monitoring and maintaining accounts; trigger events

**Why it matters.** A suitable portfolio does not stay suitable by itself. The client, the securities and the RR can all change (concept).

> **Remember:** Review the account and holdings within a reasonable time after (IDPC 3402(2)):
> 1. a deposit or transfer-in of securities;
> 2. a new RR, Portfolio Manager or Associate Portfolio Manager on the account;
> 3. a change in the client's KYC information that could make the account or a holding unsuitable;
> 4. a change in a security that could make it unsuitable;
> 5. the periodic KYC review (36 months; 12 for managed and discretionary).

### Undergoing a suitability determination at each trigger

**Extent of the review.** It depends on the type of event, the client relationship, the dealer's services and the securities held. After a significant KYC change, such as a job loss, the dealer should consider a restriction. The restriction allows only liquidating trades, transfers or disbursements. It stays until the dealer re-evaluates the KYC.

**New RR.** When a new RR takes over, the dealer's procedures require two steps (IDPC 3215(2)):
1. the new RR verifies the client information with the client as soon as practicable;
2. the new RR and the designated Supervisor acknowledge in writing that they reviewed the account application and updated it if necessary.

> **Exam trap:** Significant market fluctuations are **not** a mandatory trigger. The RDI must state whether the dealer will review suitability "in the event of significant market fluctuations" (3216(5)(ii)(d)(IV)).

### Investment Dealer and regulatory requirements for monitoring

Policies for daily and monthly retail supervision "must specifically address the detection of", among other things (IDPC 3945(2)):
- unsuitable trading;
- undue concentration;
- excessive trading;
- trading in restricted securities;
- RR–client trading conflicts;
- excessive transfers and trade cancellations indicating possible unauthorized trading;
- inappropriate or high-risk trading strategies;
- deterioration in the quality of client holdings.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Hyun-woo Park's RR retires, and the dealer assigns his account to a new RR. The last KYC review was 30 months ago.
>
> **Question.** What must happen?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. The new RR is a trigger (3402(2)(ii)). Within a reasonable time, the dealer reviews the account and its securities to determine whether they meet the 3402(1) criteria.
> 2. The new RR verifies the client information in the account application with Hyun-woo as soon as practicable. The aim is to make sure that the information is correct (3215(2)(i)).
> 3. The new RR and the designated Supervisor acknowledge in writing that they reviewed the account application and updated it if necessary (3215(2)(ii)).
> 4. The extent of the review depends on the circumstances. The 36-month review is also due soon, so the RR can combine the work.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Priya Raman opens an account and transfers in a portfolio from another dealer. One small-cap stock is half of the portfolio. The dealer cannot complete the suitability determination before it opens the account.
>
> **Question.** What must the dealer do?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. The transfer-in is a trigger (3402(2)(i)). Within a reasonable time, the dealer reviews the account and all the securities in it.
> 2. The test is 3402(1): is each holding suitable for Priya on the five factors, and does it put her interest first? Here, concentration and liquidity (factor (c)) are the main concern.
> 3. CIRO expects the dealer to complete the determination within a reasonable time after it opens the account. Until then, the dealer restricts the investment actions in the account, for example to liquidating trades, transfers or disbursements (GN s. 3.4.1).
> 4. The suitability criteria also apply to a decision or recommendation to continue to hold a security (GN s. 3.4.2).
> 5. If the RR sells holdings, the RR uses professional judgement. The RR puts Priya's interest first and is mindful of tax and other consequences (GN s. 3.4.3).

*Sources: IDPC 3209, 3215(2), 3216(5)(ii)(d), 3402(1)–(2), 3945(2); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.11.2, 3.4.1–3.4.4; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.22 Impact of changes on the suitability determination

A suitability determination is correct only for the facts at that time. When the facts change, the determination can become wrong.

### Material change to the client's circumstances

Job loss, inheritance, divorce, retirement, illness or a move can shift the client's capacity, horizon, liquidity or objectives. For example, guidance says that a job loss can change the risk profile and the investment needs and objectives. This is a KYC trigger (3402(2)(iii)), and the dealer updates the KYC (3209(3)).

### Product and market research

- **Product research.** A downgrade, a new manager or mandate, higher fees, a merger or suspended redemptions can mean that a holding no longer fits. This is a security trigger (3402(2)(iv)) and links to PDD monitoring (3301(1)(iii)).
- **Market research.** A shock to one sector can create concentration risk. A sector-specific change may need a reassessment.

### Economic, political and social events

Interest rates, recessions, elections, trade disputes and pandemics change asset-class risk and return. They can also decrease a client's income or liquidity (concept). Market and economic events are not a listed trigger in 3402(2). The RDI states whether the dealer reviews suitability after significant market fluctuations (1.21). Reassess when the event changes a client's KYC information (3402(2)(iii)) or a security (3402(2)(iv)).

At the dealer level, PDD guidance says that a general market change usually does not require a reassessment of individual approved securities (1.15).

### Emerging issues, financial trends, economic trends and world news

Demographics, technology, the climate transition and inflation shift long-run assumptions and ESG preferences (concept). The RR usually addresses them at the periodic review.

### Analyze in three steps

1. What changed: the client, the product or the environment?
2. Which KYC factor or holding does it affect?
3. Does the resulting portfolio still meet 3402(1)?

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Svetlana Ivanova, 52, has a growth profile and a 15-year horizon. She inherits $400,000 and decides to retire in two years.
>
> **Question.** How do these changes affect suitability?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. **What changed:** the client. The inheritance raises her net worth. The early retirement shortens her horizon and creates income needs.
> 2. **Trigger:** these KYC changes could make her account or holdings unsuitable, so they are a trigger (3402(2)(iii)). The RR updates her KYC within a reasonable time (3209(3)), and she confirms it (1.5).
> 3. **Result:** a growth portfolio may not fit a two-year horizon with income needs. The RR reviews whether the portfolio still meets 3402(1).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Interest rates rise by two percentage points in six months. Leonor Batista has a variable-rate loan that she used to invest.
>
> **Question.** Must the dealer reassess every security in every account?
>
> **Resolution.** No. Market and economic events are not a listed trigger in 3402(2). But Leonor's higher loan payments can change her capacity and liquidity. That change in her KYC information could make her account unsuitable, so it is a trigger (3402(2)(iii)). The RR reviews her KYC and her leveraged position.

*Sources: IDPC 3202(3), 3209(3), 3216(5)(ii)(d), 3301(1)(iii), 3402(1)–(2); GN-3300-21-001; GN-3400-21-004 s. 2.11.2; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.23 Managing conflicts of interest

**Why the rules exist.** The dealer and the RR earn money from client business, so their interests can differ from the client's. Staff note that some compensation practices "can result in behaviour that is not in the best interest of clients". To address a conflict in the client's best interest means to put the client's interests ahead of the dealer's or the RR's own.

### Identifying

The dealer and each Approved Person identify existing and reasonably foreseeable material conflicts. The Approved Person "must promptly report that conflict of interest to the Dealer Member" (IDPC 3110).

### Avoiding

The dealer and the Approved Person avoid any conflict that cannot be addressed in the client's best interest (3111(2), 3112(2)). An Approved Person may not act on a conflict that they identified until two things happen (3111(3)):
- the conflict is addressed in the client's best interest;
- the dealer consents.

### Addressing

The dealer and the Approved Person must address all material conflicts in the **client's best interest** (3111(1), 3112(1)). Controls do most of this work, such as pay that does not differ by product.

### Disclosure

The dealer discloses in writing where a reasonable client would expect it. The disclosure covers these items:
- the nature and extent of the conflict;
- its potential impact on the client and the risk to the client;
- how the dealer has addressed or will address it.

It must be prominent, specific and in plain language. It comes before opening, or in a timely manner when the conflict is identified (3113).

> **Key rule:** "A Dealer Member and an Approved Person do not satisfy subsections 3111(1) or 3112(1) solely by providing disclosure to the client." (IDPC 3113(3))

### Typical situations

- **Compensation incentives:** use neutral grids and monitoring.
- **Third-party compensation and proprietary products:** CSA and CIRO staff say that these are almost always material. Compare the shelf with non-proprietary alternatives.
- **Benefits from others:** an employee or Approved Person must not accept consideration from anyone but the dealer for client activities (3115). Section 1.25 gives the narrow exceptions and all their conditions.
- **Specific bans:**
  - gratuities to a client's employees without the client's prior written consent (3116);
  - non-cash mutual fund sales incentives (3117). Listed exceptions include a dealer-wide internal incentive programme covering all products, trailing commissions and normal business promotion (3117(3));
  - **tied selling**, but the dealer may still offer relationship pricing (3118).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A dealer pays its RRs a higher payout on its proprietary funds. Its only response to this conflict is a disclosure document.
>
> **Question.** Has the dealer addressed the conflict?
>
> **Resolution.** No. Proprietary products are almost always a material conflict, and disclosure alone never satisfies the rule. The dealer needs controls, such as equal payouts and the same selection process for all funds. It should also compare its funds with alternatives. If no control works, it must avoid the conflict.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** RR Orla Fitzgerald owns a large stake in a small listed company. She thinks that its shares suit several clients.
>
> **Question.** What must she do first?
>
> **Resolution.** Her stake is a material conflict, so she reports it promptly to the dealer. She must not recommend the shares until the conflict is addressed in the clients' best interest and the dealer consents. The clients get specific written disclosure, and each recommendation must still be suitable.

*Sources: IDPC 3110–3113, 3115(2)(i), 3116–3118; CSA/CIRO Staff Notice 31-363; GN-3100-21-001*

## 1.24 Outside activities

### Definition

CIRO guidance defines outside activities as any activities conducted outside the dealer, "whether or not the Approved Person receives compensation for such activities." Examples include directorships, a family business, insurance sales, teaching and volunteer roles.

**Why control them.** An outside activity can cause these problems (concept):
- it can take time from clients;
- it can create conflicts;
- it can tempt misuse of client information;
- it can confuse clients about who offers what.

### Pre-approval

**Rule (IDPC 2554).** The activity must not be contrary to securities laws or CIRO requirements. It must not bring the securities industry into disrepute. An Approved Person may have an outside activity only if all of these conditions apply:
1. the Approved Person informs the dealer;
2. the Approved Person obtains the dealer's **prior approval**;
3. the dealer's policies address continuous client service and conflicts;
4. the dealer notifies CIRO of the activity, as NI 33-109 requires.

**Approval considerations.**
- Time demands on client service.
- Positions (for example with public issuers) that could prevent unbiased advice.
- **No use of client information.**
- **No use of the dealer's premises, logos, staff or email.**
- Risk of client confusion and the reputation of others involved.

An Approved Person never approves their own request. Changes to an outside activity must be reported through NRD within 30 days of the change (NI 33-109 s. 4.1). The regulator's acknowledgement of an NRD notice is not an approval of the activity.

**Supervision after approval (GN-2500-22-001 s. 4).** The dealer's policies must:
- require every Approved Person to disclose an outside activity before it starts;
- let the dealer identify conflicts;
- determine the risks of a conflict and respond to it.

The dealer addresses a conflict by avoidance, or by disclosure and supervision, always in the clients' best interest. Monitoring includes these steps:
- training on outside activities, including how to report changes;
- records of the supervision;
- supervisory action on no report or a late report;
- an alternate representative for clients, where necessary;
- a check that the individual's lifestyle matches their known activities, as a fraud alert.

### Disclosure

The Approved Person must disclose any outside activity that gives a **position of influence** over a client or potential client. In such a position, a reasonable person would consider that the individual has influence over another individual. Examples are:
- a leader in a religious or similar organization;
- a medical doctor or a nurse;
- a professor, instructor or teacher at a degree- or diploma-granting institution;
- a lawyer or a notary.

A registered individual in a position of influence must not buy or sell securities for, or recommend securities to, these people:
- an individual who has a relationship with them that arises from the position, and whom a reasonable person would consider susceptible to their influence;
- a person whom the Approved Person knows is a spouse, parent, sibling, grandparent or child of that individual.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A diploma-granting college offers RR Kenji Morimoto a role to teach an evening personal-finance course.
>
> **Question.** What must Kenji and the dealer do?
>
> **Resolution.** Teaching is an outside activity, paid or not. Kenji informs the dealer and gets its approval before the course starts. The dealer checks the time demands and the risk of confusion.
>
> A teacher at a degree- or diploma-granting institution can be in a position of influence, so Kenji discloses the role. He must not trade for or advise students who are susceptible to his influence because of the role. The same applies to a student's spouse, parent, sibling, grandparent or child, if Kenji knows of the relationship.

*Sources: IDPC 2554; GN-2500-22-001 ss. 1–5 (incl. 31-103CP s. 13.4 as cited there); NI 33-109 s. 4.1*

## 1.25 Personal financial dealings with clients

> **Key rule:** "An employee or Approved Person of a Dealer Member must not, directly or indirectly, engage in any personal financial dealings with clients." (IDPC 3115(1)) The listed dealings "include, but are not limited to" the following.

**Why the prohibition exists.** Personal dealings mix the RR's interest with the client's money. A client who trusts the RR may find it hard to refuse (concept). Mutual fund dealer staff guidance says that borrowing from a client raises a conflict that almost never can be addressed in the client's best interest.

**Related Person.** Several exceptions use the Income Tax Act's Related Person concept. CRA explains that related persons include "individuals connected by blood relationship, marriage, common-law partnership or adoption". Where an exception uses it, the arrangement must follow the dealer's policies and procedures. RRs, IRs, Portfolio Managers and Associate Portfolio Managers must also disclose it to the dealer. The dealer must approve it in writing before the transaction or arrangement (3115(2)(iii)–(v)).

### Accepting any consideration

Prohibited: any pay, gratuity or benefit from anyone but the dealer for client activities. There are two exceptions:
- consideration that is non-monetary, of minimal value and infrequent, so that a reasonable person would not question whether it created a conflict or improper influence;
- pay from a client for services through an **approved outside activity**.

### Settlement agreements

Prohibited: settling with a client, or paying client losses personally. Exception: the dealer's **prior written consent** (7.12).

### Borrowing from clients

Prohibited: borrowing or receiving a guarantee. Exceptions: a lending institution in its normal course, or a Related Person.

### Lending to clients

Prohibited: lending or guaranteeing. Exception: a Related Person.

### Control or authority

Prohibited: acting as a POA, trustee or executor for a client, or having any other full or partial control over a client's finances. Exceptions: a Related Person, or authority within a discretionary or managed account agreement.

### Comingling of assets or funds (business partnerships, investment clubs)

IDPC 3115 does not name these. But its list is not exhaustive. So if an Approved Person mixes their money or assets with a client's, the general prohibition applies. This is an interpretation, not a separate rule.
- MFDA-era staff guidance says that investment clubs should be prohibited. In such a club, the representative and clients invest together, and the representative decides.
- The same guidance says that co-investment by the Approved Person and clients in pyramid-like schemes or other questionable investments should be prohibited.
- A partner, shareholder or officer role in a client's business may be possible as an **approved outside activity**.

> **Exam trap:** Unless the conditions above are met, these acts are **all** prohibited:
> - a loan from an unrelated client;
> - acting as a client's executor;
> - quietly paying a client's loss.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** An RR's mother is a client of the dealer and wants to name the RR as executor of her will.
>
> **Question.** Is this allowed?
>
> **Resolution.** Yes, with conditions. The mother is a Related Person. The arrangement must follow the dealer's policies and procedures. The RR discloses it to the dealer, and the dealer must approve it in writing before the RR accepts the role.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** An RR enters a wrong order, and the client loses $3,000. To avoid a complaint, the RR offers to pay her from his own account.
>
> **Question.** May he?
>
> **Resolution.** Not without the dealer's prior written consent. A private payment would also hide the error from the dealer.

*Sources: IDPC 3115; MFDA MSN-0047; CRA "Related persons" definition; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 1.26 Conflict-of-interest policies and procedures

The dealer's policies "must specifically address identifying, disclosing and avoiding or otherwise addressing material conflict of interest situations" (IDPC 3114). The dealer must also supervise how its Approved Persons address conflicts (3112(3)).

**Why the dealer leads.** An individual RR cannot change the product shelf or the pay structure. CSA and CIRO staff expect dealers to lead the work on these conflicts, including the product shelf and pay structures.

### Effective controls and qualified supervision

Approval should only take place "where there are effective controls and qualified supervision" (outside-activities guidance). Per CSA/CIRO staff, policies cover:
- a definition of conflicts;
- responsibilities;
- escalation;
- materiality criteria;
- controls and how they are tested;
- training;
- client disclosure;
- a review at least annually, and more often if the business model or products change.

**Training.** Staff found training inadequate if it:
- was too generic for the dealer's business;
- gave no examples of the dealer's own material conflicts;
- left out people who should attend;
- did not explain how to report or escalate a conflict.

Dealers train all registered individuals and supervisory staff on conflicts. Staff expect this to include compliance staff.

### Applicable due diligence process for approvals

Approval processes must be robust and impartial, and dealers "must be able to provide evidence of the due diligence performed." For outside activities, guidance also expects an annual canvass of employees. The approval records show any conditions that the dealer imposed and how it monitors them.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A branch manager approves an RR's request to become a director of a private company. The file has no record of the review and no plan to monitor the role.
>
> **Question.** What is wrong with the approval?
>
> **Resolution.** The dealer cannot provide evidence of the due diligence that it performed. Its records must show the review, any conditions and how it monitors compliance. Without them, the dealer cannot show effective controls and qualified supervision.

### Appropriate record-keeping

Records must demonstrate conflicts compliance. They must also document sales practices, compensation arrangements and incentive practices (IDPC 3804(2)(xvi)–(xvii)). As a conflict becomes more material, the records need more detail.

Staff expect a **conflicts inventory**, such as a conflicts matrix. For each material conflict, it records:
- a description of the conflict;
- the materiality assessment and the criteria used;
- the potential impact and risk;
- who identified the conflict and assessed it;
- the controls, and why they address the conflict in the client's best interest;
- how the dealer disclosed the conflict to clients.

Staff also expect these records:
- evidence of periodic reviews of the inventory, including tests of the controls;
- training records: the content, attendance logs and how the dealer later trained absent staff.

*Sources: IDPC 3112(3), 3114, 3804(2)(xvi)–(xvii); GN-2500-22-001 ss. 1–3.5; CSA/CIRO Staff Notice 31-363 (policies and procedures, training, record keeping)*

## 1.27 CIRO standards of conduct

> **Key rule:** A Regulated Person "in the transaction of business must observe high standards of ethics and conduct and must act openly and fairly and in accordance with just and equitable principles of trade," and "must not engage in any business conduct that is unbecoming or detrimental to the public interest" (IDPC 1402(1)).

**Why a general standard.** Specific rules cannot list every kind of misconduct, so the general standard applies to all other misconduct (concept). Rule 3100 aims to maintain "investor confidence in securities markets" (IDPC 3101(1)).

Conduct may breach the standard if it (1402(2)):
- is **negligent**;
- breaches a legal, regulatory, contractual or **dealer-policy** obligation;
- departs unreasonably from expected standards;
- is likely to diminish investor confidence.

The list applies "Without limiting the generality of the foregoing", so other conduct can also breach 1402(1). Typical examples (concept):

| 1402(2) limb | Example |
|---|---|
| Negligent | An RR enters an order in the wrong account and does not check it |
| Fails to comply with an obligation, incl. dealer policy | An RR does not report a written complaint, as dealer policy requires |
| Unreasonable departure from expected standards | An RR signs a form for the client |
| Likely to diminish investor confidence | An RR promises a guaranteed return on a stock |

**Responsibility.** The dealer answers for all acts and omissions of its employees, partners, Directors and officers. An Approved Person must avoid causing the dealer to breach CIRO requirements (1403). The dealer keeps client business within ethical bounds and good business practice (3102).

### A Registered Representative and their client

Examples of breaches:
- trades that the client did not authorize;
- a form that the RR signs for the client;
- false statements about risk or fees;
- personal use of client money or information;
- pressure on the client's risk-questionnaire answers;
- promises of returns.

### A Registered Representative and their firm

Examples of breaches:
- trades off the dealer's books;
- false KYC information or records;
- complaints, conflicts or outside activities hidden from the dealer;
- breaches of dealer policies;
- misleading statements to supervisors or CIRO.

> **Exam trap:** A breach need not be intentional or illegal. Negligence, or a breach of dealer policy, can be enough.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** An RR expects a stock to rise. He cannot reach several advisory clients, so he buys it for them and plans to explain later.
>
> **Question.** What is wrong?
>
> **Resolution.** These are unauthorized trades. In an advisory account, the client makes each decision. Discretionary trading is prohibited outside discretionary and managed accounts (IDPC 3221). Good intent is not a defence.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A client complains in writing to her RR about fees. He does not tell the dealer, as dealer policy requires, and refunds the fees from his own money.
>
> **Question.** Which standards does he breach?
>
> **Resolution.** A breach of dealer policy can itself breach the standard of conduct. He hid a complaint from the dealer, which stops it from handling the complaint (7.12). Paying the client personally also needs the dealer's prior written consent (1.25).

*Sources: IDPC 1402, 1403, 3101(1), 3102, 3115(2)(ii), 3221; CIRO Complaints Brochure; standard finance concept (verified)*
