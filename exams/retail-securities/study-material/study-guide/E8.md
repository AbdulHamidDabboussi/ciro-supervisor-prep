# Element 8: Execution and market integrity

This element covers these topics:

- UMIR abusive-trading and gatekeeper rules;
- order types;
- settlement;
- cash and margin accounts;
- client reporting.

It has **7 of 120 questions** (indicative). Expect short scenarios: name the rule that a person broke, or match an order type to a client. The syllabus asks for no calculations here. In this element, "the dealer" means the Investment Dealer, and "RR" means the Registered Representative.

## 8.1 UMIR: best execution, abusive trading and front running

The Universal Market Integrity Rules (UMIR) keep trading on Canadian marketplaces fair. A **Participant** includes a registered dealer that is one of these (UMIR 1.1):

- a member of an exchange;
- a user of a quotation and trade reporting system (QTRS);
- a subscriber of an alternative trading system (ATS).

UMIR 10.4 also applies the abusive-trading and front-running rules to each director, officer, partner and employee of the Participant.

### Best execution

**Best execution** is "obtaining the most advantageous execution terms reasonably available under the circumstances" (IDPC 3119). The client cannot see every marketplace. So the dealer's policies and procedures must specifically address best execution (IDPC 3120). For listed securities, the policies weigh four broad factors (IDPC 3121):

- price;
- speed of execution;
- certainty of execution;
- overall cost, when the dealer passes costs on to clients.

The dealer must consider client instructions (IDPC 3123). But it "must not follow client instructions" that break CIRO requirements or securities laws (GN-URPART6-25-0003). It discloses its policies in writing (IDPC 3129) and reviews them at least once a year (IDPC 3126). The duty also applies to order-execution-only (OEO) accounts. For over-the-counter (OTC) trades, the policies "must be designed to ensure fair pricing" (IDPC 3122(1)).

> **Exam trap:** Best execution is a policies-and-procedures duty, not the best price on every fill. A dealer "may not be able to achieve best execution for every single trade" (GN-URPART6-25-0003).

### Abusive trading

UMIR 2.1(1)(a), 2.2 and 2.3 apply when the Participant "knows or ought reasonably to know". So CIRO does not need to prove a deliberate purpose, only what a "reasonably prudent" Participant would know (UMIR Policy 1.2 Part 3). Cornering (Policy 2.2) and UMIR 2.1(1)(b) do require intent.

**Specific unacceptable activities (UMIR 2.1).** A Participant must not do these things:

- **Failed trade fix:** trade to fix a failed trade before the Rule 7.10 report is due (UMIR 2.1(1)(a)). This applies if it knows or ought reasonably to know that the fix will also fail.
- **Splitting orders:** intentionally enter two or more orders on one trading day to impose a Marketplace Trading Obligation on another person (UMIR 2.1(1)(b)). This applies only if one single order, or orders entered at the same time, would not impose that obligation.
- **Trades off a marketplace:** use another person to trade off a marketplace if no UMIR 6.4 exemption allows the trade (UMIR 2.1(2)(a)).
- **Trading ahead of a client's interest:** "make a pattern of trading in a particular security with knowledge of an expression of interest by a client in that particular security" (UMIR 2.1(2)(b)).
- **Principal orders ahead of client orders:** without the client's specific consent, enter orders "in such a manner as to attempt to obtain execution of a principal order in priority to the client order" (UMIR 2.1(2)(c)).

Some pre-arranged trades and intentional crosses need the prior approval of a Market Regulator (UMIR 2.1(3)). This is so if the price is below the best bid, or above the best ask, by more than both 5% and 10 trading increments.

**Manipulative and deceptive practices (UMIR 2.2(1)).** These activities are manipulative by nature (Policy 2.2 Part 1):

- fictitious trades;
- wash trades, with no change in beneficial or economic ownership;
- cornering: trades "with the intent of limiting the supply" of a security.

CIRO also names these practices, "whether they are conducted manually or electronically" (GN-URPART7-26-0007):

- **Layering:** a genuine order sits on one side of the market. Orders with no intention to trade on the other side "bait" others to trade with it at an artificial price.
- **Quote stuffing:** "excessive market data messages" flood systems to get an information advantage.
- **Quote manipulation:** non-genuine displayed orders change the best bid or best ask, to get a better price on a dark trade.
- **Spoofing:** non-genuine orders before the market opens change the "Calculated Opening Price" to the advantage of the person who enters them.
- **Abusive liquidity detection:** orders find a large buyer or seller, and the person then trades ahead of that interest.

**Artificial pricing (UMIR 2.2(2)).** A Participant must not enter an order that will, or "could reasonably be expected" to, create a false appearance of trading or an artificial price. Listed examples include these (Policy 2.2 Part 2):

- matched orders;
- purchases at successively higher prices;
- setting the close;
- orders not meant to execute;
- a buy order without "the ability or the reasonable expectation to make the payment" (see 8.4).

If a person knows, or ought reasonably to know, that they take part in a listed or similar activity, they breach UMIR 2.2(2). This is so "irrespective of whether" a false or misleading appearance of trading activity or interest, or an artificial price, results (Policy 2.2 Part 2). A price is **artificial** "if it is not justified by real demand or supply" (Policy 2.2 Part 3). Relevant factors include:

- the prices of the trades before and after the order;
- the price change that the order causes;
- the recent liquidity of the security;
- the time of entry;
- a motive to set an artificial price;
- whether one account makes up substantially all of the orders in the security.

The absence of a factor "is not determinative that a price is or is not artificial" (Policy 2.2 Part 3).

**Improper orders and trades (UMIR 2.3).** A Participant must not enter an order if it knows or ought reasonably to know that the order breaks any of these:

- securities legislation, including the insider-trading law (see 8.2);
- SRO requirements;
- marketplace rules;
- UMIR.

### Front running

Policy 4.1 prohibits trading "to take advantage of information concerning a client order that has not been entered on a marketplace". A Participant that knows of a client order that "could reasonably be expected to affect the market price" must not do these things before anyone enters that order (UMIR 4.1(1)):

- enter a principal or non-client order in the security or a related security or derivative (e.g. calls);
- solicit others to trade;
- tell others about it, except in the necessary course of business.

Exceptions (UMIR 4.1(2)):

- no decision-maker had actual knowledge of the client order;
- an order for the benefit of the client;
- an order "solicited to facilitate the trade of the client order";
- a principal order that hedges a position the Participant assumed, or agreed to assume, before it had actual knowledge of the client order. The hedge must be commensurate with the risk and follow the Participant's ordinary practice;
- a principal order to fulfil a legally binding obligation made before actual knowledge;
- an order for an arbitrage account.

> **Exam trap:** Front running needs specific knowledge of an unentered client order: "Trading based on non-specific pieces of market information, including rumours, does not constitute frontrunning" (UMIR Policy 4.1).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Cosima Vasilakis asks her RR, Matthias Rautenbach, to buy 200,000 shares of a thinly traded company. Before he enters the order, Matthias buys call options on the company for himself.
>
> **Question.** Did he break UMIR?
>
> **Resolution.** Yes. Matthias knew of an unentered order that could reasonably affect the price. A call is a related derivative, and no exception applies. UMIR 10.4 applies the rule to Matthias as an employee.

## 8.2 Gatekeeper responsibilities

### The purpose of the UMIR gatekeeping obligations

CIRO calls Participants "gatekeepers to the securities market" (GN-URPART7-26-0007). So they must have policies and procedures to "effectively address, detect, prevent and report manipulative and deceptive activity". The people who handle orders often see abuse first. An early report lets the firm stop the abuse and lets CIRO act.

> **Key rule:** If an officer, director, partner or employee believes that account activity may violate a listed rule, they "shall forthwith report to their supervisor or the compliance department of the Participant" (UMIR 10.16(1)).

Listed rules include abusive trading, front running and best execution. After a report, the supervisor or compliance department must review the activity diligently (UMIR 10.16(3)). If the review finds a possible violation, it must do these things (UMIR 10.16(4)):

- make a written record of the report and the review;
- investigate the activity diligently;
- make a written record of the findings;
- if it finds a UMIR violation, report the findings to CIRO by the 15th day of the next month.

CIRO expects a report even if a violation "has occurred only once" (GN-URPART10-26-0003).

### The client's typical financial activity and patterns, to identify suspicious transactions

You see an unusual transaction only if you know what is usual for the client. The dealer's risk assessment considers "the client's characteristics, activities and transaction patterns" (GN-3200-23-001). CIRO expects the intended use of the account to agree with the client's KYC and trades. If it does not, the dealer should investigate further (red flags: see 6.2).

### How to identify and escalate suspicious transactions

Dealers must report to FINTRAC any completed or attempted transaction if "there are reasonable grounds to suspect" that it relates to one of these (PCMLTFA s. 7):

- money laundering;
- terrorist financing;
- sanctions evasion.

This threshold is "a step above simple suspicion", which is only a "gut feeling". You "do not have to verify the facts" or prove an offence (FINTRAC STR guidance). There is no dollar minimum. The dealer must send the report "as soon as practicable" (STR Regulations).

1. Compare the activity with the client's KYC and usual pattern.
2. Escalate to your supervisor or AML compliance. If an employee reports the transaction to a superior, the employee has a defence (PCMLTFA s. 75(2)).
3. Do not tell the client about a possible report (FINTRAC guidance).

A good-faith report gives immunity from criminal or civil proceedings (PCMLTFA s. 10). A person who discloses a report to prejudice a criminal investigation commits an offence (PCMLTFA s. 8).

### Possible insider trading activity and violations

In Ontario, a person "in a special relationship with an issuer" must not do these things with an undisclosed material fact or material change (OSA s. 76):

- buy or sell the issuer's securities (s. 76(1));
- tell another person, except in the necessary course of business (s. 76(2));
- recommend or encourage a trade, except in the necessary course of business (s. 76(3.1)).

A **material fact** would "reasonably be expected to have a significant effect on the market price" (OSA s. 1(1)). The special relationship includes (s. 76(5)):

- insiders, affiliates and associates of the issuer;
- persons and firms that do business with or for the issuer;
- directors, officers and employees of the issuer;
- tippees who know, or ought reasonably to have known, that their source is in a special relationship.

For the trading and recommending rules, options on the issuer's securities count as its securities (s. 76(6)).

**Order markers.** UMIR 6.2 requires a marker on an order for the account of an insider ("IA") or a significant shareholder ("SS") of the issuer. A significant shareholder holds more than 20% of the issuer's voting securities (UMIR 1.1). The markers help regulators find possible insider trading (GN-URPART6-26-0004). For retail client accounts, the dealer's supervision policies and procedures must specifically address the detection of insider trading (IDPC 3945(2)).

### Applicable regulatory frameworks on whistleblowers

| Framework | Key features |
|---|---|
| CIRO Whistleblower Service | Confidential "to the fullest extent possible"; no award; not for complaints about your own account |
| OSC Whistleblower Program | Award of 5% to 15% of sanctions and/or voluntary payments, to a maximum of $5 million; up to $1.5 million does not depend on collection, $1.5 million to $5 million does; the tip must lead to over $1 million in sanctions and/or voluntary payments; anonymous only through a lawyer |
| OSA s. 121.6 | No reprisal for reporting, or planning to report, to the employer, the OSC, a recognized SRO or law enforcement; also protects asking for advice and co-operating or testifying; clauses barring reports to the last three are void to that extent |

The OSA protection covers a "specified individual": an employee, officer, director or contractor (s. 121.6(1)). It applies to information about an act of the employer that the person "reasonably believes is contrary to Ontario securities law" or to SRO rules (s. 121.6(2)). A reprisal includes, without limitation, these actions or threats of them (s. 121.6(3)):

- dismissal;
- demotion, discipline or suspension;
- a penalty or a withheld benefit;
- intimidation or coercion.

In a court action or arbitration, the employer must prove that it took no reprisal (s. 121.6(6)).

| Reporting channel | Key features |
|---|---|
| Approved Person → dealer | Within two business days: own possible breach; a written client complaint against you; a complaint of, e.g., manipulation or insider trading against another Approved Person (IDPC 3702; see 7.12) |
| Dealer → CIRO | Client complaints (except service complaints), internal investigations and results, charges and proceedings (IDPC 3703(2)); UMIR gatekeeper findings (UMIR 10.16(4)) |

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Valentina Zielinski works for a drug company listed on the TSX. She phones her RR: "Sell all my company shares today. Our trial failed, but we announce it next week."
>
> **Question.** Can the RR take the order?
>
> **Resolution.** No. As an employee, Valentina is in a special relationship with the issuer (OSA s. 76(5)). The failed trial is an undisclosed material fact, so a sale breaks OSA s. 76(1). The RR knows this, so the order is improper (UMIR 2.3). The RR declines it and reports it (UMIR 10.16). The RR must not trade on the fact, or tell it to others except in the necessary course of business (OSA s. 76(2)).

## 8.3 Order types and client requirements

Each order type gives more **certainty of execution** or more **control of price**. No order type gives the maximum of both. Match the order to the client's priority.

| Order | How it works | Suits a client who |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Buys at the best ask, or sells at the best bid, on entry (UMIR 1.1) | Wants certainty of execution |
| Limit | Buys at no more than a set maximum, or sells at no less than a set minimum: the limit or better (UMIR 1.1) | Wants price control; may not fill |
| Stop-loss / stop-limit | At the trigger price, becomes a market or limit order (sell stops sit below the market, buy stops above) | Wants to limit a loss or protect a gain |
| Immediate-or-cancel (IOC) | Fills what it can at once and cancels the rest | Accepts a partial fill now |
| Fill-or-kill (FOK) | Fills the whole quantity at once, or nothing | Wants all-or-nothing |
| Iceberg | Displays part of the size; a hidden reserve refills it | Has a large order and wants to limit market impact |
| Short sale | Sells a security the client does not own (UMIR 1.1) | Is bearish; the position is margined in a margin account (IDPC 5113) |

**Risks.** A limit order may fill only in part, or not at all. A market order fills at once in a liquid market, but the client does not know the price in advance. In a thin or fast market, it can fill far from the last price. An IOC order can leave the client with an unfilled part. In a thin market, an FOK order may never fill.

CIRO expects dealers to tell OEO clients how "market orders and stop-loss market orders" work and their "associated execution risks" (GN-URPART6-25-0003). IOC, FOK and iceberg are market terms, not UMIR definitions.

### On-stop orders

When a stop-loss order with no limit triggers, it "will become a 'market order'" (GN-URPART7-25-0003). In a fast market, it can then fill far from the trigger price. So CIRO encourages dealers to require limit prices on all stop-loss orders. If a dealer still allows market stop-loss orders, CIRO says each order should meet all these conditions:

- the security is very liquid, with relatively low historic price volatility;
- the trigger price is near the market price at entry;
- the order size is not much greater than the average trade size.

A stop-limit order controls the price. But if the price moves past the limit in one jump, the order may not fill.

### Iceberg orders

A large visible order can move the price against the client. An iceberg order shows only part of the size, so it limits market impact. CIRO calls them "partially disclosed (i.e., iceberg orders)" (GN-URPART7-26-0007).

### Short sales

In a short sale, the client sells first and plans to buy back later at a lower price. The loss has no limit, because the price can rise without limit. So a short sale suits a client who expects a fall and accepts that risk, in a margin account (IDPC 5113). The order rules are (details in 6.5):

- UMIR 3.2: mark the order as a short sale;
- UMIR 3.2: no short sale of a Short Sale Ineligible Security;
- UMIR 3.2: arrange to borrow the securities first, but only for a Pre-Borrow Security;
- UMIR 3.3: have a reasonable expectation to settle the trade.

> **Exam trap:** There is no tick test. The last-sale-price restriction on short sales was repealed effective October 15, 2012.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Augustin Fairbairn holds 2,000 shares of a thinly traded stock. He will travel for a month and wants protection against a sharp fall.
>
> **Question.** Should he use a stop-loss order or a stop-limit order?
>
> **Resolution.** The stock is thinly traded, so it fails CIRO's "very liquid" condition for market stop-loss orders. A triggered stop-loss order could fill far below the trigger price. So use a stop-limit order. Tell Augustin that if the price falls past his limit in one jump, the order may not fill.

## 8.4 Trade execution and settlement

### Awareness of the various marketplaces

NI 21-101 names four types of marketplace:

- exchanges (e.g., TSX);
- quotation and trade reporting systems;
- alternative trading systems (ATSs);
- dealers that execute trades of exchange-traded securities outside a marketplace.

An exchange lists issuers and disciplines its members. An ATS does not list issuers, and it disciplines subscribers only by exclusion (NI 21-101). Unless an exemption applies, a trade must occur by "the entry of an order on a marketplace" (UMIR 6.4). One security can trade on several marketplaces. So best execution considers "order and trade information from all appropriate marketplaces" (IDPC 3121(2)).

### Placing orders, including processes for handling order errors and changes

An **order** is "a firm indication by a person or company, acting as either principal or agent, of a willingness to buy or sell a security" (NI 21-101). The dealer keeps a record of each order, executed or not. At a minimum, the record shows these items, among others (IDPC 3815(1)):

- the terms of the order "and of any modification or cancellation thereof";
- the account;
- the time of entry;
- if the order uses the dealer's discretionary power, a statement to that effect (see 8.5);
- the price and, if feasible, the time of execution.

So if a client changes or cancels an order, the record must show the change. Sometimes the person who places an order is not the account holder or a person authorized in writing. Then the dealer records the name, sales number or designation of that person (IDPC 3815(2)).

UMIR 6.4 lets the dealer "adjust by a journal entry an error in connection with a client order". So the dealer can correct an error, such as a wrong quantity, without a new marketplace trade. Supervision watches for "excessive transaction transfers and trade cancellations indicating possible unauthorized trading" (IDPC 3945).

### The settlement and delivery process

Since May 27, 2024, most equity and long-term debt trades settle **T+1**: "the next business day following T" (NI 24-101; CIRO Bulletin 23-0150). For trades not cleared and settled through a clearing corporation, IDPC 4805 sets same-day delivery for Government of Canada T-bills (see 2.3). Delivery is normally book-entry through the clearing agency. A trade that does not settle on the expected settlement date is a **failed trade**. If it has not settled 10 trading days after settlement date, the Participant must notify CIRO (GN-URPART3-24-0002).

Cash accounts can also use two special arrangements (Form 1 Schedule 4 Note 7):

- **DAP (delivery against payment):** the dealer delivers a purchase against full payment. It settles on the later of settlement date and the date the dealer gives notice that the securities are available.
- **RAP (receipt against payment):** the dealer receives the securities of a sale against payment to the client, on settlement date.

### Considerations for foreign exchange transactions and currency conversions to facilitate transactions

USD purchases from a CAD account convert at the dealer's rate, including a spread. A USD account avoids repeated conversions but not currency risk. For orders sent to a foreign market, best-execution policies consider "the extent of exposure to fluctuations in foreign currency exchange" (GN-URPART6-25-0003). CIRO recommends that confirmations of foreign fills show the exchange rate (GN-3800-21-003).

### The purpose and application of the cash account rule

In a cash account, each trade must settle by payment or delivery on settlement date (Form 1 Schedule 4 Note 7(i)). The client can pay with:

- cash or other immediately available funds;
- proceeds of securities held long in a cash account, if the equity is more than the amount of the trade;
- a transfer from a margin account, if adequate margin stays in that account.

An unpaid trade puts the dealer's own capital at risk. So if a cash-account trade does not settle, the dealer must set aside capital against the unpaid balance (Note 7(i), Note 8). From 6 business days past regular settlement date, this amount is the margin deficiency, calculated as if the cash accounts were margin accounts (Note 8).

**Isolated transactions (Note 7(v)).** In an isolated instance, a client may do one of these:

- settle a regular or DAP trade by a sale of the same security, when the account equity does not cover the trade;
- move a cash-account trade to a margin account before payment in full;
- move a DAP trade to a margin account within 10 business days after settlement date.

### The restriction process on overdue cash accounts

> **Key rule:** "When any portion of the money balance for a cash account of a customer is outstanding 20 business days or more after settlement date the customer shall be restricted from entering into any other transactions (other than liquidating transactions) in any account of the customer" (Note 7(vi)(a)).

The restriction lifts on any of these (Note 7(vi)(a) and 7(vii)):

- payment of the balance that is 20 business days or more overdue;
- a liquidating trade, after which no part of the balance is 20 business days or more overdue;
- for a client with no margin account, transfer of all open cash-account trades to a new, adequately margined margin account.

A DAP balance also causes a restriction if it is unpaid 5 business days or more after the required DAP settlement date. For clients outside continental North America, the period is 15 business days. The client can then enter only liquidating transactions in any other account (Note 7(vi)(b)). This restriction lifts when the DAP trade settles in full, or on the Note 7(vii) transfer.

The restrictions do not apply to the accounts of these (Note 7(viii)):

- acceptable institutions;
- acceptable counterparties;
- non-Dealer Member brokers;
- regulated entities.

> **Exam trap:** For regular cash accounts, the count starts at settlement date, not trade date. A partial payment does not lift the restriction while any portion stays 20 business days or more overdue.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Laszlo Quintal has a cash account and an RRSP at the dealer, and no margin account. A cash-account purchase is unpaid 21 business days after settlement date. He asks to buy a bond in his RRSP.
>
> **Question.** Can the RR enter it?
>
> **Resolution.** No. The restriction covers "any account of the customer", so it covers the RRSP. Laszlo can enter only liquidating trades. To lift it, he pays the overdue balance or sells enough to clear it. He can also move all open cash-account trades to a new, adequately margined margin account.

## 8.5 Margin accounts, special situations and trading authorizations

### Long and short margin accounts

Leverage makes gains and losses larger, so margin rules keep client leverage appropriate (IDPC 5110). The client must be "aware of the risks and benefits associated with trading on margin" (IDPC 3246). The dealer gets a signed margin agreement before it opens the account (IDPC 3247; see 1.10).

| | Long margin | Short margin |
|---|---|---|
| Loan value | (100% − rate) × market value | (100% + rate) × market value, held as a credit |
| 1,000 shares at $20, 50% rate | Dealer lends $10,000; client puts up $10,000 | $20,000 proceeds + $10,000 deposit = $30,000 credit |
| Margin call if the price… | falls | rises |

IDPC 5310 sets minimum client rates by price band, and dealers may set higher "house" rates. At $2.00 or more, the rate is **30%** for a security on the List of Securities Eligible for Reduced Margin (LSERM), otherwise **50%**. The full table is in 6.5.

The dealer may sell an indebted client's securities, or buy securities to close a short position (IDPC 5116). If a margin account becomes undermargined, the dealer must restrict it within 20 business days. The client can then enter only trades that reduce the margin deficiency, until the account is fully margined (Form 1 Schedule 4 Note 5(iii)).

### Special margin situations

Some positions or accounts carry more risk for the dealer, so the rules change the margin:

- **Control blocks:** the margin rate is 100%, unless the position is part of an underwriting commitment (IDPC 5340). A control block can be hard to sell without a large price drop.
- **Concentrated positions:** the dealer may reduce the loan value (GN-5300-21-001).
- **Convertibles into LSERM stocks:** the reduced rate applies, plus any conversion loss (LSERM page).
- **Registered-account debit balances** (GN-FORM1-22-001):
  - from a specific security trade: cash account rules;
  - from items in the trust agreement, such as administrative fees: margin account rules;
  - from anything else: unsecured, with 100% margin.
- **Account guarantees:** a guarantee gives margin relief (IDPC 5824). The dealer tells the guarantor in writing that suitability "will not be reviewed in relation to the guarantor" (IDPC 5820).

### The need for specialized trading authorizations

The account holder decides the trades in an account. So another person needs written authority, and some account types need extra approvals:

- **Another person:** the dealer keeps a signed trading authorization from the account holder, and a record of everyone with trading authority (IDPC 3220).
- **Discretion:** individuals who trade for the dealer may use it only in discretionary or managed accounts (IDPC 3221(1)).
- **Discretionary account:** the dealer enters into a discretionary account agreement with the client before it accepts the account. A designated Supervisor approves the account and the signed agreement (IDPC 3273). The agreement lasts 12 months or less and cannot be renewed (IDPC 3274).
- **The RR:** an RR can trade a discretionary account only if both of these are true (IDPC 3275; see 1.10):
  - the RR has at least two years of active experience in trading, advising or analysis for all the product types traded;
  - the account is at the RR's own dealer.
- **Derivatives:** a signed derivatives trading agreement comes before the first derivatives trade (IDPC 3251).

> **Exam trap:** Time-and-price discretion is not account discretion. For an order "for a specific amount or a specific security", the RR may choose the time and price for best execution without a discretionary account (IDPC 3221(2)).

## 8.6 Reporting to clients: confirmations and statements

### Requirements to confirm orders with clients, including fees and commissions

The dealer must "promptly send the client a written confirmation of all purchases and sales" (IDPC 3816). The client uses it to check each trade against the instructions. At a minimum, it shows (IDPC 3816(2)):

- the trade day and the marketplace, or accepted wording: "Traded on one or more marketplaces or markets, details available upon request";
- the settlement date;
- the quantity and description of the security, and the consideration;
- any regulatory fee;
- the name of the salesperson, if any;
- the name of any dealer that the dealer used as its agent for the trade;
- whether the dealer acted as principal or agent.

For an agency trade on an equity marketplace, the client can ask for the name of the other party (IDPC 3816(2)(i)(d)).

**Fees and commissions:** confirmations include "any commission charged in respect of the trade" (GN-3800-21-003, on IDPC 3816). Retail confirmations for OTC securities other than debt securities show each charge and the total of all charges (IDPC 3816(2)(v)). For a retail client's debt trade that is not a primary market transaction, a different rule applies (IDPC 3816(2)(vi)(c)). The confirmation shows one of these:

- the total mark-up or mark-down, commission and other service charges;
- the total commission. If the dealer also applied a mark-up, mark-down or other service charge, the confirmation also has a notice. The notice says that dealer remuneration was added to the price (purchase) or deducted from it (sale).

Details are in 2.4.

**Debt yields and notations:** for a debt purchase, the confirmation also shows the yield (IDPC 3816(2)(vi)(a)–(b)). For a bond that is not a strip, it shows the YTM. If the issuer can call the bond before maturity, it shows "callable". If the coupon rate is variable, it shows "The coupon rate may vary." For a strip, it shows two yields (details: 2.3, 2.5 and 2.6).

### Investment Dealer responsibility for trade confirmations and account statements to be sent to clients

| Statement | Sent when (IDPC 3808, 3809) |
|---|---|
| Monthly | The client asks for it, or there was a transaction or a cash/position change (not just dividends or interest) in the month, or the account holds an open option, futures, forward or CFD position at month-end |
| Quarterly (minimum) | Any debit or credit balance, or securities, derivatives or bullion, held at quarter-end |
| Outside holdings report | Quarterly, to retail clients with outside holdings: off-book positions where the dealer is dealer of record (e.g. mutual funds held at the fund company) or receives continuing compensation |

Statements show at least these items (IDPC 3808(4)):

- the opening and closing cash balance;
- all deposits, credits, withdrawals and debits;
- the description and quantity of each position;
- the market value of each position, or a notice if the value is not determinable;
- the total market value of all positions;
- for a retail client's quarterly statement only (IDPC 3808(4)(vi), (viii)):
  - the cost of each position, or a notice if the cost is not determinable;
  - a notation that defines the cost method used (book cost or original cost);
  - the total cost of all positions.

The dealer sends all confirmations and statements promptly (IDPC 3845).

> **Exam trap:** The dealer, not the RR, is responsible for confirmations and statements. A dividend alone does not trigger a monthly statement (IDPC 3808(2)). But a dormant account still gets quarterly statements if it holds a cash balance or securities at quarter-end.

*Sources: IDPC 1201, 3808, 3809, 3816, 3845; GN-3800-21-003*
