# Element 7: Investment recommendations

This element applies earlier material to individual clients: fitting objectives and risk to a portfolio, handling biases and personal values, making and explaining a suitable recommendation, funding goals with time-value-of-money maths, using Canadian tax rules, and handling complaints. It carries about **14 of the 120 questions**.

Expect client scenarios and short calculations on a basic/scientific calculator (an annuity payment, a capital gain, tax on interest versus dividends). Every tax figure states its year. Provincial dividend tax credits are not in the sources, so tax examples are federal only.

## 7.1 Objectives, risk profile and performance

**Investment objectives** say what the money is for: capital preservation, income, growth, or investing in line with personal values. **Investment needs** include liquidity. The **risk profile** limits how far the portfolio can go to reach the objectives, and expected **performance** follows from the risk taken: a capital-preservation portfolio cannot be expected to earn equity-like returns.

The risk profile is the lower of tolerance and capacity (1.3).

Where a Registered Representative and client agree on goals (for example, in an investment policy statement), the guidance says they should be specific and measurable, and the client should be updated periodically on progress.

**Analyzing the fit.** Work from the goal to the required return, then ask whether that return is achievable within the risk profile. If it is not, change the goal (save more, retire later, accept a smaller target), not the risk profile. Judge performance against a benchmark that matches the agreed asset mix.

> **Exam trap:** A goal that needs a 7% return when the client's low risk profile supports about 4% does **not** justify a riskier portfolio. The goal, savings rate or timeline changes; the risk profile does not.

*Sources: GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.3, 2.3.5; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.2 Non-financial constraints

CIRO's KYC guidance recognizes investing "in accordance with their personal values" as an objective, including environmental, social and governance (ESG) criteria or other personal preferences.

| Constraint | Typical form | Portfolio impact |
|---|---|---|
| **ESG criteria** | Exclusion screens (tobacco, weapons, fossil fuels), best-in-class, thematic or impact funds | Smaller universe; sector tilts (for example, under-weight energy); tracking error against a broad index |
| **Equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI)** | Favour issuers with diverse boards and management and inclusive policies | Narrower universe; style or sector bias; reliance on issuer disclosure and ratings |
| **Other personal preferences** | Faith-based screens, avoiding certain companies, "buy Canadian" | May remove whole asset classes, reducing diversification and raising concentration |

**Analyzing the impact.** Constraints can reduce diversification, shift risk and return, raise costs (specialized funds often charge more) and complicate benchmarking. The guidance says dealers should give clients the opportunity to express their needs and objectives in their own terms. In practice, note the restriction on file, explain the trade-offs, and check what an "ESG" product actually holds.

> **Exam trap:** A values-based restriction narrows the choices but never relaxes suitability. The portfolio must still fit the risk profile, time horizon and liquidity needs.

*Sources: GN-3400-21-004 s. 2.3.3; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.3 Behavioural finance biases

Behavioural finance studies predictable departures from rational decisions. Biases lead clients to over-trade, hold losers, chase winners and under-diversify, which lowers returns. The syllabus groups them as follows.

| Group | Bias | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Cognitive errors | **Representativeness** | Judging by stereotype or a short pattern: "a great company must be a great stock" |
| | **Illusion of control** | Believing one can influence or predict largely random outcomes |
| | **Confirmation** | Seeking evidence that supports one's view and ignoring the rest |
| | **Hindsight** | "I knew it all along": past events seem more predictable than they were |
| Information processing | **Framing** | Same choice judged differently by presentation ("90% chance of gain" vs "10% chance of loss") |
| | **Anchoring** | Fixing on a reference number (purchase price, past high) and adjusting too little |
| | **Mental accounting** | Treating money differently by source or "bucket" (spending a refund while carrying card debt) |
| | **Availability** | Over-weighting recent or vivid information, such as headlines |
| Emotional | **Loss aversion** | A loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases; holding losers to avoid realizing them |
| | **Overconfidence** | Over-rating one's skill or information, leading to excessive trading and concentration |
| | **Endowment** | Valuing what one owns (inherited or employer shares) above market value |
| Capital market | **Survivorship** | Judging a category only by surviving funds or firms, which overstates past returns |
| | **Gambler's fallacy** | Expecting a reversal after a run ("it has fallen five days, so it must rise") |
| | **Herding** | Following the crowd into popular assets, feeding bubbles and panics |
| | **Home country** | Over-weighting domestic securities (for Canadians, heavy in financials and energy) |

**Working with biases.** Cognitive errors can often be corrected with information. Emotional biases are harder to change and are usually managed with a written plan, automatic contributions, rebalancing rules and diversified products.

> **Exam trap:** Anchoring (fixation on a number) differs from loss aversion (pain of losing), though both produce "I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid." Match the bias to the client's stated reason.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.4 Suitability in specific situations

Every investment action or recommendation for a retail client must pass the IDPC 3402(1) suitability determination and put the client's interest first (factors: 1.17). This section applies it to specific situations.

**Selecting suitable products.** The product's risk, liquidity, complexity and cost must fit the client's objectives, risk profile and time horizon across the whole portfolio.

**The product shelf.** The alternatives considered are limited to what the firm offers.

> **Key rule:** "a consideration of a reasonable range of alternative actions available to the Registered Representative, Portfolio Manager, or Associate Portfolio Manager through the Dealer Member at the time the determination is made" (IDPC 3402(1)(i)(e))

CIRO's KYP guidance adds that Approved Persons "must not purchase for, or recommend securities to, a client unless the Dealer has approved those securities" (GN-3300-21-001). The CSA calls trading in or recommending proprietary products an inherent, almost always material conflict of interest that the firm must address in clients' best interest. Where a dealer offers only a few model portfolios, an in-depth review of alternatives is not expected if only one clearly fits.

**Net worth and liquidity.** Net worth is assets minus liabilities. Liquidity needs are gathered at KYC (1.2); here, check that the recommendation leaves enough liquid assets for them.

**Cash management and savings** (common practice). Keep an emergency fund (rule of thumb: three to six months of expenses) in cash, savings or short GICs; pay down high-interest debt first; save automatically; hold money needed within a few years in low-volatility assets. The guidance also expects dealers to identify clients better suited to cash deposits or guaranteed products.

**Government pension programs** (canada.ca; Retraite Québec for QPP).

| Program | Key features |
|---|---|
| **CPP retirement pension** | Taxable, monthly, for life; age 60+ with one valid contribution. Standard start 65; early −0.6%/month (max −36% at 60); late +0.7%/month (max +42% at 70). Maximum at 65 (January 2026): $1,507.65/month. Pension sharing with a spouse can lower tax |
| **QPP retirement pension** (Québec workers) | Normal age 65; from 60 reduced 0.5%–0.6% a month (30%–36% at 60); after 65 increased 0.7% a month, up to 58.8% at 72 |
| **OAS pension** | Age 65+, citizen or legal resident; 10 years' residence after 18 if living in Canada (20 if living abroad); full pension needs 40 years, otherwise partial (years ÷ 40). Deferral +0.6%/month to 36% at 70. Maximum (Oct–Dec 2026, age 65–74): $762.50/month. Taxable; 15% recovery tax on net income above the threshold ($93,454 for 2025) |
| **GIS / Allowance** | Income-tested; **not taxable**. GIS tops up low-income OAS pensioners. The Allowance is for a 60–64-year-old whose spouse or common-law partner receives GIS; the Allowance for the Survivor is for a 60–64-year-old widow(er) |

The recovery tax takes 15% of the excess only: net income $10,000 over the 2025 threshold costs $1,500.

> **Exam trap:** CPP can start at 60; OAS cannot start before 65. CPP deferral adds 0.7% a month, OAS 0.6%.

*Sources: IDPC 3402(1); GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.2, 2.3.5, 3.2.4; GN-3300-21-001; Companion Policy 31-103CP; canada.ca CPP and OAS/GIS pages (2025–2026); Retraite Québec QPP pages (2026)*

## 7.5 Choosing a portfolio from KYC information

1. **Inputs:** needs and objectives, risk profile, time horizon, liquidity, tax position, restrictions.
2. **Current portfolio:** asset mix, concentration, risk level and costs of existing holdings.
3. **Shortfall:** project the current plan against the goal. Close a gap by saving more, extending the horizon or lowering the target; add risk only if the profile allows.
4. **Strategy:** strategic asset mix; passive or active; laddering for income; dollar-cost averaging for regular savers.
5. **Implement and monitor** with suitable products from the shelf.

Suitability is judged on the whole account: registrants "cannot determine suitability on an individual trade/position basis".

**Expectations versus risk profile.** When the client's expectations exceed what the risk profile supports, follow the conflict-resolution steps in 1.3: discuss risk and return, never override the profile, explain the alternatives, and document the outcome.

**Cash flow analysis** compares after-tax income with spending to find the investable surplus. It shows whether a goal is realistic and how much liquidity the portfolio must supply. For example, $6,500 a month after tax less $5,500 of spending leaves $1,000 a month ($12,000 a year) to save or invest.

*Sources: GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.5, 3.1; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.6 Factors in addressing investment actions

**Significant KYC changes.** A trigger event under IDPC 3402(2) (list in 1.21), such as a change in the client's KYC information, requires a review of the account and its existing holdings against 3402(1). Updating the KYC itself (3209(3)) and the account restrictions to consider after a job loss are covered in 1.7 and 1.21. Here, the question is what action follows: whether each holding still fits, what selling or switching would cost, and which alternatives are available.

**Unexpected impact on the account.** A reasonable-looking trade can over-concentrate the account in one issuer, sector or illiquid exempt security, or lock up money the client needs. Dealers should set and monitor concentration and liquidity thresholds, and consider all of a client's accounts.

**Costs.** "Cost" includes direct and indirect fees, commissions, trailing commissions and dealer or Registered Representative compensation, whoever pays it.

> **Key rule:** "the potential and actual impact of costs on the retail client's returns" (IDPC 3402(1)(i)(d))

**Reasonable range of alternatives.** Registered Representatives must consider alternatives available through the Investment Dealer. What range is reasonable depends on the securities and services offered, the Registered Representative's skill and proficiency, and the client's circumstances. In practice, alternatives might include holding cash, doing nothing, or a cheaper way to get the same exposure.

> **Exam trap:** After a trigger event, existing holdings must be reviewed, not just new purchases.

*Sources: IDPC 3402(1), 3402(2), 3209; GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.11.2, 3.2.3–3.2.5*

## 7.7 The recommendation process

A sound recommendation covers four points:

1. **Pros and cons**: what the client gains (income, growth, diversification, tax efficiency) and gives up (liquidity, upside, fees).
2. **Risks**: the Approved Person should be able to explain a security's "structure, features, risks, initial and ongoing costs and the impact of those costs", including possible loss of principal, liquidity and redemption risk.
3. **What it accomplishes**: tie it to the goal (for example, "moves you from 80% to 60% equity, matching your post-retirement risk profile").
4. **Securing commitment**: summarize, check understanding, answer questions, confirm the decision and document it.

The guidance also asks Registered Representatives in advisory accounts to disclose material negative and positive factors, make sure the client understands the risks, and document that disclosure.

**Unsuitable client instructions.** If the client rejects the recommendation and insists on an unsuitable action, the three IDPC 3402(5) steps apply (1.18).

*Sources: GN-3300-21-001; GN-3400-21-004 s. 3.2.1; IDPC 3402(5); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.8 Time value of money for financial goals

The exam calculator has no TVM keys, so use the formulas (general TVM formulas: 2.9). Convention: **ordinary annuity** (end-of-period payments), with the rate per period matching the payment frequency (monthly: r ÷ 12, n × 12). Dividing by 12 assumes the quoted annual rate is nominal, compounded monthly; if it is an effective annual rate, the monthly rate is (1 + r)^(1/12) − 1. The examples below use annual payments and annual compounding.

```
Future value of an annuity:   FV = PMT × [((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r]
Present value of an annuity:  PV = PMT × [(1 − (1 + r)^−n) ÷ r]
Deposit needed for a goal:    PMT = FV × r ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1)
Income from a lump sum:       PMT = PV × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
where PMT = payment per period, r = rate per period, n = number of periods
```

> **Worked example**
> **Saving for a known goal.** Ana needs $50,000 in 10 years, earns 5% a year and deposits at each year-end.
> (1.05)^10 = 1.628895
> PMT = 50,000 × 0.05 ÷ (1.628895 − 1) = 2,500 ÷ 0.628895 = **$3,975.23 a year**

> **Worked example**
> **Income from a single investment.** Leon invests $300,000 at 4% and wants equal year-end payments for 20 years, ending at zero.
> (1.04)^−20 = 0.456387
> PMT = 300,000 × 0.04 ÷ (1 − 0.456387) = 12,000 ÷ 0.543613 = **$22,074.53 a year**
> If payments start immediately (annuity due): 22,074.53 ÷ 1.04 = $21,225.51.

Higher returns or longer horizons reduce the deposit needed; use realistic after-fee return assumptions consistent with the risk profile.

> **Exam trap:** For the same present value, beginning-of-period payments (annuity due) are smaller than end-of-period payments.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.9 Personal and corporate tax planning

**Tax-preferred accounts** (limits for the year stated).

| | FHSA | RRSP | TFSA | RESP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | First home | Retirement | Any | Post-secondary education |
| Who | Resident, 18+, first-time buyer, 71 or younger in the year opened | Contribute until Dec 31 of the year you turn 71 | Resident, 18+; no earned income needed | Subscriber for a beneficiary |
| Limit | $8,000 a year, plus up to $8,000 of unused room carried forward; $40,000 lifetime | New room each year: lesser of 18% of prior-year earned income and the dollar limit ($33,810 for 2026), minus any pension adjustment; unused room carries forward | $7,000 for 2025 and 2026; withdrawals restored next Jan 1 | No annual limit; $50,000 lifetime per beneficiary |
| Deductible? | Yes (RRSP transfers in are not) | Yes; contributions up to 60 days after year-end count for that year | No | No |
| Withdrawals | Qualifying home withdrawal tax-free, no repayment | Taxable; withholding 10/20/30% (outside Quebec); HBP up to $60,000 | Tax-free | Contributions back tax-free; EAPs taxable to the student |
| Excess | 1% per month | 1% per month above room + $2,000 | 1% per month | 1% per month on the excess over $50,000 (each subscriber on their share) |

An FHSA must close by Dec 31 of the year of the earliest of its 15th anniversary, the holder turning 71, or the year after the first qualifying withdrawal. RESP: the CESG adds 20% of the first $2,500 contributed each year (lifetime maximum $7,200); an accumulated income payment to the subscriber is taxed at their rate plus 20% (12% in Quebec). At 71 an RRSP must be withdrawn, moved to a RRIF or used to buy an annuity.

**Minimizing tax.**
- *Claim all deductions and credits*: RRSP/FHSA deductions, dividend tax credit, foreign tax credit, pension income amount.
- *Income splitting.* Income on property transferred or lent to a spouse, or to a related minor under 18, is taxed back to the transferor; so are a spouse's capital gains on it. A minor's capital gains are **not** attributed, so gifting growth assets to children can split income. Exceptions: fair market value transfers (for a spouse, with an election out of the spousal rollover) and loans at no less than the prescribed rate in effect when made (3% for October–December 2026), with each year's interest paid within 30 days after year-end. Also: **spousal RRSPs** (withdrawals taxed back to the contributor if they contributed that year or in the two years before), **pension income splitting** (up to 50% of eligible pension income) and **CPP sharing**.
- *Tax-loss harvesting.* Sell losers to offset realized gains, avoiding the **superficial loss** rule: the loss is denied if you or an affiliated person (e.g. a spouse or controlled corporation) buys, or has a right to buy, identical property within 30 days before or after the sale and still owns it, or has a right to buy it, 30 days after. The denied loss is usually added to the substitute's ACB: a $5,000 denied loss on shares rebought for $10,500 gives an ACB of $15,500.

**Corporations.**
- *Active vs passive.* A CCPC's active business income gets the small business deduction: a 9% net federal rate instead of the 15% general rate (CRA rates page, October 2026) on up to $500,000 a year (the business limit). Passive income generally does not, and prior-year passive income over $50,000 grinds the limit by $5 per $1, so it is gone at $150,000.
- *Integration*: income earned through a corporation and paid out as dividends should bear about the same total tax as income earned directly; the gross-up and dividend tax credit (7.11) and refundable taxes make it work.
- *Notional accounts.* **RDTOH**: part of the corporate tax on investment income is refunded when the corporation pays taxable dividends, up to its refundable account balances. There are two. **NERDTOH** holds the refundable Part I tax on investment income and is recovered only by paying non-eligible dividends; **ERDTOH** holds mainly Part IV tax on eligible dividends received and is recovered by any taxable dividend (eligible dividends draw only on ERDTOH). **GRIP**: income taxed at the general rate, payable as *eligible* dividends. **CDA**: mainly the non-taxable half of capital gains, paid by election as tax-free *capital dividends*.

*Sources: ITA ss. 74.1, 74.2, 74.5, 83(2), 89(1), 125, 129, 146; CRA corporation tax rates page (2026); CRA FHSA, RRSP, TFSA, superficial-loss and prescribed-rate pages; CRA T4040, RC4092, T4012 (2025); ESDC RESP pages; CRA plan limits (2025–2026)*

## 7.10 Capital gains

```
Capital gain (loss) = Proceeds of disposition − (ACB + outlays and expenses of sale)
Taxable capital gain = Capital gain × inclusion rate
ACB per unit (identical properties) = Total cost of units held ÷ Number of units held
```

ACB includes purchase costs such as commissions. Identical properties use average cost; CRA's most common examples are "shares of the same class of the capital stock of a corporation or units of a mutual fund trust" (for funds, units in the same class or series: 5.11). The average is recalculated at each purchase; sales do not change it. A return of capital reduces the ACB; a negative ACB is a deemed capital gain (5.11). Foreign amounts are converted to Canadian dollars at the rate when each arose.

**Treatment.** For 2025 the inclusion rate is generally **1/2**. Capital losses first reduce capital gains of the year, not other income. A **net capital loss** can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely against taxable capital gains.

> **Worked example**
> Jon buys 200 shares at $20 ($50 commission), then 300 at $25 ($50 commission).
> ACB = (4,050 + 7,550) ÷ 500 = $23.20 per share.
> He sells 400 at $30 with a $60 commission. Proceeds $12,000; ACB of shares sold = 400 × 23.20 = $9,280.
> Gain = 12,000 − 9,280 − 60 = $2,660. Taxable capital gain (2025, 1/2) = **$1,330**. The 100 remaining shares keep an ACB of $23.20.

**Minimizing capital gains tax.** Realize losses against gains (minding the superficial loss rule); time sales into lower-income years; hold growth assets in a TFSA (tax-free) or RRSP (deferred only: RRSP withdrawals are fully taxable income, so the capital-gains inclusion advantage is lost); donate appreciated listed securities or fund units to a qualified donee (zero inclusion rate); use the lower-rate spouse within the attribution rules.

> **Exam trap:** The CRA says you cannot "claim a deduction for capital losses within your RRSP" (T4040). TFSA gains and losses are not reported either, since income in the plan is tax-free, so neither plan's losses can offset gains in a non-registered account.

*Sources: CRA capital gains and capital losses pages (2025); CRA "Tax treatment of mutual funds"; CRA T4037 (2025); CRA T4040 (2025); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.11 Income tax on investment income

Each federal rate applies only to its slice of taxable income; provincial tax is added on top.

| 2026 federal taxable income | Rate |
|---|---|
| $0 – $58,523 | 14% |
| $58,523.01 – $117,045 | 20.5% |
| $117,045.01 – $181,440 | 26% |
| $181,440.01 – $258,482 | 29% |
| Over $258,482 | 33% |

For 2025 the lowest rate is 14.5%, because it fell from 15% to 14% on July 1, 2025.

> **Worked example**
> **Federal tax on 2026 taxable income of $70,000.**
> First bracket: 58,523 × 14% = 8,193.22
> Second bracket: (70,000 − 58,523) × 20.5% = 11,477 × 20.5% = 2,352.79
> Federal tax before credits = 8,193.22 + 2,352.79 = **$10,546.01**; non-refundable credits (such as the dividend tax credit) are then subtracted.

**Interest** is fully taxable. On multi-year investments such as compound GICs, interest earned during each complete investment year (anniversary to anniversary) is reported annually, even though it is paid at maturity (see 2.4).

**Canadian dividends** are grossed up, then reduced by a dividend tax credit (DTC) (see 3.6). The syllabus's "qualifying" and "non-qualifying" dividends are read here as the CRA's eligible and non-eligible ("other than eligible") dividends.

| | Eligible | Non-eligible ("other than eligible") |
|---|---|---|
| Gross-up (2025 and 2026) | 38% (× 1.38) | 15% (× 1.15) |
| Federal DTC (ITA s. 121) | 6/11 of the gross-up (15.0198% of the grossed-up amount) | 9/13 of the gross-up (9.0301% of the grossed-up amount) |
| Typical source | Income taxed at the general corporate rate (a CCPC pays these from GRIP) | CCPC income that used the small business deduction |

**Foreign dividends** get no gross-up and no DTC. They are reported in Canadian dollars and taxed in full like interest; foreign tax withheld may be claimed as a foreign tax credit, within limits (withholding and registered-plan treatment: 5.10).

> **Worked example**
> **$1,000 of each, 2026 federal tax only, 20.5% bracket.**
> Interest: 1,000 × 20.5% = **$205.00**
> Eligible dividend: 1,380 × 20.5% = 282.90; DTC = 6/11 × 380 = 207.27; tax = **$75.63**
> Non-eligible dividend: 1,150 × 20.5% = 235.75; DTC = 9/13 × 150 = 103.85; tax = **$131.90**
> Capital gain (1/2 inclusion, the rate CRA T4037 states for 2025; the sources give no 2026 rate): 500 × 20.5% = **$102.50**
> Foreign dividend: 1,000 × 20.5% = $205.00 before any foreign tax credit.

> **Exam trap:** The gross-up raises net income although no extra cash is received, which can increase income-tested clawbacks such as the OAS recovery tax.

*Sources: CRA tax rates and brackets (2025, 2026); CRA lines 12000/12010, 12100, 40425, 40500; ITA ss. 82(1), 121*

## 7.12 Complaints handling

**Recognition.** IDPC Part E covers a retail client's *recorded* expression of dissatisfaction alleging misconduct, or a *verbal* one where a preliminary investigation shows the allegation may have merit. Misconduct includes breach of confidentiality, theft, fraud, misappropriation, forgery, unsuitable investments, misrepresentation and unauthorized trading. A matter in civil action or arbitration is not a complaint here. Dealers also need policies for complaints that allege no misconduct.

**Process.** Supervisory or compliance staff handle complaints, and each dealer appoints a **designated complaints officer**. New clients receive a summary of complaint procedures and the CIRO-approved brochure. An **acknowledgement letter is due within five business days**.

**Resolution.** The substantive response gives a summary, the investigation result, the final decision with reasons, and options: arbitration, civil action, a complaint to CIRO, or the ombudsman (OBSI).

> **Key rule:** "A Dealer Member must respond to each client complaint as soon as possible and not later than 90 days from the date of receipt of the complaint" (IDPC 3726(4))

If the dealer will miss 90 days, it must tell the client (with reasons and a new date) and CIRO. Clients can go to OBSI without waiting for a final response if the dealer has not responded within 90 days (60 days for Quebec residents, per OBSI); after a final response they have **180 days** to go to OBSI, which can recommend up to **$350,000** per complaint; its recommendations are not binding. An Approved Person who changes firms must keep co-operating until the complaint is resolved.

**Reporting and records.**

| Who | Requirement | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Approved Person → Dealer | Report being the subject of a written client complaint | Within two business days |
| Dealer → CIRO | Report all client complaints except service complaints | As CIRO prescribes |
| Dealer | Keep a complaint file (name, date, nature, person, product, materials, interviews, decision) | Up-to-date central, readily accessible record for two years from receipt; a copy of each complaint file kept **seven years** |
| Dealer | Keep documents on matters reported to CIRO under 3703 | At least seven years from resolution |

**Prohibited practices.** An Approved Person needs the dealer's **prior written consent** to enter into any settlement with a client, whatever its form and whether or not it arises from a complaint. (An Approved Person authorized by the dealer to settle in the normal course, on matters not involving their own activities, is exempt.) A release may not include confidentiality terms that stop the client from complaining to regulators or pursuing a complaint.

> **Exam trap:** A settlement with a "gag clause" barring complaints to regulators is never allowed.

*Sources: IDPC 3702, 3703, 3710, 3711, 3720–3728, 3785, 3786; OBSI consumer pages*
