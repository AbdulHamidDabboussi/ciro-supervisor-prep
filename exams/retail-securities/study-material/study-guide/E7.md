# Element 7: Investment recommendations

This element applies the earlier elements to individual clients. It covers six tasks:

- Fit the objectives and the risk profile to a portfolio.
- Deal with behavioural biases and personal values.
- Make a suitable recommendation and explain it.
- Fund goals with time-value-of-money calculations.
- Use the Canadian tax rules.
- Handle complaints.

The element carries about **14 of the 120 questions**. Expect client scenarios and short calculations. Examples of calculations:

- an annuity payment;
- a capital gain;
- the tax on interest compared with the tax on dividends.

Use the on-screen basic or scientific calculator for these calculations. Every tax figure states its year. The sources do not give provincial dividend tax credits, so the tax examples are federal only.

Here, "the dealer" means the Investment Dealer (the IDPC's "Dealer Member"). CIRO guidance uses "Registered Individual" for Registered Representatives (RRs), Portfolio Managers and Associate Portfolio Managers together.

## 7.1 Objectives, risk profile and performance

### Investment objectives and needs

**Investment objectives** say what the client wants the money to do. CIRO's KYC guidance gives examples, "such as capital preservation, income generation, capital growth, or investing in accordance with their personal values". **Investment needs** include liquidity, which is the client's need to take cash out of the account (see 1.2). Without clear objectives, the RR cannot test whether an action is suitable (see 1.17).

### Specific and measurable goals

An RR and a client can agree on goals, for example in an investment policy statement. The guidance says agreed goals "should be specific and measurable". The RR should also consider a written record of the return assumptions that the goals need. If they set goals, the RR should tell the client about progress at regular intervals. A measurable goal, such as "$400,000 at age 65", shows a shortfall early, while there is still time to correct it.

### Risk profile

The risk profile has two parts: risk tolerance (the willingness to accept risk) and risk capacity (the ability to endure a financial loss). The risk profile is the lower of the two (see 1.3). One reason for this rule is that it protects the client from two failures:

- A client who is willing but not able can suffer a loss that they cannot absorb.
- A client who is able but not willing can panic and sell at the bottom of a market fall.

The guidance adds that "A client’s risk profile should not be manipulated to justify recommending higher-risk products".

### Performance

Expected **performance** follows from the risk taken. Do not expect a capital-preservation portfolio to earn equity-like returns.

Judge performance against a benchmark that matches the agreed asset mix (see 4.10 and 9.2). For example, compare a portfolio of 40% equity and 60% bonds with a blended benchmark of the same mix, not with an equity index.

A loss alone does not prove that an action was unsuitable. The guidance says that meeting the suitability obligation "does not guarantee any particular client outcome".

### Analyzing the fit

1. Start from the goal and find the return that the goal needs (see 7.8).
2. Ask whether the risk profile supports that return.
3. If it does not, change the goal: save more, retire later, or accept a smaller target.
4. Do not change the risk profile to fit the goal (see 7.5).
5. Measure progress against a matching benchmark and report it to the client.

> **Exam trap:** A goal that needs more return than the risk profile supports does **not** justify a riskier portfolio. Change the goal, not the profile.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Dagny Gallant is 58, has no employer pension, and wants $400,000 at 65. The goal needs about 7% a year. She says she is comfortable with risk. But her savings are small compared with her needs, so her risk capacity is low. Her risk profile is therefore low, and it supports about 4%.
>
> **Question.** Can her RR move her to an aggressive equity portfolio to reach the goal?
>
> **Resolution.** No. The risk profile is the lower of tolerance and capacity, so her high tolerance does not decide it. A portfolio above her profile is unsuitable, even if it could reach the goal. The RR explains the alternatives: save more, work to 67, or accept a smaller target. They record the discussion and agree a measurable revised goal.

*Sources: GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.3, 2.3.5, 3.1; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.2 Non-financial constraints

A client can limit their own choices for reasons that are not financial, usually personal values. CIRO's KYC guidance accepts values as a valid objective. It names "investing in accordance with environmental, social and governance criteria or other personal preferences". The guidance also says that "Dealers should provide their clients with the opportunity to express their investment needs and objectives in terms that are meaningful to them".

### Equity, diversity and inclusion considerations

An EDI preference favours issuers with diverse boards and management, and with inclusive policies. The client can apply it as a tilt toward the best issuers or as an exclusion of the worst. EDI data comes from issuer disclosure and third-party ratings. These sources can be incomplete, so two EDI products can hold very different securities. An EDI screen can also cause a style or sector bias.

### Environmental, social and governance criteria

ESG criteria look at three groups of issuer practices:

- **Environmental:** emissions, pollution and resource use.
- **Social:** treatment of employees, customers and communities.
- **Governance:** board independence, executive pay and shareholder rights.

An **exclusion screen** removes whole industries, such as tobacco, weapons or fossil fuels. A **best-in-class** approach keeps every sector but holds the issuers with the best ESG scores. A **thematic** fund holds one theme, such as clean energy. An **impact** fund aims for a measurable social or environmental result together with a financial return.

### Other personal preferences

Other preferences include:

- faith-based screens;
- the exclusion of certain companies;
- "buy Canadian".

Some faith-based screens exclude interest-bearing securities, which removes most fixed income. A "buy Canadian" rule adds home country bias (see 7.3). The Canadian market is heavy in financials and energy, so the portfolio becomes more concentrated.

### Analyzing the impact

- **Diversification:** fewer eligible securities usually mean more unsystematic risk (see 6.4).
- **Risk and return:** sector tilts change risk and return, so the portfolio return can be higher or lower than the market return.
- **Costs:** specialized funds often charge higher fees (see 5.10).
- **Benchmark:** a broad index may no longer be fair, so explain the tracking error or use a matching index.

In practice, do these steps:

1. Record the restriction on file.
2. Explain the trade-offs.
3. Check what an "ESG" product actually holds.

> **Exam trap:** A values-based restriction narrows the choices but never relaxes suitability. The portfolio must still fit the risk profile, the time horizon and the liquidity needs.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Torvald Akintola is 45, with a growth objective and a medium risk profile. He wants no fossil-fuel companies and prefers companies with diverse boards. A friend suggests that he move his whole portfolio into a concentrated clean-energy fund because it "matches his values".
>
> **Question.** How do the restrictions change the recommendation?
>
> **Resolution.** The RR records both restrictions. An energy exclusion removes a large part of the Canadian market, so returns will differ from a broad Canadian index. A diversified equity fund with a fossil-fuel screen and board-diversity criteria can respect his values and fit his risk profile. As his whole portfolio, the clean-energy fund adds sector risk above his profile. The values match does not make it suitable.

*Sources: GN-3400-21-004 s. 2.3.3; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.3 Behavioural finance biases

Behavioural finance studies predictable departures from rational decisions. Traditional theory assumes rational investors (see 6.9), but real clients use mental shortcuts and emotions. Biases can cause clients to make these errors, which lower returns:

- trade too much;
- keep investments that lose money;
- buy investments after a price rise;
- diversify too little.

Biases can also distort KYC answers, such as risk tolerance just after a market fall.

### Cognitive errors

Cognitive errors are faults in reasoning or memory. Information and education can often correct them.

| Bias | What it looks like | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Representativeness** | Judging by stereotype or a short pattern: "a great company must be a great stock" | Buys popular stocks at high prices |
| **Illusion of control** | Believing one can influence or predict largely random outcomes | Trades too much; holds too few securities |
| **Confirmation** | Seeking evidence that supports one's view and ignoring the rest | Ignores warning signs |
| **Hindsight** | "I knew it all along": past events seem more predictable than they were | Takes too much risk |

### Information processing biases

These biases come from the way a person receives and handles information. Present information neutrally and in more than one way. Show the whole portfolio, not one holding.

| Bias | What it looks like | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Framing** | Same choice judged differently by presentation ("90% chance of gain" vs "10% chance of loss") | Choices follow the wording, not the facts |
| **Anchoring** | Fixing on a reference number (purchase price, past high) and adjusting too little | Holds a loser until it "gets back" |
| **Mental accounting** | Treating money differently by source or "bucket" (spending a refund while carrying card debt) | Ignores the whole portfolio and debt cost |
| **Availability** | Over-weighting recent or vivid information, such as headlines | Buys after good news, sells after bad |

### Emotional biases

Emotional biases come from feelings, so they are harder to change than cognitive errors. These tools usually help to manage them:

- a written plan;
- automatic contributions;
- rebalancing rules;
- diversified products.

| Bias | What it looks like | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Loss aversion** | A loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases; holding losers to avoid realizing them | Sells winners early, keeps losers |
| **Overconfidence** | Over-rating one's skill or information | Higher trading costs and concentration |
| **Endowment** | Valuing what one owns (inherited or employer shares) above market value | Keeps a concentrated position |

### Capital market biases

These biases are errors in how investors read markets and past returns. Use complete data over long periods, including funds and companies that closed. Global diversification reduces home country bias.

| Bias | What it looks like | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Survivorship** | Judging a category only by surviving funds or companies | Expects returns that are too high |
| **Gambler's fallacy** | Expecting a reversal after a run ("it has fallen five days, so it must rise") | Trades against a trend for no reason |
| **Herding** | Following the crowd into popular assets, feeding bubbles and panics | Buys near highs, sells near lows |
| **Home country** | Over-weighting domestic securities (for Canadians, heavy in financials and energy) | Less diversification |

> **Exam trap:** Anchoring (fixation on a number) differs from loss aversion (the pain of a loss). Both can produce "I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid." Match the bias to the reason that the client states.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Evander Quist holds 60% of his portfolio in shares of his former employer. He says, "I would never buy these at today's price, but they are worth more to me." He will sell only when the price gets back to $80, its old high.
>
> **Question.** Which biases does Evander show?
>
> **Resolution.** He values shares he owns above their market value, which is the endowment bias. He fixes on a past high, which is anchoring. Endowment is emotional, so a plan works better than an argument. The RR shows the concentration risk and proposes a staged sale over time.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.4 Suitability in specific situations

Except in the accounts that IDPC 3404 exempts, every investment action or recommendation for a retail client must pass the IDPC 3402(1) suitability determination. It must also put the client's interest first (see 1.17). The factors include the client's KYC information and "the Dealer Member’s assessment of and an Approved Person’s understanding of the security" (see 1.16).

IDPC 3404 exempts these accounts from section 3402:

- order-execution-only (OEO) and direct electronic access accounts (the account-level test in 3402(3)(i) still applies);
- accounts at a dealer that is only a carrying broker or only gives execution, clearing, settlement or custody services.

### Selecting suitable products

The product's risk, liquidity, complexity and cost must fit the client's objectives, risk profile and time horizon. The test applies to the whole portfolio. IDPC 3402(4) looks at the "account portfolio of investments that would result from the investment action". A risk rating alone is not enough. The guidance says that "using the risk rating of a security as the only input in determining its suitability for a client is not sufficient".

If several products are suitable, the client's interest comes first, ahead of "a higher level of remuneration or other incentives".

### The impact of the product shelf

The RR must consider a reasonable range of alternatives that the dealer makes available.

> **Key rule:** "a consideration of a reasonable range of alternative actions available to the Registered Representative, Portfolio Manager, or Associate Portfolio Manager through the Dealer Member at the time the determination is made" (IDPC 3402(1)(i)(e))

The shelf filters products. If product due diligence finds a security unsuitable for all clients, the dealer "should not make the security available on its product shelf". Approved Persons "must not purchase for, or recommend securities to, a client unless the Dealer has approved those securities" (GN-3300-21-001).

The CSA calls trading in or recommending proprietary products an inherent conflict of interest, almost always a material one (see 1.23). The CSA expects dealers to be able to show that they address this conflict in the client's best interest. The relationship disclosure must say "whether the firm will primarily or exclusively provide proprietary products to the client" (IDPC 3216(5)(ii)(b)).

Some dealers offer only a few model portfolios. If it is clear that only one or a few of them are suitable, CIRO does not expect an in-depth review of alternatives.

### Net worth and liquidity needs

Net worth is assets minus liabilities. The RR may need to ask about assets and debts that the client holds outside the dealer. Low net worth usually means lower risk capacity (see 1.3).

Liquidity needs come from the KYC (see 1.2). The guidance says to consider "whether the client has other means to cover their expenditures, whether the needs are expected or unexpected". It also asks whether the money "will be withdrawn on a regular basis". Check that the recommendation leaves enough liquid assets for these needs. If not, the client may have to sell a volatile or illiquid asset at a bad time.

### Cash management planning and savings strategies

These are common personal-finance practices, not rules:

- Keep an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses (a rule of thumb) in cash, savings or short GICs.
- Pay down high-interest debt first.
- Save automatically, for example with a transfer on payday.
- Hold money needed within a few years in low-volatility assets.

The guidance also expects dealers to identify clients who are "more suited to placing their money in cash deposits or guaranteed products because they are unwilling or unable to accept the risk of loss of capital".

### Features of government pension programs

Government pensions give a base of lifetime income, so the portfolio needs to fund only the gap.

| Program | Key features |
|---|---|
| **CPP retirement pension** | Taxable, monthly, for life; age 60+ with one valid contribution. Standard start 65; early −0.6%/month (max −36% at 60); late +0.7%/month (max +42% at 70). Maximum at 65 (January 2026): $1,507.65/month. Pension sharing with a spouse can lower tax |
| **QPP retirement pension** (Quebec workers) | Normal age 65; from 60 reduced 0.5%–0.6% a month (30%–36% at 60); after 65 increased 0.7% a month, up to 58.8% at 72 |
| **OAS pension** | Age 65+, citizen or legal resident; 10 years' residence after 18 if living in Canada (20 if living abroad); full pension needs 40 years, otherwise partial (years ÷ 40). Deferral +0.6%/month to 36% at 70. Maximum (Oct–Dec 2026, age 65–74): $762.50/month. Taxable; 15% recovery tax on net income above the threshold ($93,454 for 2025 income; $95,323 for 2026 income) |
| **GIS / Allowance** | Income-tested; **not taxable**. GIS tops up low-income OAS pensioners. The Allowance is for a 60–64-year-old whose spouse or common-law partner receives GIS. The Allowance for the Survivor is for a 60–64-year-old whose spouse or common-law partner has died and who has not remarried or entered a common-law relationship |

The recovery tax takes 15% of the excess only. Net income of $10,000 over the 2025 threshold costs $1,500.

> **Exam trap:** CPP can start at 60, but OAS cannot start before 65. CPP deferral adds 0.7% a month, and OAS deferral adds 0.6%.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Winifred Halloran's dealer offers its own mutual funds and third-party funds. Her RR earns a higher payout on the dealer's own funds. Two balanced funds fit her KYC: a proprietary fund with a higher fee and a third-party fund with a lower fee.
>
> **Question.** Can the RR recommend the proprietary fund?
>
> **Resolution.** Only if it puts Winifred's interest first. Both funds are in the reasonable range of alternatives on the shelf. The higher payout cannot decide the choice, and the higher fee lowers her return. So the proprietary fund needs a client-centred reason. The RR records the comparison.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Jasper Ngata is 60, lives outside Quebec, and has just retired. He has CPP contributions and has lived in Canada all his adult life.
>
> **Question.** When should he start CPP and OAS?
>
> **Resolution.** CPP can start now, but with a 36% reduction for life. A start after 65 adds 0.7% a month, up to 42% at 70. OAS cannot start before 65, and deferral adds 0.6% a month, up to 36% at 70. If his savings can cover the early years, a later start gives a larger monthly pension for life. Whether it pays more in total depends on how long he lives.

*Sources: IDPC 3216(5), 3402(1), 3402(4), 3404; GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.2, 2.3.5, 3.1.1, 3.1.3, 3.2.4; GN-3300-21-001; Companion Policy 31-103CP; canada.ca CPP and OAS/GIS pages (2025–2026); Retraite Québec QPP pages (2026)*

## 7.5 Choosing a portfolio from KYC information

The dealer judges suitability on the whole account: registrants "cannot determine suitability on an individual trade/position basis". The process has five steps:

1. **Inputs:** needs and objectives, risk profile, time horizon, liquidity, tax position and restrictions.
2. **Current portfolio:** asset mix, concentration, risk level and costs of existing holdings.
3. **Shortfall:** project the current plan against the goal.
4. **Strategy:** choose the strategies that fit (below).
5. **Implement and monitor** with suitable products from the shelf.

### Investment needs, objectives and risk profile

The inputs come from the KYC (see 1.2 and 7.1). The time horizon controls how much risk the portfolio can take. The guidance says that "a short investment time horizon may mean that conservative investments may be the only suitable option".

### Current portfolio, risk level and potential shortfall

Review what the client already holds before you recommend anything new. Then project the current plan to the goal date with realistic returns (see 7.8). The difference between the projected value and the goal is the **shortfall**. To close it, save more, extend the horizon or lower the target. Add risk only if the risk profile allows it.

### Investment management strategies

- **Strategic asset mix:** a long-term target mix of cash, fixed income and equity that matches the risk profile (see 6.1).
- **Passive or active management:** passive management follows an index at low cost. Active management tries to get a higher return than the index (see 6.10 and 6.11).
- **Laddering:** bonds or GICs with staggered maturities, which give regular cash. A ladder balances interest-rate risk and reinvestment risk. Some reinvestment risk remains, because a part matures each year (see 6.12).
- **Dollar-cost averaging:** a fixed amount invested at regular intervals, which buys more units when prices are low.

### Resolving conflicts between expectations and risk profile

When the client expects more return than the risk profile supports, follow the six steps in 1.3. For the choice of portfolio, the main step is this: do not override the risk profile to reach a return target. Instead, close the shortfall in the ways that the section above describes.

### The benefit of cash flow analysis

**Cash flow analysis** compares after-tax income with spending to find the surplus that the client can invest. It shows whether a goal is realistic and how much liquidity the portfolio must supply. For example, $6,500 a month after tax less $5,500 of spending leaves $1,000 a month, or $12,000 a year.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Marisol Tkachuk is 40. She earns $6,500 a month after tax and spends $5,500. She wants to retire at 55 and expects 9% a year. Her risk profile is medium-low, and she holds one technology stock. A projection at a return that fits her profile shows a shortfall at 55.
>
> **Question.** How should the RR build her portfolio?
>
> **Resolution.** Cash flow analysis shows $1,000 a month to invest. The RR discusses risk and return and does not raise her profile to close the shortfall. The alternatives are to retire at 60, spend less, or accept a smaller target.
>
> The portfolio uses a balanced mix, monthly dollar-cost averaging, and a staged sale of the single stock. The RR records the discussion and her choice.

*Sources: GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.3.5, 2.3.6, 3.1.4; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.6 Factors in addressing investment actions

After the first portfolio, the client still buys, sells, switches and holds. The guidance says that an investment action "also includes a recommendation or decision to hold securities". Each action needs a suitability determination, except in the accounts that IDPC 3404 exempts (see 7.4).

### Significant changes to the client's KYC information

IDPC 3402(2) requires a review of the account after a trigger event (list in 1.21). Two triggers matter most here:

- the dealer becomes aware of a change in the client's KYC information that could make a holding or the account fail 3402(1);
- a deposit or transfer of securities into the account.

The guidance says a "significant change" includes a change to the risk profile, the time horizon, or the needs and objectives. It also includes any other change that could reasonably and significantly affect net worth or income.

The dealer must take reasonable steps to keep the KYC information current. After the dealer becomes aware of a significant change, it must update the KYC within a reasonable time (IDPC 3209(3), see 1.7). The guidance gives a job loss as an example. The dealer "should consider restricting activities in the client’s account to liquidating trades, transfers or disbursements" until it re-evaluates the KYC.

Then ask these questions:

- Does each holding still fit?
- What does a sale or switch cost?
- Which alternatives exist?

> **Exam trap:** After a trigger event, the review covers the existing holdings, not only new purchases.

### Unexpected impact on the account: concentration and liquidity

A trade that seems reasonable can over-concentrate the account. It can also put money that the client needs into an illiquid security. The rule makes this impact a factor in every suitability determination.

> **Key rule:** "the impact of the action on the retail client’s account, including the concentration of securities, derivatives or precious metals bullion, within the account and the liquidity of those securities, derivatives or precious metals bullion" (IDPC 3402(1)(i)(c))

The guidance says: "Over-concentration in any one security, sector or industry can have a significant negative impact on the risk and liquidity in a client’s account." Dealers should set and monitor concentration and liquidity thresholds. The guidance names three examples of over-concentration:

- "illiquid exempt market securities as compared to more liquid publicly traded securities";
- securities of a single issuer or a group of related issuers;
- issuers that give exposure to a single industry or asset class.

Consider all of the client's accounts at the dealer. In general, the higher the concentration, the more steps the dealer or the RR should take and document to show suitability.

### The potential and actual impact of costs

"Cost" has a broad meaning. It includes direct and indirect fees, commissions, trailing commissions, and compensation to the dealer or RR, whoever pays it.

> **Key rule:** "the potential and actual impact of costs on the retail client’s returns" (IDPC 3402(1)(i)(d))

The guidance says: "Costs can significantly impact a client’s return over time." A fee decreases the return every year, so the loss compounds. The RR "must document the reasonable basis for their conclusions".

### A reasonable range of alternative actions

RRs must consider alternatives available through the dealer. A reasonable range depends on the securities and services that the dealer offers, the RR's skill and proficiency, and the client's circumstances. In practice, alternatives can include:

- cash;
- no action;
- a cheaper product with the same exposure.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Bogdan Lazarev, 52, holds a growth portfolio with an exempt-market real estate investment. He tells his RR that he lost his job and will need his savings within a year.
>
> **Question.** What must the dealer do?
>
> **Resolution.** The job loss is a significant change that lowers his risk capacity and shortens his horizon. The dealer updates the KYC and reviews every existing holding against 3402(1). Until then, it should consider a restriction to liquidating trades, transfers or disbursements. The illiquid exempt investment is a problem because he needs cash soon. The RR compares the cost of each sale with the alternatives and documents the decisions.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ifeoma Nwosu has a medium risk profile. She transfers in an account where one bank stock is 70% of her portfolio. She asks to buy more of the same stock.
>
> **Question.** Can the RR buy more of the stock for her?
>
> **Resolution.** The transfer-in is a trigger, so the dealer reviews the account. The RR weighs the concentration, the costs, and alternatives such as a diversified fund. Adding to a 70% single-issuer position does not meet 3402(1) for her profile, so they do not recommend it. If Ifeoma still instructs the purchase, the 3402(5) steps apply (see 7.7).

*Sources: IDPC 3402(1), 3402(2), 3209, 3404; GN-3400-21-004 ss. 2.11.2, 3.1.2, 3.1.4, 3.2.3–3.2.5*

## 7.7 The recommendation process

A sound recommendation covers four points.

### The pros and cons of each product and action

Say what the client gains, such as income, growth, diversification or tax efficiency. Say what the client gives up, such as liquidity, upside or money paid in fees. In an advisory account, the guidance asks the RR to do three things:

- Disclose "any material negative and positive factors involved in the transaction".
- Make sure the client understands the risks, especially a client with limited investment experience.
- Document this disclosure.

A recommendation that shows only the benefits fails this standard.

### The risks of each product and action

The Approved Person should be able to explain a security's "structure, features, risks, initial and ongoing costs and the impact of those costs" (GN-3300-21-001). The guidance gives examples:

- **Features**, "including potential returns, use of leverage, conflicts of interest, investment time horizon, overall complexity of the security".
- **Risks**, "including the possibility that clients may lose some or all of the principal amount invested, liquidity risk, redemption risk".
- **Costs**, such as commissions, sales charges, trailing commissions and management fees.

### What the recommended action can accomplish

Tie the action to the client's goal in measurable terms. For example, say "this moves you from 80% to 60% equity, which matches your risk profile after retirement". A clear link to the goal lets the client judge the advice and lets the dealer supervise it.

### Securing the client's commitment

1. Summarize the recommendation and its reasons.
2. Check that the client understands it.
3. Answer the client's questions.
4. Confirm the client's decision.
5. Document the recommendation, the disclosure and the decision.

Do not put pressure on the client. Get the commitment only after the client understands the recommendation.

The client can instead give an instruction that fails 3402(1). Then the dealer may carry it out only after it does the three IDPC 3402(5) steps (see 1.18). The last step is recorded confirmation of the client's instruction to proceed.

The RR does not have to accept such an order. The guidance says that it is "not sufficient for the Registered Individual to mark the order as “unsolicited”". The 3402(5) steps still apply.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Lars Eklund, 64, retires next month with a new medium-low risk profile. His portfolio is 80% equity, including 30% in one technology stock. For his profile, a 30% position in one stock makes the account unsuitable. His RR recommends 60% equity, a bond ladder, and a staged sale of most of the stock.
>
> **Question.** What must the recommendation include, and what if Lars refuses to sell the stock?
>
> **Resolution.** The RR explains the gains: lower volatility, regular income and less single-stock risk. They explain what he gives up: some upside, plus trading costs and possible capital gains tax (see 7.10). They explain each product's risks, such as the interest rate risk of the bonds, and link the plan to his income goal. If Lars instructs the dealer to keep the stock, this is an investment action (a decision to hold). Keeping the stock does not meet 3402(1), so the dealer must complete the three 3402(5) steps before it follows his instruction.

*Sources: GN-3300-21-001; GN-3400-21-004 ss. 3.1.2, 3.2.1, 3.10; IDPC 3402(5); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.8 Time value of money for financial goals

Many goals are a series of equal payments. A client saves the same amount each year for a future goal, or takes the same income each year from a single sum. Both are **annuities**. The exam calculator has no TVM keys, so use the formulas (general TVM formulas: 2.9).

**Convention.** These formulas assume an **ordinary annuity**, with payments at the end of each period. The rate per period must match the payment frequency. For monthly payments, use r ÷ 12 and n × 12, which assumes a nominal annual rate compounded monthly. For an effective annual rate, the monthly rate is (1 + r)^(1/12) − 1. The examples use annual payments and annual compounding.

```
Future value of an annuity:   FV = PMT × [((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r]
Present value of an annuity:  PV = PMT × [(1 − (1 + r)^−n) ÷ r]
Deposit needed for a goal:    PMT = FV × r ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1)
Income from a lump sum:       PMT = PV × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
where PMT = payment per period, r = rate per period, n = number of periods
```

### Regular investment to meet a known future objective

The goal is a future value, such as a home down payment or a known liability. Each deposit earns interest until the goal date, so the deposits grow to more than their total. The "deposit needed" formula solves the future value of an annuity for the payment.

> **Worked example**
> **Saving for a known goal.** Ana needs $50,000 in 10 years. She earns 5% a year and deposits at each year-end.
> - (1.05)^10 = 1.628895
> - PMT = 50,000 × 0.05 ÷ (1.628895 − 1) = 2,500 ÷ 0.628895 = **$3,975.23 a year**

### Regular income from a single investment

The client has a single sum today and wants equal payments that use it up over a set period. This is the present value of an annuity, solved for the payment.

> **Worked example**
> **Income from a single investment.** Ulrich invests $300,000 at 4%. He wants equal year-end payments for 20 years. After the last payment, the balance is zero.
> - (1.04)^−20 = 0.456387
> - PMT = 300,000 × 0.04 ÷ (1 − 0.456387) = 12,000 ÷ 0.543613 = **$22,074.53 a year**
> - If payments start immediately (annuity due), divide the unrounded payment by 1.04: 22,074.5251 ÷ 1.04 = $21,225.50.

Higher returns or longer horizons reduce the deposit needed. Use realistic after-fee return assumptions that fit the risk profile (see 7.1). An optimistic rate makes the deposit look small and hides a shortfall.

> **Exam trap:** For the same present value, beginning-of-period payments (annuity due) are smaller than end-of-period payments.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Hiroko Sato is 70 and has $150,000. A 3% annual return fits her low risk profile. She wants $12,000 at the end of each year for 15 years. After the last payment, the balance is zero.
>
> **Question.** Can the sum support that income?
>
> **Resolution.** Use the income formula with PV = 150,000, r = 0.03 and n = 15. First, (1.03)^−15 = 0.641862. Then PMT = 150,000 × 0.03 ÷ (1 − 0.641862) = 4,500 ÷ 0.358138 = $12,564.99 a year. The sum supports a little more than $12,000, so the goal is realistic without more risk.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.9 Personal and corporate tax planning

Tax planning increases the after-tax return without extra investment risk. Limits change each year, so every figure states its year.

### Tax-preferential accounts and their uses

| | FHSA | RRSP | TFSA | RESP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | First home | Retirement | Any | Post-secondary education |
| Who | Resident, 18+, first-time buyer, 71 or younger in the year opened | Contribute until Dec 31 of the year you turn 71 | Resident, 18+; no earned income needed | Subscriber for a beneficiary |
| Limit | $8,000 a year, plus up to $8,000 of unused room carried forward; $40,000 lifetime | New room each year: lesser of 18% of prior-year earned income and the dollar limit ($33,810 for 2026), minus any pension adjustment; unused room carries forward | $7,000 for 2025 and 2026; withdrawals restored next Jan 1 | No annual limit; $50,000 lifetime per beneficiary |
| Deductible? | Yes (RRSP transfers in are not) | Yes; contributions up to 60 days after year-end count for that year | No | No |
| Withdrawals | Qualifying home withdrawal tax-free, no repayment | Taxable; withholding 10/20/30% (outside Quebec); HBP up to $60,000 | Tax-free | Contributions back tax-free; EAPs taxable to the student |
| Excess | 1% per month | 1% per month above room + $2,000 | 1% per month | 1% per month on the excess over $50,000 (each subscriber on their share) |

The accounts give two different advantages:

- **Deferral (RRSP).** The contribution reduces tax now. Income in the plan "is usually exempt from tax as long as the funds remain in the plan". Withdrawals are taxed later, often at a lower rate in retirement.
- **Tax-free growth (TFSA).** There is no deduction, but income and withdrawals are tax-free. TFSA income "will not impact your federal income-tested benefits and credits", which helps a retiree near the OAS recovery threshold (see 7.4).

The FHSA gives both: a deduction in and a tax-free qualifying withdrawal out. The holder's maximum participation period ends on December 31 of the year of the earliest of these events:

- the 15th anniversary of opening the holder's first FHSA;
- the holder turns 71;
- the year after the first qualifying withdrawal.

The holder should close all FHSAs before this period ends. Up to the end of this period, the holder can generally transfer the property directly to an RRSP or RRIF tax-deferred. During this period, the holder can instead take a taxable withdrawal. Property left after the period ends is taxable income at its fair market value.

For an RESP, the CESG adds 20% of the first $2,500 contributed each year (lifetime maximum $7,200). An accumulated income payment to the subscriber is taxed at the subscriber's rate plus 20% (12% in Quebec).

In the year the holder turns 71, the holder must choose one of these options for the RRSP:

- withdraw it;
- transfer it to a RRIF;
- use it to buy an annuity.

### Claiming all deductions and credits

Make sure the client claims every deduction and credit that applies. A deduction reduces taxable income. A credit reduces the tax itself. Common items for investors:

- **RRSP and FHSA contributions** are generally deductible. An RRSP-to-FHSA transfer is not.
- **Dividend tax credit:** for dividends from taxable Canadian corporations, not foreign dividends (see 7.11).
- **Foreign tax credit:** for foreign income tax paid on income reported in Canada (see 7.11).
- **Pension income amount:** on RRIF income, if the client was 65 or older on December 31 of the year.

### Income splitting

Income splitting moves income from a high-rate family member to a lower-rate one. The **attribution rules** limit it:

- Income on property transferred or lent to a spouse is taxed back to the transferor. So are the spouse's capital gains on it.
- Income on property transferred or lent to a related minor under 18 is also taxed back.
- A minor's capital gains are **not** attributed, so a gift of growth assets to children can split income.

The main exceptions to attribution are:

- A **fair market value transfer**. The consideration must be worth at least the property's fair market value. A debt in the consideration needs the same interest terms as a loan (below). For a spouse, the transferor must also elect out of the spousal rollover.
- A **loan** at no less than the prescribed rate in effect when it was made, or a lower arm's-length rate. The borrower must pay each year's interest within 30 days after the year-end.

The CRA sets prescribed rates each quarter. For October–December 2026, its rate for low-interest loans is 3%.

Other tools:

- **Spousal RRSPs.** A contribution in the year of a withdrawal or the two years before can matter. In that case, the contributor may have to include all or part of the withdrawal in income.
- **Pension income splitting** of up to 50% of eligible pension income.
- **CPP sharing.**

### Tax-loss harvesting

Tax-loss harvesting means selling losers to offset realized gains. The **superficial loss** rule denies the loss if both of these occur:

1. You or an affiliated person buys, or has a right to buy, identical property within 30 days before or after the sale. Affiliated persons include a spouse and a controlled corporation.
2. You or the affiliated person still owns it, or has a right to buy it, 30 days after the sale.

If the client is the person who buys the substitute property, the client can usually add the denied loss to its ACB. A $5,000 denied loss on shares rebought for $10,500 gives an ACB of $15,500. The rule stops a client who keeps the same investment from claiming the loss.

### Corporations: active and passive income, integration, notional accounts

**Active vs passive.** A CCPC's active business income gets the small business deduction. Its net federal rate is 9% instead of the 15% general rate (CRA rates page, October 2026). It applies on up to $500,000 a year (the business limit). Associated corporations share one business limit. Passive investment income generally does not get the deduction.

High passive income also reduces the business limit. The test uses the prior-year adjusted aggregate investment income of the corporation and its associated corporations. Each $1 over $50,000 reduces the limit by $5, so the limit is gone at $150,000.

**Integration** means that income earned through a corporation and paid out as dividends should bear about the same total tax as income earned directly. The corporate tax rate depends on the type of income: small business, general, or investment income. The gross-up approximates the corporate income before tax. The dividend tax credit then allows for the corporate tax already paid (see 7.11). So general-rate income pays eligible dividends, with the larger gross-up and credit, and small-business income pays non-eligible dividends. On investment income, refundable taxes return part of the corporate tax when the corporation pays dividends.

**Notional accounts** track amounts that the corporation can later pay out with a tax benefit:

- **RDTOH:** part of the corporate tax on investment income is refunded when the corporation pays taxable dividends, up to its refundable account balances. **NERDTOH** holds mainly the refundable Part I tax on investment income, plus Part IV tax on non-eligible dividends received. Only non-eligible dividends recover NERDTOH. **ERDTOH** holds mainly Part IV tax on eligible dividends received. Any taxable dividend recovers it, and eligible dividends draw only on ERDTOH.
- **GRIP:** income taxed at the general rate, payable as *eligible* dividends.
- **CDA:** mainly the non-taxable half of capital gains, paid by election as tax-free *capital dividends*.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Nkechi Abara is 28, is a resident of Canada, rents her home, and has never owned one. She has $8,000 to save this year and plans to buy a home in four years.
>
> **Question.** Which account should she use first?
>
> **Resolution.** The FHSA fits best, because she is a resident, 18 or older and a first-time buyer. Her contribution is deductible, and a qualifying withdrawal is tax-free with no repayment. The RRSP Home Buyers' Plan, by contrast, needs repayment.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ezekiel Szabo is in the top tax bracket. His spouse has no income. He wants to give her $200,000 to invest so that the income is taxed at her rate.
>
> **Question.** How can he shift the investment income to his spouse?
>
> **Resolution.** A gift does not work, because income and capital gains on it are taxed back to Ezekiel. A loan at no less than the prescribed rate in effect when he makes it avoids attribution. His spouse must pay each year's interest within 30 days after the year-end. Ezekiel reports that interest. The return above the loan rate is taxed at her lower rate.

## 7.10 Capital gains

Only part of a capital gain is taxed, so a gain usually bears less tax than the same amount of interest (see 7.11).

### Calculation of capital gains and capital losses

```
Capital gain (loss) = Proceeds of disposition − (ACB + outlays and expenses of sale)
Taxable capital gain = Capital gain × inclusion rate
ACB per unit (identical properties) = Total cost of units held ÷ Number of units held
```

A sale for more than the ACB plus the outlays and expenses of sale gives a capital gain. A sale for less gives a capital loss.

The **adjusted cost base (ACB)** includes purchase costs such as commissions. Identical properties use the average cost. CRA's most common examples are "shares of the same class of the capital stock of a corporation or units of a mutual fund trust". For funds, units of the same class or series are identical properties (see 5.11). Recalculate the average ACB after each purchase. A sale does not change the average ACB of the units that remain.

Other ACB rules:

- A return of capital reduces the ACB. For units of a mutual fund trust, a negative ACB is a deemed capital gain (see 5.11).
- Convert foreign amounts to Canadian dollars at the rate when each amount arose.

> **Worked example**
> Jon buys 200 shares at $20 ($50 commission), then 300 at $25 ($50 commission).
> - ACB = (4,050 + 7,550) ÷ 500 = $23.20 per share.
> - He sells 400 at $30 with a $60 commission. Proceeds $12,000; ACB of shares sold = 400 × 23.20 = $9,280.
> - Gain = 12,000 − 9,280 − 60 = $2,660. Taxable capital gain (2025, 1/2) = **$1,330**.
> - The 100 remaining shares keep an ACB of $23.20.

### Tax treatment of gains and losses

For 2025, the inclusion rate is generally **1/2**. The same fraction of a capital loss is the **allowable capital loss**. Capital losses first reduce capital gains of the year, not other income. A loss that remains becomes part of the **net capital loss**. The client can carry a net capital loss back three years or forward indefinitely, against taxable capital gains only.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** In 2025, Solveig Berg has a capital gain of $8,000 on shares and a capital loss of $11,000 on fund units.
>
> **Question.** What is the tax result for 2025?
>
> **Resolution.** At the 2025 inclusion rate of 1/2, the taxable capital gain is $4,000 and the allowable capital loss is $5,500. The loss reduces the gain to zero. The remaining $1,500 cannot reduce her salary. It becomes part of her 2025 net capital loss. She can apply it to taxable capital gains of the three previous years or any future year.

### Strategies for minimizing capital gains tax

- Realize losses against gains, but avoid the superficial loss rule (see 7.9).
- Time sales into lower-income years, for example after retirement.
- Hold growth assets in a TFSA, where gains are tax-free.
- Use an RRSP with care: it defers tax, but withdrawals are fully taxable income, so the inclusion-rate advantage is lost.
- Donate appreciated listed securities or fund units to a qualified donee, for a zero inclusion rate on the gain.
- Use the lower-rate spouse, within the attribution rules (see 7.9).

> **Exam trap:** The CRA says you cannot "claim a deduction for capital losses within your RRSP" (T4040). So an RRSP loss cannot offset a gain in a non-registered account. In a TFSA, income, including capital gains, is generally tax-free (see 7.9).

*Sources: CRA capital gains and capital losses pages (2025); CRA "Tax treatment of mutual funds"; CRA T4037 (2025); CRA T4040 (2025); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 7.11 Income tax on investment income

Each type of investment income has its own tax treatment:

- Interest is taxed in full.
- Foreign interest and dividends are also taxed in full.
- Canadian dividends get a dividend tax credit.
- Only part of a capital gain is taxed (see 7.10).

Compare investments on their after-tax return.

### Calculation of income tax payable

Each federal rate applies only to the part of taxable income in its bracket. Provincial tax is added to the federal tax. The **marginal rate** is the rate on the next dollar of income. Use it when you compare types of investment income.

| 2026 federal taxable income | Rate |
|---|---|
| $0 – $58,523 | 14% |
| $58,523.01 – $117,045 | 20.5% |
| $117,045.01 – $181,440 | 26% |
| $181,440.01 – $258,482 | 29% |
| Over $258,482 | 33% |

For 2025, the lowest rate is 14.5%, because it fell from 15% to 14% on July 1, 2025.

> **Worked example**
> **Federal tax on 2026 taxable income of $70,000.**
> - First bracket: 58,523 × 14% = 8,193.22
> - Second bracket: (70,000 − 58,523) × 20.5% = 11,477 × 20.5% = 2,352.79
> - Federal tax before credits = 8,193.22 + 2,352.79 = **$10,546.01**. Non-refundable credits, such as the dividend tax credit, are then subtracted.

### Tax treatment of interest

**Interest** is fully taxable at the marginal rate. Some multi-year investments, such as compound GICs, pay interest only at maturity. For these investments, the client reports the interest earned in each complete investment year, from anniversary to anniversary (see 2.4). For example, a client who invests on July 1, 2024 reports the interest earned up to June 30, 2025 on the 2025 return. So the client pays tax before the cash arrives.

### Tax treatment of dividends: qualifying, non-qualifying and foreign

**Canadian dividends** are grossed up, and a dividend tax credit (DTC) then reduces the tax (see 3.6). This guide reads the syllabus terms "qualifying" and "non-qualifying" as the CRA's eligible and non-eligible ("other than eligible") dividends. An eligible dividend is one "that is designated by that corporation to be an eligible dividend".

| | Eligible | Non-eligible ("other than eligible") |
|---|---|---|
| Gross-up (2025 and 2026) | 38% (× 1.38) | 15% (× 1.15) |
| Federal DTC (ITA s. 121) | 6/11 of the gross-up (15.0198% of the grossed-up amount) | 9/13 of the gross-up (9.0301% of the grossed-up amount) |
| Typical source | Income taxed at the general corporate rate (a CCPC pays these from GRIP) | CCPC income that used the small business deduction |

The gross-up and credit allow for the corporate tax already paid (integration, see 7.9).

**Foreign dividends** get no gross-up and no DTC. The client reports them in Canadian dollars and pays tax on the full amount, like interest ($205 in the example below). The client may claim foreign tax withheld as a foreign tax credit, within limits (withholding and registered plans: 5.10).

> **Worked example**
> **$1,000 of each, 2026 federal tax only, 20.5% bracket.**
> - Interest: 1,000 × 20.5% = **$205.00**
> - Eligible dividend: 1,380 × 20.5% = 282.90; DTC = 6/11 × 380 = 207.27; tax = **$75.63**
> - Non-eligible dividend: 1,150 × 20.5% = 235.75; DTC = 9/13 × 150 = 103.85; tax = **$131.90**
> - Capital gain (1/2 inclusion, the rate CRA T4037 states for 2025; the sources give no 2026 rate): 500 × 20.5% = **$102.50**

> **Exam trap:** The gross-up raises net income although the client receives no extra cash. This can increase income-tested clawbacks such as the OAS recovery tax.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Wojciech Asante is 68, receives OAS, and his net income is already above the OAS recovery threshold. He plans to replace $10,000 of interest with $10,000 of eligible dividends from Canadian bank shares.
>
> **Question.** What happens to his net income?
>
> **Resolution.** The dividends are grossed up, so 10,000 × 1.38 = $13,800 goes into net income instead of $10,000. The extra $3,800 can cost up to 15%, or $570, in OAS recovery tax. The dividend tax credit still lowers his income tax. The RR shows both effects and considers holding the shares in a TFSA.

*Sources: CRA tax rates and brackets (2025, 2026); CRA lines 12000/12010, 12100, 40425, 40500; CRA eligible dividends page; ITA ss. 82(1), 121*

## 7.12 Complaints handling

These rules help protect clients. They also help the dealer find problems early (see Process). IDPC Rule 3700, Part E, sets the rules for retail clients.

### Recognition

Part E applies to two forms of complaint by a retail client, or by a person authorized to act for the client:

- a *recorded* expression of dissatisfaction that alleges misconduct;
- a *verbal* expression of dissatisfaction that alleges misconduct, where "a preliminary investigation indicates that the allegation may have merit".

Alleged misconduct "includes, but is not limited to":

- breach of confidentiality, theft, fraud, misappropriation, forgery, unsuitable investments, misrepresentation and unauthorized trading;
- "other inappropriate financial dealings with clients";
- "engaging in Dealer Member related activities outside of the Dealer Member".

A matter in civil action or arbitration is not a complaint here. Dealers also need policies for complaints that allege no misconduct.

### Process

Supervisory or compliance staff handle complaints. The compliance department gets a copy of each complaint. A **designated complaints officer** must have "the requisite experience and authority to oversee the complaint handling process".

Under IDPC 3723(2), the dealer's policies and procedures must address:

- a fair and thorough investigation, and an assessment of the merits;
- how to decide the offer when a complaint has merit, and remedial actions within the dealer;
- a procedure so that complaints "are not dismissed without proper consideration of the facts";
- a balanced approach that objectively considers the interests of all parties;
- a process that makes sure the relevant employees, Approved Persons and their Supervisors know about all complaints from their clients;
- procedures to tell an appropriate Executive about any serious misconduct;
- procedures to monitor the general nature of complaints.

In either of these cases, the dealer must review its internal procedures and practices (IDPC 3723(3)):

- the dealer determines that the number or severity of complaints is significant;
- the dealer detects frequent, repeated complaints on the same or similar matters that together may show a serious problem.

The dealer must also send recommendations to fix the problem to the appropriate management level.

At account opening, the dealer gives each new client a written summary of its complaint procedures. The client also gets the CIRO-approved brochure.

The dealer must send an **acknowledgement letter within five business days** of receipt of a complaint. The letter names the person who handles the complaint. It also states the 90-day timeline.

### Resolution

The substantive response letter must be "fair, clear and not misleading". The CIRO-approved brochure goes with it. The letter gives a summary, the investigation result, and the final decision with reasons. It also lists the options if the client is not satisfied:

- arbitration or civil action;
- a complaint to CIRO;
- the ombudsman service (OBSI), if the client asks within the OBSI time limit;
- an affiliate's internal ombudsman, if any, with its voluntary use and expected length explained;
- any other options that apply.

> **Key rule:** "A Dealer Member must respond to each client complaint as soon as possible and not later than 90 days from the date of receipt of the complaint" (IDPC 3726(4))

The 90 days include all internal processes of the dealer that the client can use, except an affiliate's internal ombudsman. If the dealer cannot respond within 90 days, it must tell the client the reasons for the delay and a new estimated date. It must also tell CIRO the reasons for the delay.

A client can go to OBSI with no final response if the dealer has not responded within 90 days. For Quebec residents, the dealer has 60 days. In exceptional circumstances, this can extend to 90 days. After a final response, the client has **180 days** to go to OBSI. OBSI can recommend up to **$350,000** per complaint, but its recommendations are not binding.

If an Approved Person moves to another dealer, they must continue to co-operate with the old dealer until the dealer resolves the complaint.

### Reporting

| Who | Requirement | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Approved Person → Dealer | Report being the subject of a written client complaint | Within two business days |
| Approved Person → Dealer | Report a client complaint (written or other form) about another Approved Person that alleges theft, fraud, misappropriation, forgery, money laundering, market manipulation, insider trading, misrepresentation or unauthorized trading | Within two business days |
| Dealer → CIRO | Report all client complaints except service complaints | As CIRO prescribes |
| Dealer | Keep a complaint file (name, date, nature, person, product, materials, interviews, decision) | Up-to-date central, readily accessible record for two years from receipt; a copy of each complaint file kept **seven years** |
| Dealer | Keep documents on matters reported to CIRO under 3703 | At least seven years from resolution |

### Prohibited practices

An Approved Person needs the dealer's **prior written consent** to enter into any settlement with a client. This applies whatever the form, and whether or not the settlement comes from a complaint. One exemption applies. It covers an Approved Person whom the dealer authorizes to settle in the normal course, on matters outside their own activities.

A release must not include confidentiality terms that prevent the client from:

- making a complaint to securities regulators, SROs or other enforcement authorities;
- continuing a pending complaint;
- taking part in further proceedings of these authorities.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Fenella Marsh phones her dealer. She says her RR sold her bond fund without her permission. She sends nothing in writing. The dealer's preliminary investigation finds no instruction on record.
>
> **Question.** Is this a Part E complaint?
>
> **Resolution.** It is a verbal allegation of unauthorized trading, which is misconduct. The preliminary investigation indicates that the allegation may have merit, so Part E applies. The dealer acknowledges it within five business days and responds within 90 days. It also reports the complaint to CIRO and keeps the complaint file.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A client complains in writing to Gideon Pelletier, an RR, about an unsuitable investment. Gideon offers to pay part of her loss himself if she agrees never to contact CIRO.
>
> **Question.** What must Gideon do, and is the offer allowed?
>
> **Resolution.** Gideon must report the written complaint to the dealer within two business days. He needs the dealer's prior written consent before any settlement, and the exemption does not apply to his own activities. The dealer could not approve the term either, because a release must not prevent a complaint to regulators.

*Sources: IDPC 3702, 3703, 3710, 3711, 3720–3728, 3785, 3786; OBSI consumer pages*
