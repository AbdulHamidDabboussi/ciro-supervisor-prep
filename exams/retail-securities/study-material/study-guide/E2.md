# Element 2: Fixed income

Government, corporate and deposit-type debt: features, risks and costs, special-feature bonds, the IDPC debt-market rules, and bond arithmetic. About **10 of the 120 questions**: definitions, scenarios and short calculations that work step by step on the basic/scientific on-screen calculator.

## 2.1 Regulatory requirements for debt markets

**General requirements.** IDPC Rule 7100 sets trading and settlement practices "to promote fair and efficient debt securities markets", for institutional and retail markets alike unless a provision says otherwise. A Dealer Member's debt trading must breach no applicable law, regulation, direction or requirement, binding or not (including Bank of Canada or Department of Finance directions), and it must not condone or knowingly facilitate breaches of Rule 7100 by affiliates, clients or counterparties.

**Policies and procedures** must specifically address debt-market trading and conduct, giving reasonable assurance of compliance, and cover:

| Required content | Rule |
|---|---|
| Controls on non-client account trading; no use of inside information; no front-running | 7103(2)(i)–(iii) |
| Fair allocation of new issues; prompt, accurate conflict disclosure | 7103(2)(iv)–(v) |
| Retail: written mark-up, mark-down and commission guidelines for Registered Representatives, plus monitoring to detect charges above the firm's maximums | 7103(2)(vi) |
| Confidential information restricted to staff who need it; trading by designated personnel confined to restricted-access office areas; secure communications | 7105(2) |

An Executive of the relevant business group approves them; they are reviewed regularly for fit with the business's size, nature and complexity. The rule also requires qualified, trained and supervised traders (7104), client duties ahead of the dealer's interests (7107(2)), and monitoring of staff debt trading, and prompt reporting of breaches of CIRO requirements or of suspicious or irregular market conduct to CIRO or another authority with jurisdiction, including the Bank of Canada (7113).

**Prohibited practices: obligations on the Investment Dealer.** The dealer must not:

- engage in manipulative or deceptive practices (e.g. trades that artificially inflate volume or move prices, false rumours, collusion);
- take unfair advantage of clients, e.g. trading for itself ahead of a same-side client order without disclosure and client approval (compare front running under UMIR 4.1: 8.1);
- accept an order or trade it knows, or has reasonable grounds to believe, would breach CIRO requirements or the law;
- offer consideration to a partner, director, officer, employee, agent or shareholder of a client (or their associates) without the client's prior written consent.

**Prohibited practices: obligation on the Approved Person.**

> **Key rule:** "An Approved Person or employee of a Dealer Member must not accept any material consideration, including remuneration, gratuity or benefit, from any person other than the Dealer Member for any activities conducted on behalf of a client." (IDPC 7112(2))

Infrequent non-monetary items of minimal value that would not make a reasonable person question whether a conflict exists are not consideration, whether accepted (7112(2)) or offered by the firm (7112(3)). Compare the general ban on accepting consideration in IDPC 3115 (1.25).

*Sources: IDPC 7101(1), 7102, 7103, 7104, 7105(2), 7107(2), 7109, 7110, 7112, 7113*

## 2.2 Types and features of fixed-income products

| Issuer | Main products | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| **Government of Canada** | T-bills; marketable bonds (benchmark terms about 2 to 30 years); Real Return Bonds (existing issues still trade) | Highest quality, most liquid; T-bills sold at a discount, mature at par; set the benchmark yield curve |
| **Provincial** | Bonds, T-bills, agency debt (often provincially guaranteed) | Spread over GoC; quality varies by province |
| **Municipal** | Debentures, often **serial/instalment** (part matures each year) | Backed by taxing power; thinner secondary market |
| **Corporate** | Secured bonds (mortgage, collateral trust, equipment trust); debentures; subordinated debentures; commercial paper | Yield depends on rating and rank; terms set in a trust indenture |
| **GICs** | Fixed-term deposits of banks, trust companies and other deposit-takers: non-redeemable, cashable or market-linked | Issuer-set return; generally non-transferable (no secondary market, no price changes); non-redeemable GICs usually cannot be cashed early |

**Rank.** Secured bonds rank ahead of debentures, which rank ahead of subordinated debentures; the lower the rank, the higher the yield.

> **Exam trap:** A debenture relies only on the issuer's general credit, but that does not mean a weak issuer: GoC bonds are unsecured yet the safest in Canada.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.3 Terminology of fixed-income products in specific situations

- **Par (face) value:** the amount repaid at maturity. Prices are quoted per $100 of par: 98.50 on $10,000 par is $9,850.
- **Coupon rate:** annual interest on par, normally paid half-yearly in Canada.
- **Maturity date** vs **term to maturity:** the repayment date vs the shrinking time left until it.
- **Bond price:** the PV of remaining cash flows (2.9); a *premium* (above 100) when the coupon exceeds the market yield, a *discount* when below.
- **Yield to maturity (YTM):** the discount rate equating the PV of remaining payments to the price, assuming the bond is held to maturity and coupons are reinvested at the YTM. A purchase confirmation for a non-strip debt security must show the YTM, calculated according to market conventions.
- **Settlement** (cash for securities): for debt trades not cleared and settled through a clearing corporation, IDPC 4805 sets *regular delivery* the same day for GoC T-bills and the first business day after the trade for GoC, provincial, municipal and corporate bonds. Fixed-interest securities trade on an accrued-interest basis (IDPC 4803): the buyer pays the price **plus accrued interest** since the last coupon date. For example, $10,000 par of a 4% bond settling 73 days after the last coupon carries 10,000 × 0.04 × 73 ÷ 365 = $80 accrued (actual/365, for illustration).
- **Covenants:** trust-indenture promises. *Positive* ones require actions (e.g. maintain insurance); *negative* ones restrict them (e.g. limits on new debt or dividends).

*Sources: IDPC 3816(2)(vi)(b); IDPC 4802; IDPC 4803(1); IDPC 4805(1); otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.4 Characteristics of fixed-income products

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Investor** | Predictable income; principal repaid at maturity; ranks ahead of shareholders; diversification | No share in growth; inflation erodes payments; prices fall when rates rise |
| **Issuer** | Interest generally tax-deductible; no dilution of control | Fixed obligations; a missed payment is a default; covenants restrict flexibility |

**Risks:** interest-rate (worst for long, low-coupon bonds); reinvestment (short, high-coupon, callable bonds); credit/default; inflation; liquidity; call; currency. **Returns:** coupons, reinvestment income, and capital gains or losses (pull to par, or a sale after rates move).

**Acquisition costs.** Bonds trade over the counter, usually with the dealer as principal, so its compensation is a **mark-up** in the price that lowers the buyer's yield (a mark-down cuts the seller's proceeds). As principal, a dealer may trade OTC securities with a client only at an aggregate price, including any mark-up or mark-down, that is "fair and reasonable, taking into consideration all relevant factors" (IDPC 3122(2)–(3)); as agent, its commission must not exceed a fair and reasonable amount (IDPC 3122(4)). A retail client's secondary-market debt confirmation discloses the mark-up or mark-down, either as a total or through a prescribed notice that dealer remuneration was added to or deducted from the price (confirmation content: 8.6).

**Holding costs** include account fees and tax. A compound GIC pays interest only at maturity, yet interest is reported each complete investment year, so outside a registered plan tax can precede payment (likewise strips). For a T-bill that matures, the difference between price paid and proceeds is reported as interest.

> **Exam trap:** The same dollar mark-up cuts a short-term bond's yield far more than a long-term bond's: the cost is spread over fewer years.

*Sources: IDPC 3122(2)–(4); IDPC 3816(2)(vi)(c); CIRO Strip Bonds and Strip Bond Packages Information Statement; CRA Line 12100 (2025 tax year); otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.5 Characteristics of special-feature instruments

**Strips.** A strip is "sold at a discount to face value and matures at par". It pays no interest; the yield compounds semi-annually and is paid at maturity. Strips come from separating a bond into interest components ("coupons") and principal (the "residual"); a *strip bond package* combines two or more, e.g. for annuity-like income.

Per CIRO's information statement, longer strips are more price-volatile; strips typically yield more than conventional bonds of the same issuer, term and credit rating, reflecting that volatility; and dealers are not obligated to maintain a secondary market in strips, so liquidity may be limited and buyers should generally be prepared to hold to maturity. The CRA treats strips as prescribed debt obligations: notional interest is taxable yearly though none is paid, so strips may suit non-taxable accounts. Provincial regulation requires clients to receive the statement before trading strips of Government of Canada, provincial and certain foreign government bonds.

| Feature | Option held by | Yield vs plain bond | Benefits holder when | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Floating rate** | none | Resets to reference rate plus spread | rates rise | Price stays near par |
| **Callable** | issuer | Higher | never: issuer calls when rates fall | Upside capped near call price |
| **Puttable / retractable** | holder | Lower | rates rise (redeem at par, reinvest higher) | Elect within window |
| **Extendible** | holder | Lower | rates fall (keep the coupon longer) | Elect within window |
| **Convertible** | holder | Lower coupon | the share price rises | Worth at least its conversion value (shares received × share price) |

**Sinking vs purchase fund.** Both retire debt early, lowering default risk. A **sinking fund** *obliges* the issuer to retire a set amount yearly; a **purchase fund** buys *only if* bonds trade at or below a set price, supporting the price.

> **Exam trap:** A holder's option (retractable, extendible, convertible) *lowers* the yield; an issuer's call *raises* it. "Retractable" is Canadian usage for puttable.

*Sources: CIRO Strip Bonds and Strip Bond Packages Information Statement; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.6 Standard calculations of bond yields

Symbols: C = annual coupon ($); P = price; F = par; n = years to maturity.

```
Current (income) yield = C ÷ P
Approximate YTM        = [C + (F − P) ÷ n] ÷ [(F + P) ÷ 2]
```

**Convention:** this guide uses the (F + P) ÷ 2 denominator; some texts use (F + 2P) ÷ 3. Both are estimates.

> **Worked example**
> Leo's bond: $1,000 par, 5% semi-annual coupon ($50 a year), 10 years to maturity, price $920.
> ```
> Current yield = 50 ÷ 920 = 5.43%
> Approx. YTM  = [50 + (1,000 − 920) ÷ 10] ÷ [(1,000 + 920) ÷ 2] = 58 ÷ 960 = 6.04%
> ```
> True YTM (semi-annual compounding) ≈ 6.08%. Discount bond: coupon rate < current yield < YTM.

**Zero-coupon (strip) yield.** Canadian strip yields compound semi-annually.

```
Semi-annual:  y = 2 × [(F ÷ P)^(1 ÷ 2n) − 1]
Annual:       y = (F ÷ P)^(1 ÷ n) − 1
```

> **Worked example**
> A 10-year strip costs $61.03 per $100 of maturity value.
> ```
> Semi-annual: 2 × [(100 ÷ 61.03)^(1/20) − 1] = 5.00%
> Annual:      (100 ÷ 61.03)^(1/10) − 1 = 5.06%
> ```
> A strip purchase confirmation must show both yields; the annual one allows comparison with GICs.

**Yield curves** plot same-quality (usually GoC) yields against term. *Normal* (upward): stable or rising rate expectations plus a term premium. *Inverted*: expected rate cuts, often before a slowdown. *Flat/humped*: a transition. Theories: *expectations* (long rates reflect expected short rates), *liquidity preference* (a term premium tilts the curve up), *market segmentation* (each maturity range has its own supply and demand). Corporate and provincial yields are quoted as **spreads** over GoC.

*Sources: IDPC 3816(2)(vi)(a); otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.7 Price, yield, duration and economic relationships

**Price-volatility rules.** For a given yield change:

1. Price moves **inversely** to yield.
2. The **longer** the term, the larger the price change.
3. The **lower** the coupon, the larger the price change; strips are the most volatile for their term.
4. The **lower** the starting yield, the larger the price change.
5. A fall in yields raises the price more than an equal rise lowers it (convexity).

**Macaulay duration** is the PV-weighted average time, in years, to receive a bond's cash flows. A zero-coupon bond's duration equals its term; a coupon bond's is shorter. Duration generally rises with term and falls as the coupon or yield rises.

**Modified duration** is the approximate % price change for a 1-percentage-point yield change:

```
ModD = Macaulay duration ÷ (1 + y ÷ k)
y = YTM (decimal); k = coupon payments per year (2 for Canadian bonds)
```

**Economic factors.** Rising inflation (or expectations of it), a higher Bank of Canada policy rate, strong credit demand and heavy government borrowing push yields up and prices down. Recession, falling inflation and rate cuts usually do the reverse, but a weakening economy **widens credit spreads**, so weaker corporates can fall as GoC yields decline.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.8 Modified duration and price change

```
% change in price ≈ − ModD × Δy      (Δy in decimal, with its sign: +0.40% = +0.0040)
```

Ignoring convexity, it slightly overstates losses when yields rise and understates gains when they fall.

> **Worked example**
> Priya's bond is priced at $1,040 with a modified duration of 7.5. Yields rise 0.40%.
> ```
> % change ≈ −7.5 × 0.0040 = −3.0%  →  −0.030 × 1,040 = −$31.20  →  new price ≈ $1,008.80
> ```

> **Worked example**
> A semi-annual bond has a Macaulay duration of 8.2 years and a 5% YTM. Yields fall 0.25%. Convert to ModD first.
> ```
> ModD = 8.2 ÷ (1 + 0.05 ÷ 2) = 8.0;   % change ≈ −8.0 × (−0.0025) = +2.0%
> ```

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.9 Time value of money in investment calculations

**Bond price as present value.** C = coupon per period; r = yield per period; N = number of periods; F = par.

```
P = C × [1 − (1 + r)^(−N)] ÷ r  +  F ÷ (1 + r)^N
Semi-annual bond: C = annual coupon ÷ 2;  r = YTM ÷ 2;  N = years × 2
```

> **Worked example**
> A $1,000 par bond, 4% semi-annual coupon, 3 years to maturity, discounted at a 5% yield.
> ```
> C = 20, r = 0.025, N = 6
> PV of coupons = 20 × [1 − 1.025^(−6)] ÷ 0.025 = 20 × 5.5081 = $110.16
> PV of par     = 1,000 ÷ 1.025^6 = 1,000 ÷ 1.15969 = $862.30
> Price         = $972.46 (below par: the 4% coupon is below the 5% yield)
> ```

**Missing variable.** PV = present value; FV = future value; r = rate per period; n = number of periods.

```
FV = PV × (1 + r)^n          PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)^n
r  = (FV ÷ PV)^(1 ÷ n) − 1   n  = ln(FV ÷ PV) ÷ ln(1 + r)
PMT (level payment) = PV × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)^(−n)]
```

> **Worked example**
> 1. *Rate:* $10,000 grows to $14,000 in 6 years. r = 1.4^(1/6) − 1 = 5.77% a year.
> 2. *Time:* doubling at 6% takes n = ln 2 ÷ ln 1.06 = 0.6931 ÷ 0.0583 = 11.9 years.

Annuity applications are in 7.8. **A bond's YTM** has no closed form: start from approximate YTM (2.6) and test it in the price formula, raising the rate if the price comes out too high, lowering it if too low. On a coupon date, discounting at the coupon rate gives par.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*
