# Element 2: Fixed income

This element covers:

- debt securities and their features;
- the risks and costs of debt securities;
- the IDPC rules for debt markets;
- the arithmetic of bond prices and yields.

Element 2 has about **10 of the 120 questions**. Some questions need a short calculation on the on-screen basic/scientific calculator. In this element, "dealer" means an Investment Dealer, which the IDPC calls a "Dealer Member".

## 2.1 Regulatory requirements for debt markets

Most bonds trade over the counter, with no exchange price. So clients depend on the dealer for a fair price and fair treatment.

### General requirements: fair and efficient debt markets

Rule 7100 sets trading and settlement practices "to promote fair and efficient debt securities markets". Unless a provision says otherwise, it applies to institutional and retail markets alike. The general requirements are:

- **Obey all applicable rules.** The dealer's debt trading must not breach any applicable law, regulation, direction or requirement, binding or not. This includes the directions of the Bank of Canada and the Department of Finance (Canada).
- **Do not help others to break the rule.** The dealer must not "condone or knowingly facilitate conduct by its affiliates, clients, or counterparties that contravenes Rule 7100" (7102(2)).

### Policies and procedures: the requirement

A dealer's policies and procedures must specifically address debt-market trading and conduct. They must give reasonable assurance of compliance with securities laws and CIRO requirements. An Executive of the relevant business group must approve them (7103(3)). The dealer must review them regularly. They must fit the size, nature and complexity of its business (7103(4)).

### Policies and procedures: the content

| Required content | Rule |
|---|---|
| Controls on trading in non-client accounts; no use of inside information; no front-running | 7103(2)(i)–(iii) |
| Fair allocation of new issues among clients; prompt, accurate disclosure of conflicts to clients and counterparties | 7103(2)(iv)–(v) |
| Retail: written mark-up, mark-down and commission guidelines for Registered Representatives (RRs), plus monitoring to detect charges above the dealer's maximums and to justify any deviation | 7103(2)(vi) |
| Confidential information only for staff who need it; trading by designated personnel only in restricted-access office areas; secure communications and technology | 7105(2) |

The procedures support other duties in the rule:

- **Personnel (7104).** The dealer must make sure that all its debt traders are properly qualified and trained. The dealer must also make sure that they know CIRO requirements and the laws for debt trading (7104(1)(ii)). A Supervisor must supervise the trading.
- **Conflicts (7107).** The dealer must put its duties to clients before its own interests or those of its personnel (see 1.23).
- **Surveillance (7113).** The dealer must monitor the debt trading and conduct of its employees and agents. It must promptly report breaches of CIRO requirements, and suspicious or irregular market conduct. It reports to CIRO or another authority with jurisdiction, including the Bank of Canada.

### Prohibited practices: obligations on the Investment Dealer

**Manipulate or deceive (7109).** The dealer must not, directly or indirectly, take part in a practice that it knows, or ought reasonably to know, is manipulative or deceptive. Examples are:

- trades intended to artificially increase trading volumes;
- trades intended to artificially change trading prices;
- rumours about issuers that are known, or ought reasonably to be known, to be false or misleading. The dealer must not spread them or tacitly consent to them.
- collusion with another market participant to manipulate or unfairly deal in the debt markets.

**Take unfair advantage of clients or counterparties (7110).** Examples are:

- a proprietary trade ahead of a same-side client order. The dealer may do this only if it first discloses its intention to the client and gets the client's approval.
- the use of material non-public information;
- a trade at a price clearly outside the prevailing market, which comes from a manifest error.

Compare front running under UMIR 4.1 (see 8.1). The prohibitions in 7109 and 7110 also apply to trading in derivatives of debt securities.

**Other prohibited practices (7112).** The dealer must not:

- accept an order or carry out a trade that it knows, or has reasonable grounds to believe, would breach CIRO requirements or the law;
- offer consideration to a partner, director, officer, employee, agent or shareholder of a client, or their associates, without the client's prior written consent.

### Prohibited practices: obligation on the Approved Person

> **Key rule:** "An Approved Person or employee of a Dealer Member must not accept any material consideration, including remuneration, gratuity or benefit, from any person other than the Dealer Member for any activities conducted on behalf of a client." (IDPC 7112(2))

An item is not consideration under 7112(2) (accept) and 7112(3) (offer) only if all of these conditions apply (IDPC 7112(4)):

- It is non-monetary.
- It has minimal value.
- It is infrequent.
- It will not cause a reasonable person to question whether it created a conflict of interest.

Compare the general ban in IDPC 3115 on the acceptance of consideration (see 1.25).

**After a breach.** The guidance note warns that sanctions can come from:

- the regulator (now CIRO);
- the Government of Canada;
- the Department of Finance;
- provincial securities regulators.

For Government of Canada securities, the Bank of Canada can suspend or remove a dealer's right to bid at auctions.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Fabrizio is an Approved Person. A bond issuer that wants his clients' trades offers him a paid ski holiday. Separately, a counterparty gives him a coffee mug with its logo. This is the first gift that he gets from that counterparty.
>
> **Question.** Can Fabrizio accept either item?
>
> **Resolution.** The holiday is material consideration from a person other than his dealer, for activities on behalf of a client. IDPC 7112(2) prohibits it. The mug is the first gift from that counterparty. It meets all four 7112(4) conditions in the list above. So under 7112(4), it is not consideration.

## 2.2 Types and features of fixed-income products

A fixed-income product is a loan from the client to an issuer. The issuer promises interest and the return of principal on set dates. The type of issuer sets:

- the credit quality;
- the liquidity;
- the yield.

### Government of Canada securities

The federal government has the widest taxing power in Canada. So Government of Canada (GoC) debt is the highest quality and the most liquid in Canada. The main products are:

- **T-bills:** short-term debt, usually one year or less. A T-bill pays no coupon. The client buys it at a discount and receives par at maturity.
- **Marketable bonds:** fixed coupons, with benchmark terms of about 2 to 30 years. Their yields set the benchmark yield curve (see 2.6).
- **Real Return Bonds:** the principal adjusts for inflation. Existing issues still trade.

### Provincial and municipal government securities

Provinces issue bonds and T-bills. Provincial agencies also issue debt, which the province often guarantees. Provincial yields are a spread over GoC yields. Credit quality varies by province.

Municipalities often issue **serial (instalment)** debentures, so a part of the issue matures each year. The municipal taxing power backs the debt. The secondary market for municipal debt is usually thinner than for provincial debt.

### Corporate securities

A corporation sets the terms of its bonds in a **trust indenture**. A trustee acts for the bondholders. The rank of the debt decides who receives payment first after a default:

1. **Secured bonds:** mortgage bonds (real property), collateral trust bonds (pledged securities) and equipment trust certificates (equipment).
2. **Debentures:** no specific security, only the general credit of the issuer.
3. **Subordinated debentures:** they rank after the other debt of the issuer.

The lower the rank, the higher the yield. The yield also depends on the credit rating. **Commercial paper** is short-term, unsecured corporate debt, usually for less than one year. Convertible debentures are in 2.5.

> **Exam trap:** A debenture relies only on the issuer's general credit. That does not mean a weak issuer: GoC bonds are unsecured yet the safest in Canada.

### Guaranteed investment certificates (GIC)

A GIC is a fixed-term deposit with a bank, trust company or other deposit-taker. The issuer sets the return. The types are:

- **Non-redeemable:** the client usually cannot cash it before maturity.
- **Cashable:** the client can cash it early, usually at a lower rate.
- **Market-linked:** the return depends on a market index.

A GIC is generally non-transferable. It has no secondary market, so its value does not change with interest rates. The tax on compound GIC interest is in 2.4.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Henrietta needs $40,000 for a home in about three years, but she may need it earlier. She compares a non-redeemable GIC and a GoC bond.
>
> **Question.** What is the main difference for her?
>
> **Resolution.** The GIC has a fixed return and no price change, but she usually cannot cash it early. She can sell the GoC bond at any time in a liquid market. But if rates rise, its price can be below her cost.

## 2.3 Terminology of fixed-income products in specific situations

### Par value

**Par (face) value** is the amount that the issuer repays at maturity. It is also the base for the coupon. Dealers quote prices per $100 of par. A price of 98.50 on $10,000 par is $9,850.

### Coupon rate

The **coupon rate** is the annual interest as a percentage of par. Canadian bonds normally pay it semi-annually. A 4% coupon on $10,000 par pays $400 a year, as two payments of $200. For a fixed-coupon bond, the coupon stays the same when market yields change.

### Maturity date and term to maturity

The **maturity date** is the date of repayment. The **term to maturity** is the time left until that date. It gets shorter every day. Term is a main driver of price volatility (see 2.7).

### Bond price

The **bond price** is the present value of the remaining cash flows (see 2.9). The price moves so that a buyer earns the current market yield:

- If the coupon rate is above the market yield, the bond trades at a **premium** (above 100).
- If the coupon rate is below the market yield, the bond trades at a **discount** (below 100).
- If the coupon rate equals the market yield, the bond trades at par.

### Yield to maturity

**Yield to maturity (YTM)** is the discount rate that makes the PV of the remaining payments equal to the price. It assumes that the client holds the bond to maturity and reinvests each coupon at the YTM. A purchase confirmation for a non-strip debt security must show the YTM, calculated in line with market conventions (see 8.6).

### Settlement

**Settlement** is the exchange of cash for securities. IDPC 4805 sets *regular delivery* for debt trades not cleared and settled through a clearing corporation:

- GoC T-bills: the same day as the trade.
- GoC, provincial, municipal and corporate bonds: the first business day after the trade. Accrued interest stops on that day.

All trades are for regular delivery, unless all parties agree otherwise in writing at the time of the trade (IDPC 4805(2)). The cycle for trades through a clearing corporation is in 8.4.

For these non-clearing-corporation trades, IDPC 4803 requires an accrued-interest basis for fixed-interest securities. This does not apply to sale and repurchase agreement transactions. The buyer pays the price **plus accrued interest** since the last coupon date. The buyer receives the full next coupon. So the buyer pays the seller the part of that coupon that the seller earned.

Accrued-interest trading stops at maturity, or at a default, or when the debtor announces a default, whichever is first (IDPC 4803(1)). After a default or an announcement, these rules apply (IDPC 4803(3)):

- The bond trades "flat", with no accrued interest.
- All matured and unpaid coupons stay attached to the bond.
- Flat trading continues until the issuer pays all arrears of interest and pays one current coupon when due.

### Covenants

**Covenants** are promises of the issuer in the trust indenture. They protect the bondholders. *Positive* covenants require actions, for example to maintain insurance. *Negative* covenants restrict actions, for example limits on new debt or on dividends.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ines buys $20,000 par of a provincial bond with a 5% coupon at 101.25. The trade is not settled through a clearing corporation.
>
> **Question.** What does she pay, and when does the trade settle?
>
> **Resolution.** The price is $20,250, a premium, so the 5% coupon is above the market yield. She also pays accrued interest since the last coupon date. Unless the parties agreed otherwise in writing, regular delivery is the first business day after the trade (IDPC 4805(1)(ii), 4805(2)).

*Sources: IDPC 3816(2)(vi)(b); IDPC 4802; IDPC 4803(1), (3); IDPC 4805(1), (2); otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.4 Characteristics of fixed-income products

### Advantages and disadvantages to the investor and to the issuer

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Investor** | Predictable income; principal repaid at maturity; ranks ahead of shareholders; diversification | No share in growth; inflation erodes payments; prices fall when rates rise |
| **Issuer** | Interest generally tax-deductible; no dilution of control | Fixed obligations; a missed payment is a default; covenants restrict flexibility |

Compare the equity view in 3.5.

### Sources of risks and potential returns

The main risks are:

- **Interest-rate risk:** if rates rise, prices fall. It is highest for long, low-coupon bonds (see 2.7).
- **Reinvestment risk:** the client may have to reinvest cash flows at lower rates. It is highest for short, high-coupon and callable bonds.
- **Credit (default) risk:** the issuer may miss a payment, or its rating may fall.
- **Inflation risk:** fixed payments buy less over time.
- **Liquidity risk:** the client may not be able to sell quickly at a fair price.
- **Call risk:** if rates fall, the issuer may repay early (see 2.5).
- **Currency risk:** if the Canadian dollar rises, a foreign-currency bond loses value in Canadian dollars.

Interest-rate risk and reinvestment risk work in opposite directions. The general risk types are in 6.2.

**Returns** come from:

- coupons;
- the reinvestment of coupons;
- capital gains or losses.

A capital gain or loss comes from the pull to par, or from a sale after rates move.

### Impact of the costs of acquiring fixed-income products

Bonds trade over the counter. The dealer usually acts as principal, so its compensation is a **mark-up** in the price. A mark-up lowers the buyer's yield. A mark-down decreases the seller's proceeds. The rules control this cost:

- **As principal,** the dealer must trade OTC securities with a client at a fair and reasonable aggregate price (IDPC 3122(2)–(3)). This price includes any mark-up or mark-down. It takes into consideration "all relevant factors". Examples of these factors are:
  - the fair market value of the securities;
  - the expense of the trade;
  - the dealer's right to a profit.
- **As agent,** the dealer's commission must not exceed a fair and reasonable amount (IDPC 3122(4)).

**On the confirmation (IDPC 3816(2)(vi)(c)).** This rule applies if a debt trade is not a primary market transaction and the confirmation goes to a retail client. The confirmation must show one of these (see 8.6):

- the total of any mark-up or mark-down, commission or other service charges; or
- the total commission. If the dealer also applied a mark-up, mark-down or other non-commission charge, the confirmation adds a prescribed notice, or a substantially similar notice. The notice says that dealer remuneration was added to or deducted from the price.

### Impact of the costs of holding fixed-income products

**Holding costs** include account fees and tax. Tax can come before the cash:

- A compound GIC pays interest only when the client cashes it or when it matures. But the client must report the interest for each complete investment year. So outside a registered plan, tax can come before payment. Strips have the same tax result (see 2.5).
- For a T-bill that matures, the client reports the difference between the price paid and the proceeds as interest.
- If the client sells a T-bill before maturity, there can also be a capital gain or loss, in addition to the interest.

Registered plans change this result (see 7.9). RRSP income is usually exempt from tax while the funds remain in the plan. But the client generally pays tax on payments from the plan. TFSA interest is generally tax-free, even on withdrawal.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Anselm compares a one-year strip and a 25-year strip. The dealer adds the same commission per $100 of maturity amount to each.
>
> **Question.** Which purchase does the commission hurt more?
>
> **Resolution.** The one-year strip. Its cost falls on one year of return, not 25. The strip information statement shows this with a 5.5% yield before commission. A $0.25 commission cuts the one-year strip's yield to 5.229%. The same commission cuts the 25-year strip's yield only to 5.460%.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Kwabena is 70 and needs steady income. He wants all of his savings in one 25-year corporate debenture with a high coupon, in a non-registered account.
>
> **Question.** Analyze the risks.
>
> **Resolution.** The income is steady, but these risks are high:
> - The long term gives high interest-rate risk. Higher rates can decrease the price significantly before maturity.
> - A high coupon usually signals higher credit risk (a lower rating).
> - One issuer gives concentrated credit risk. A debenture has no specific security.
> - If he needs cash, all his savings in one OTC bond give high liquidity risk.
> - Inflation over 25 years decreases the value of the fixed payments.
>
> Shorter bonds from several issuers give more stable value and less credit concentration. But shorter bonds bring more reinvestment risk. So a ladder of maturities balances interest-rate risk and reinvestment risk. Some reinvestment risk remains, because a part matures each year. The suitability analysis is in 1.17.

## 2.5 Characteristics of special-feature instruments

Most special features give an option to the issuer or to the holder. An option has value. So the party that holds the option pays for it through the yield. A floating rate and sinking or purchase funds are not options of this type: they change the coupon or retire debt.

### Strips

A strip is "sold at a discount to face value and matures at par". It pays no interest. The yield compounds semi-annually and is paid at maturity. Strips come from the separation of a bond into interest components ("coupons") and principal (the "residual"). A *strip bond package* combines two or more components, for example for annuity-like income.

The return of a strip is fixed at purchase, so it can suit a need for a fixed amount on a future date. CIRO's information statement adds:

- A longer strip generally has larger price changes than a shorter strip of the same issuer and yield.
- Strips typically yield more than conventional bonds of the same issuer, term and credit rating. This reflects their greater volatility.
- Dealers are not obligated to maintain a secondary market in strips. Liquidity may be limited for certain strips. So clients should generally be prepared to hold a strip to maturity.

**Tax.** The CRA treats strips as prescribed debt obligations. The client must include notional interest in income each year. The issuer pays no interest until maturity. So strips may suit non-taxable accounts, such as self-directed registered plans. Strips issued or guaranteed by the GoC, or issued by a province or territory, are qualified investments for registered plans.

**Disclosure.** Provincial regulation requires clients to receive the statement before they trade strips of GoC, provincial and certain foreign government bonds. The purchase confirmation shows two yields (see 2.6).

### Floating rate

A floating-rate bond resets its coupon to a reference rate plus a spread. So its price stays near par, but the income is uncertain. The reset protects the price from rate changes, but not from credit changes. If the issuer's credit weakens, the fixed spread is too low. Then the price falls below par. A purchase confirmation for a debt security with a variable coupon must include the notation "The coupon rate may vary."

### Callable/puttable

A **callable** bond lets the issuer repay early at a set call price. When rates fall, the issuer calls the bond. Then the holder must reinvest at lower rates. The call also caps the price near the call price. A purchase confirmation for a callable debt security must include the notation "callable".

A **puttable** (retractable) bond lets the holder sell it back to the issuer at par on set dates. When rates rise, the holder uses this option.

### Convertible

A convertible bond or debenture lets the holder exchange it for a set number of common shares of the issuer (the conversion ratio). It keeps the income and rank of debt. If the share price is high, its price follows the shares. If the share price is low, it trades like a bond. Its bond value then acts as a floor.

A convertible also helps the issuer. The issuer gets a lower coupon and possible equity without immediate dilution. If a convertible is callable, the issuer can call it to force conversion.

### Extendable

An extendable bond lets the holder extend the maturity. When rates fall, the holder extends the bond to keep the higher coupon longer. If the holder does not elect within the window, the bond matures on the original date. So when rates rise, it behaves like the short bond. When rates fall, it behaves like the long bond.

### Sinking/purchase funds

Both funds retire debt early, which lowers default risk.

- A **sinking fund** *requires* the issuer to retire a set amount each year. It calls bonds by lot or buys them in the market.
- If the bonds trade at or below a set price, a **purchase fund** buys them. This supports the price.

| Feature | Option held by | Yield vs plain bond | Benefits holder when | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Floating rate** | none | Resets to reference rate plus spread | rates rise | Price stays near par |
| **Callable** | issuer | Higher | never: issuer calls when rates fall | Upside capped near call price |
| **Puttable / retractable** | holder | Lower | rates rise (redeem at par, reinvest higher) | Elect within window |
| **Extendable** | holder | Lower | rates fall (keep the coupon longer) | Elect within window |
| **Convertible** | holder | Lower coupon | the share price rises | Worth at least its conversion value (shares received × share price) |

> **Exam trap:** A holder's option (retractable, extendable, convertible) *lowers* the yield; an issuer's call *raises* it. "Retractable" is Canadian usage for puttable.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Esperanza wants $50,000 in exactly 12 years, when she retires. She has a non-registered account and a self-directed RRSP with funds in it. She looks at a 12-year GoC strip with a $50,000 maturity value.
>
> **Question.** Can a strip fit her need, and in which account?
>
> **Resolution.** A 12-year strip pays a fixed amount on a fixed date, so it fits. A GoC strip is a qualified investment for the RRSP. In the non-registered account, she pays tax each year on notional interest without cash. The RRSP removes this annual tax. But an RRSP withdrawal is taxable income, so her after-tax amount is less than $50,000 (see 7.9).

## 2.6 Standard calculations of bond yields

A yield converts a price into a rate of return, so you can compare bonds.

### Income (or current) yield

Symbols in 2.6: C = annual coupon ($); P = price; F = par; n = years to maturity.

```
Current (income) yield = C ÷ P
```

The current yield shows the cash income per dollar invested today. It ignores the gain or loss from now to maturity. So it gives an incorrect return for a bond far from par.

### (Approximate) yield to maturity

```
Approximate YTM = [C + (F − P) ÷ n] ÷ [(F + P) ÷ 2]
```

The numerator is the average annual return: the coupon plus the yearly share of the pull to par. The denominator is the average amount invested over the life of the bond. **Convention:** this guide uses the (F + P) ÷ 2 denominator; some texts use (F + 2P) ÷ 3. Both are estimates. The true YTM needs trial and error (see 2.9).

> **Worked example**
> Halvard's bond: $1,000 par, 5% semi-annual coupon ($50 a year), 10 years to maturity, price $920.
> ```
> Current yield = 50 ÷ 920 = 5.43%
> Approx. YTM  = [50 + (1,000 − 920) ÷ 10] ÷ [(1,000 + 920) ÷ 2] = 58 ÷ 960 = 6.04%
> ```
> True YTM (semi-annual compounding) ≈ 6.08%. Discount bond: coupon rate < current yield < YTM.

> **Worked example**
> Mireille's bond: $1,000 par, 6% coupon ($60 a year), 5 years to maturity, price $1,045.
> ```
> Current yield = 60 ÷ 1,045 = 5.74%
> Approx. YTM  = [60 + (1,000 − 1,045) ÷ 5] ÷ [(1,000 + 1,045) ÷ 2] = 51 ÷ 1,022.5 = 4.99%
> ```
> True YTM (semi-annual compounding) ≈ 4.97%. Premium bond: coupon rate > current yield > YTM, because the client loses the $45 premium by maturity.

### Yield on a zero-coupon instrument

A zero-coupon (strip) instrument has only one cash flow, so its yield has an exact formula. Canadian strip yields compound semi-annually.

```
Semi-annual:  y = 2 × [(F ÷ P)^(1 ÷ 2n) − 1]
Annual:       y = (F ÷ P)^(1 ÷ n) − 1
```

> **Worked example**
> A 10-year strip costs $61.03 per $100 of maturity value.
> ```
> Semi-annual: 2 × [(100 ÷ 61.03)^(1/20) − 1] = 5.00%
> Annual:      (100 ÷ 61.03)^(1/10) − 1 = 5.06%
> ```
> A strip purchase confirmation must show both yields; the annual one allows comparison with GICs.

The annual yield is higher because it counts the interest earned on interest within each year.

### Yield curves

A yield curve plots the yields of same-quality bonds, usually GoC bonds, against term. Its shape shows what the market expects:

- **Normal (upward):** the market expects stable or higher rates, plus a term premium.
- **Inverted:** expected rate cuts, often before a slowdown.
- **Flat or humped:** a transition between the two.

Three theories explain the shape:

1. **Expectations:** long rates reflect the expected future short rates.
2. **Liquidity preference:** investors want a term premium for longer bonds, which tilts the curve up.
3. **Market segmentation:** each range of maturities has its own supply and demand.

Dealers quote corporate and provincial yields as **spreads** over GoC. A wider spread shows more credit or liquidity risk.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Gaspard sees that 2-year GoC bonds yield more than 10-year GoC bonds.
>
> **Question.** What does the curve show?
>
> **Resolution.** The curve is inverted. Under the expectations theory, the market expects short rates to fall. This often comes before an economic slowdown. It is not a certainty, because a term premium and supply also affect the shape.

*Sources: IDPC 3816(2)(vi)(a); otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 2.7 Price, yield, duration and economic relationships

A bond price is the present value of its cash flows. A higher discount rate lowers the price. The value of distant cash flows changes the most.

### Coupon, yield, term-to-maturity and price volatility

**Price-volatility rules.** For a given yield change:

1. Price moves **inversely** to yield.
2. The **longer** the term, the larger the price change.
3. The **lower** the coupon, the larger the price change; strips are the most volatile for their term.
4. The **lower** the initial yield, the larger the price change.
5. A fall in yields raises the price more than an equal rise lowers it (convexity).

Rule 2 applies because a long bond pays its fixed coupon for longer. Rule 3 applies because a low-coupon bond receives more of its value at maturity. The strip statement shows both rules when rates rise from 6% to 7%:

- Rule 2: a 5-year 6% bond falls 4.16%, but a 20-year 6% bond falls 10.68%.
- Rule 3: a 20-year strip falls 17.61%, against 10.68% for the 20-year 6% bond. A 5-year strip falls 4.73%.

### Macaulay duration, modified duration and price sensitivity

**Macaulay duration** is the PV-weighted average time, in years, to receive a bond's cash flows. A zero-coupon bond's duration equals its term; a coupon bond's is shorter. Duration generally rises with term. It falls as the coupon or the yield rises. So duration combines rules 2 to 4 into one number. This guide treats Macaulay duration as a concept only.

**Modified duration** is the approximate % price change for a 1-percentage-point yield change:

```
ModD = Macaulay duration ÷ (1 + y ÷ k)
y = YTM (decimal); k = coupon payments per year (2 for Canadian bonds)
```

A higher duration means higher price sensitivity. The calculation is in 2.8. Duration management and immunization of bond portfolios are in 6.12.

### Changes in economic factors and price of fixed-income products

These factors push yields **up** and prices **down**:

- higher inflation, or higher expected inflation;
- a higher Bank of Canada policy rate;
- strong demand for credit;
- heavy government borrowing.

These factors usually do the reverse:

- recession;
- lower inflation;
- rate cuts.

But a weaker economy **widens credit spreads**. So when GoC yields decline, weaker corporate bonds can fall in price. The link between the economy and rates is in 4.13.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Wren expects interest rates to fall soon. She wants the largest price gain from a bond position.
>
> **Question.** Which bond fits her view best: a 3-year 6% bond, a 20-year 6% bond, or a 20-year strip?
>
> **Resolution.** The 20-year strip. It has the longest duration, because all its value comes at maturity. Long term and no coupon both increase the price change. If her view is wrong and rates rise, the strip also has the largest loss.

## 2.8 Modified duration and price change

Modified duration converts a yield change into an estimated price change. It lets an RR show a client the price risk of a bond with one number.

```
% change in price ≈ − ModD × Δy      (Δy in decimal, with its sign: +0.40% = +0.0040)
```

The minus sign shows the inverse relationship: a rise in yield gives a fall in price. To get the dollar change, multiply the % change by the current price. If you have only the Macaulay duration, convert it first with the formula in 2.7.

**Why it works.** Modified duration measures the slope of the price–yield curve at the current yield. For a small yield change, the curve is almost a straight line. So the slope gives a good first estimate of the price change.

**Convexity.** The real curve bends upward, so the formula ignores a part of the change. When yields rise, it slightly overstates losses. When yields fall, it understates gains. The error is small for small yield changes. It grows for large changes and for long bonds.

> **Worked example**
> Petronella's bond has a price of $1,040 and a modified duration of 7.5. Yields rise 0.40%.
> ```
> % change ≈ −7.5 × 0.0040 = −3.0%  →  −0.030 × 1,040 = −$31.20  →  new price ≈ $1,008.80
> ```

> **Worked example**
> A semi-annual bond has a Macaulay duration of 8.2 years, a 5% YTM and a price of $950. Yields fall 0.25%. Convert to ModD first.
> ```
> ModD = 8.2 ÷ (1 + 0.05 ÷ 2) = 8.0;   % change ≈ −8.0 × (−0.0025) = +2.0%
> $ change ≈ +0.020 × 950 = +$19.00   →  new price ≈ $969.00
> ```

> **Exam trap:** Enter Δy as a decimal: 25 basis points = 0.25% = 0.0025. If yields fall, Δy is negative, so the price change is positive. Do not use 0.25 for a 0.25% change.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ottoline is a conservative client. She can accept a price fall of about 3% at most. She compares Bond A (ModD 3.2) and Bond B (ModD 11.5). She thinks yields can rise 0.75%.
>
> **Question.** Which bond fits her limit?
>
> **Resolution.** Estimate each price change with −ModD × Δy:
> ```
> Bond A: −3.2 × 0.0075 = −2.4%
> Bond B: −11.5 × 0.0075 = −8.6% (−8.625%)
> ```
> Only Bond A stays inside her limit. Bond B's actual fall is slightly smaller, because of convexity, but it is still far above 3%.

## 2.9 Time value of money in investment calculations

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar later, because it can earn interest. Time value of money (TVM) compares cash flows at different dates.

**Symbols in 2.9.** All symbols are per period: c = coupon per period; r = rate per period; N = number of periods. In 2.6, C is the annual coupon and n is in years.

### Present value of a fixed-income security

**Bond price as present value.** F = par.

```
P = c × [1 − (1 + r)^(−N)] ÷ r  +  F ÷ (1 + r)^N
Semi-annual bond: c = annual coupon ÷ 2;  r = YTM ÷ 2;  N = years × 2
```

The first part is the PV of the coupons, which form an annuity. The second part is the PV of par. Use the discount rate that the market requires today, not the coupon rate.

> **Worked example**
> A $1,000 par bond, 4% semi-annual coupon, 3 years to maturity, discounted at a 5% yield.
> ```
> c = 20, r = 0.025, N = 6
> PV of coupons = 20 × [1 − 1.025^(−6)] ÷ 0.025 = 20 × 5.5081 = $110.16
> PV of par     = 1,000 ÷ 1.025^6 = 1,000 ÷ 1.15969 = $862.30
> Price         = $972.46 (below par: the 4% coupon is below the 5% yield)
> ```

On a coupon date, a discount rate equal to the coupon rate gives a price of par. Use this to check your work.

### Any missing variable in a time value of money calculation

Symbols: PV = present value; FV = future value; r and N as above.

```
FV = PV × (1 + r)^N          PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)^N
r  = (FV ÷ PV)^(1 ÷ N) − 1   N  = ln(FV ÷ PV) ÷ ln(1 + r)
PMT (level payment) = PV × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)^(−N)]
```

Match r and N to the payment period. For semi-annual compounding, halve the annual rate and double the years.

> **Worked example**
> 1. *Rate:* $10,000 grows to $14,000 in 6 years. r = 1.4^(1/6) − 1 = 5.77% a year.
> 2. *Time:* at 6%, money doubles in N = ln 2 ÷ ln 1.06 = 0.6931 ÷ 0.0583 = 11.9 years.
> 3. *Present value:* a strip pays $10,000 in 8 years. The yield is 4%, compounded semi-annually. PV = 10,000 ÷ 1.02^16 = $7,284.46.

Annuity applications are in 7.8. **A bond's YTM** has no closed form. Start from the approximate YTM (2.6) and test it in the price formula. If the calculated price is too high, raise the rate. If it is too low, lower the rate.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Corentin bought the 4% bond in the first worked example at par. Market yields then rose to 5%, with 3 years left.
>
> **Question.** Why can he sell it for only about $972?
>
> **Resolution.** A buyer now wants 5%, but the bond pays only 4% on par. So the buyer pays less than par. The pull to par gives the buyer the difference. At a 5% yield, the price formula gives $972.46. If Corentin holds to maturity, he still receives $1,000.
