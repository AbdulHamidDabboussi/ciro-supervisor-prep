# Element 5: Managed products and other investments

This element covers managed products: a professional manager invests money that many investors give to one pool. The element includes mutual funds, ETFs, closed-end funds, income trusts, REITs, wraps and pooled funds. It also includes alternatives such as hedge funds, structured products, private equity and crypto-asset funds. The element has about **16 of the 120 questions**.

Expect comparisons (trust or corporation, ETF or mutual fund, which document and when) and short scenarios on costs, redemptions and suitability. The main calculations are in 5.9: NAVPS, units issued, the value of a holding and simple total return. 5.8 shows a simple holding period return. Other numbers in this element are one-line illustrations only.

**Terms.** This element uses each term in one meaning only:

- *investor*: a person who buys or holds a managed product;
- *client*: an investor who deals with a Registered Representative (RR);
- *dealer*: the Investment Dealer that sells the product (the IDPC's "Dealer Member");
- *units*: fund securities in general (*shares* only when the corporate form matters);
- *NAVPS*: net asset value (NAV) per unit;
- *trailing commission*: the ongoing commission paid out of the management fee.

## 5.1 Types of managed products

### Why investors pool their money

Each investor owns a part of the pool, in proportion to the units held. The CRA says that "your money is pooled with the money of other investors and invested on your behalf by the fund manager". A pool gives small investors diversification and professional management.

### The legal test: can the investor redeem on demand?

- A **mutual fund** is an issuer "whose primary purpose is to invest money provided by its security holders and whose securities entitle the holder to receive on demand" an amount based on its net assets (Ontario *Securities Act*).
- A **non-redeemable investment fund** (a closed-end fund) also has the primary purpose "to invest money provided by its securityholders", but it "is not a mutual fund".
- NI 81-106 uses the term **investment fund** for both: "a mutual fund or a non-redeemable investment fund".

By definition, a non-redeemable investment fund does not invest to control another issuer or to take part actively in its management. The exception is an investment in another mutual fund or non-redeemable investment fund.

The redemption right has practical effects. A mutual fund must keep its assets liquid (see 5.14) and pay redemptions fast (see 5.11). A non-redeemable investment fund may hold more illiquid assets.

### The product types

**Mutual fund trust.** Investors buy units of a trust, and a trustee holds the assets for them (see 5.3). Income flows through to the investors on T3 slips.

**Mutual fund corporation.** Investors buy shares, often in one of several classes of one corporation. The corporation pays dividends and capital gains dividends, and it issues T5 slips.

**Income trust.** An income trust is a listed trust that holds an operating business and pays out most of its cash flow. The payout is not guaranteed.

**Closed-end fund.** The fund issues units mainly at its initial public offering (IPO), with no continuous offering. Investors usually exit by a sale on an exchange, at a **discount** (or premium) to NAV. Some closed-end funds also let investors redeem on dates set in the prospectus. The redemption price "must not be a price that is more than the net asset value". Many such funds pay NAV only once a year and a lower price at other times.

**REIT.** A real estate investment trust is a listed trust that owns income-producing real estate.

**ETF.** In law, an ETF is "a mutual fund in continuous distribution" whose units are listed and trade on an exchange (NI 41-101). An "index participation unit" is the narrow legal term for a unit of a fund that holds an index's securities "in substantially the same proportion".

**Wrap, fund of funds, ETF wrap.** One account or one fund holds several funds or ETFs at a target mix, for one fee. NI 81-102 s. 2.5 sets conditions for funds that hold other funds. For example, the top fund must not pay a management fee or incentive fee that, "to a reasonable person, would duplicate a fee payable by the other investment fund for the same service". An **asset allocation service** allocates an investment "among mutual funds".

**Pooled fund.** A pooled fund has no public prospectus. Dealers sell it under exemptions, for example to accredited investors (see 5.14), or managers use it inside discretionary programs.

> **Exam trap:** Closed-end funds and ETFs both trade on exchanges. Only an ETF has a creation and redemption process (see 5.5) that keeps its price close to NAV. A closed-end fund can trade at a persistent discount.

## 5.2 Considerations for investors in managed products

### Range of exposures

One purchase can give almost any exposure:

- **Income and growth.** Income funds (money market, bond, dividend) aim at regular cash flow. Growth funds (equity, small-cap) aim at capital gains, with more volatility.
- **Asset classes.** Funds can hold cash, bonds, equities, real estate or commodities, some of which are hard for a small investor to buy directly.
- **Sectors.** A sector fund holds one industry, such as energy, with that industry's return potential and risk.
- **Geography.** Funds can be Canadian, U.S., international, emerging-market or global. Foreign funds add currency risk (see 5.4) and can suffer withholding tax (see 5.10).
- **Ethical and ESG considerations.** Mandates can screen out industries, integrate ESG factors into analysis, or target a measurable impact. Labels vary, so read the fund's objectives and holdings.
- **Diversification and concentration.** Broad funds spread risk over many issuers. Sector, thematic and single-country funds add concentration.

### The single-issuer limit

Securities law supports diversification in a conventional mutual fund. Such a fund must not buy a security if more than **10% of its NAV** would then be "invested in securities of any one issuer". The limit is 20% for an alternative mutual fund or a non-redeemable investment fund. The limit does not apply to the purchase of these securities:

- a government security;
- a security issued by a clearing corporation;
- a security of another investment fund, if the purchase meets s. 2.5;
- an index participation unit (IPU) of an investment fund;
- an equity security that a fixed portfolio investment fund buys under its investment objectives.

The IPU itself is exempt, but the fund looks through it. For the limit, the fund holds "its proportionate share of the securities held by the issuer of the index participation unit". A security that is less than 10% of the IPU's holdings does not count.

If one issuer fails, the damage to the fund stays limited. But the limit counts issuers, not sectors, so a fund can obey it and hold only one sector.

### Advantages and disadvantages of managed products

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Professional management | Ongoing fees (MER) reduce returns every year |
| Diversification with a small investment | No control over holdings or the timing of gains |
| Liquidity: daily redemption or exchange trading | Taxable distributions the investor did not choose |
| Automatic plans, reinvestment, record-keeping | Active funds may lag their benchmark |
| Access to hard-to-reach markets | Closed-end funds and REITs can trade below NAV |

The same points are part of the suitability determination. Under IDPC 3402(1), the dealer must consider "the impact of the action on the retail client's account, including the concentration" and liquidity of the securities in it. It must also consider "the potential and actual impact of costs on the retail client's returns" (see 1.17 and 5.12).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Soraya holds 70% of her portfolio in one technology fund with 40 stocks. She asks her RR, Hamza, to buy more of the fund. Hamza tells her that her account is already concentrated. Soraya says, "It is a fund, so I am diversified."
>
> **Question.** Is she right, and what must the dealer consider?
>
> **Resolution.** Only in part. The fund spreads her money over 40 issuers. But all of them are in one sector, so one sector shock hits her whole holding. Before the purchase, the dealer, through Hamza, must consider this concentration as a suitability factor (IDPC 3402(1)(i)(c)).

## 5.3 Main features of mutual funds

### Access to mutual funds in Canada

Registered dealers sell mutual funds, and the registration category limits what each may sell:

- an **investment dealer** may deal in "any security";
- a **mutual fund dealer** may deal only in mutual funds and labour-sponsored funds.

Investors reach mutual funds through several channels:

- an RR at an investment dealer or a mutual fund dealer;
- a bank branch, through the bank's mutual fund dealer;
- an order-execution-only (OEO) account, which gives no advice;
- a dealer that is affiliated with the fund manager.

Minimum investments are usually low. An investor can buy with a lump sum or through a pre-authorized purchase plan. The dealer must normally deliver the latest **Fund Facts** before it accepts the purchase instruction (exceptions: 5.7).

### Structure: trust or corporation

In a **trust**, the trustee holds legal title to the assets for the investors, and the custodian has physical custody. Income and gains flow through to the investors. A **corporation** issues shares, often in several classes, and pays dividends and capital gains dividends. A switch between classes of a mutual fund corporation can be a taxable disposition. If only the fees or expenses differ, a switch between series of the same class is not a disposition (see 5.11).

### Participants: trustee, manager, distributor, custodian

| Participant | Role |
|---|---|
| Trustee | Holds legal title for investors (trusts only) |
| Manager | Directs the fund's business and hires the portfolio adviser. The term excludes "others, such as trustees" who do not direct it |
| Distributor | Sells units: a principal distributor and/or participating dealers |
| Custodian | Holds the portfolio assets: they are held under the custodianship of **one custodian**, which may use sub-custodians (limited exceptions for margin and collateral deposits) |

**Why a separate custodian?** The rules split the duties. The manager directs the fund's business, but it "must not act as custodian or sub-custodian" of the fund. For assets in Canada, the custodian must be a qualified institution. Examples are a Schedule I, II or III bank and a trust company with equity of not less than $10,000,000. This split protects investors, because the firm that directs the fund does not also hold its assets.

CSA guidance gives a related purpose for the s. 6.5 rules on how a fund's assets are held. These rules are "designed to protect an investment fund from loss in the event of the insolvency of those holding its portfolio assets".

### Fee structures

The investor pays some fees directly. The fund pays other fees, and these reduce its return.

- **Paid by the investor:** a **front-end sales charge** (usually negotiable, deducted from the amount invested), switch fees and short-term trading fees.
- **Paid by the fund:** the **management fee plus operating expenses**, which together form the **MER** (see 5.10).
- **Trailing commission:** part of the management fee. It is "paid for as long as you own the fund" and it is "for the services and advice that your representative and their firm provide to you".

Under NI 81-102, a manager must not charge an investor a fee for a redemption. So a manager cannot charge a deferred sales charge (DSC). Where a fund imposes short-term trading fees, the fund usually receives them, not the manager. **Fee-based series** pay no trailing commission, and investors hold them in fee-based accounts.

### Daily pricing

The fund fills each order at the NAVPS "next determined after the receipt" of the order. This is **forward pricing**. The fund may treat an order after the cut-off time as received on the next business day.

Forward pricing protects the other investors in the fund. If investors could buy at a known, stale price after good news, the other investors would pay for their gain.

NI 81-106 sets these minimum NAV frequencies:

- **weekly**, if the fund does not use specified derivatives or sell short;
- **every business day**, if the fund uses specified derivatives or sells short.

One exception applies to the weekly minimum only. A fund that calculated NAV at least monthly when NI 81-106 came into force may keep that frequency. The exception does not apply to a fund that must calculate NAV every business day. Daily pricing is industry practice, not a universal rule.

## 5.4 Mutual fund considerations and risk ranking

### Advantages and disadvantages to the investor and to the provider

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Investor** | Diversification, management, low minimums, redemption at NAV, automatic plans, plain-language Fund Facts | Fees, forced taxable distributions, no intraday trading, possible underperformance |
| **Provider** | Asset-based fee income, scale, recurring contributions, dealer distribution | Redemptions force asset sales, disclosure and compliance costs, fee pressure from ETFs |

If many investors redeem at the same time, the manager must sell assets, perhaps at poor prices. The remaining investors share that cost.

### Sources of return

Return comes from income distributions (interest, dividends, foreign income), capital gains distributions and NAV growth. The CRA lists the possible distributions: "capital gains, capital gains dividends, dividends, foreign income, interest, other income, return of capital". A return of capital is the investor's own money paid back, not a return on it (see 5.11).

### Sources of risk

Risk comes from the market, interest rates, credit, currency, concentration, liquidity and the manager. For example, if the Canadian dollar rises, unhedged foreign holdings fall in Canadian-dollar value.

Fund Facts states the basic point: "The value of the fund can go down as well as up. You could lose money." If a fund has no guarantee, Fund Facts adds that "You may not get back the amount of money you invest."

### Risk-ranking methodology

Each mutual fund must set its investment risk level "at least annually" under NI 81-102 Appendix F. Appendix F sets one mandatory method, so all funds use the same scale:

1. Take the fund's monthly returns over the most recent 10 years (120 months).
2. Calculate the returns from NAV with distributions reinvested.
3. Find the annualized standard deviation of these returns.
4. Place the result in one of five bands.

| Annualized standard deviation (%) | Risk level |
|---|---|
| 0 to less than 6 | Low |
| 6 to less than 11 | Low to medium |
| 11 to less than 16 | Medium |
| 16 to less than 20 | Medium to high |
| 20 or greater | High |

Fund Facts explains that funds with higher volatility "typically have a greater chance of losing money and may have a greater chance of higher returns."

**Special cases.** If a fund has less than 10 years of history, it uses its own returns. It must fill in the missing years from a proxy:

- if it is a clone fund and its underlying fund has 10 years of history, it uses the underlying fund's returns;
- if another NI 81-102 fund has 10 years of history and the same manager, portfolio manager, objectives and strategies, it uses that fund's returns;
- only if neither of the first two cases applies, it uses a reference index that "reasonably approximates" its standard deviation.

The first two cases have equal rank. The rule does not put one before the other. If a clone or matching fund exists, the new fund must use the returns of that clone or matching fund. The manager cannot choose a reference index instead.

If a fund uses a reference index, it must monitor the index at least annually. It must also describe the index in its prospectus.

If a series has an attribute that results in a different risk level, the series uses its own returns. Currency hedging is an attribute that could have this effect. The manager may raise the level if that is "reasonable in the circumstances". The manager must keep records of how it set the level.

> **Exam trap:** The rating measures past **volatility**, not the chance of loss. Fund Facts must say it "doesn't tell you how volatile the fund will be in the future" and that a low-rated fund "can still lose money".

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Lucien works for the manager of the Tamarack Global Equity Fund. The fund has 4 years of returns. No underlying fund exists. No other fund with 10 years of history has the same manager, portfolio manager, objectives and strategies.
>
> **Question.** How does the manager set the fund's risk level?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. The fund has less than 10 years of history, so it uses its own 4 years of monthly returns.
> 2. No clone or matching fund qualifies. So the manager selects a reference index that reasonably approximates the fund's standard deviation, and uses its returns for the other 6 years.
> 3. It places the annualized 10-year standard deviation in a band, and it may raise the level if that is reasonable.
> 4. It keeps records, monitors the index at least annually and describes it in the prospectus.

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 15.1.1, Appendix F Items 1–5; Form 81-101F3 Part I Item 4; CRA "Tax treatment of mutual funds"; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.5 ETFs

### Access to ETFs

Investors buy and sell ETFs on an exchange through a brokerage account during trading hours. ETF Facts explains: "ETFs hold a basket of investments, like mutual funds, but trade on exchanges like stocks."

A dealer that acts as agent for the purchaser must deliver the **ETF Facts** "not later than midnight on the second business day after" the purchase. This does not apply if the dealer has delivered it before. No prospectus delivery applies to ETFs (see 5.7).

### Creation and redemption

Authorized dealers, including the ETF's designated broker, deliver a basket of securities to the ETF for a large block of new units (*creation*). They return a block of units for the basket (*redemption*). If an investor redeems fewer than the manager-prescribed number of units, an ETF in continuous distribution may use a closing exchange price. That price is the one "next determined after the receipt" of the redemption order.

**Why it matters.** Arbitrage keeps the market price near NAV. When the price rises above NAV, authorized dealers create units and sell them. When it falls below NAV, they buy units and redeem them. In-kind redemptions also mean fewer taxable sales inside the fund. If the underlying securities are hard to trade, the price can drift from NAV.

### Pricing: market price vs net asset value (NAV)

"ETFs have two sets of prices: market price and net asset value (NAV)." The market price moves during the day with supply and demand. The fund calculates the NAV after each close.

- **Bid and ask.** "The bid is the highest price a buyer is willing to pay if you want to sell". The ask is the lowest price at which a holder will sell.
- **Spread.** A smaller **bid–ask spread** generally means a more liquid ETF.
- **Orders.** A market order fills at the best price available now. A limit order sets the highest price to buy or the lowest price to sell, and it may not fill.
- **Timing.** Prices can be more volatile near the open and the close. So ETF Facts suggests "using a limit order or placing a trade at another time" of day.

### ETF management styles

Most ETFs use passive indexing. An index ETF can track its index in two ways:

- **full replication**: it holds every security in the index, at the index weights;
- **sampling**: it holds a representative subset, which costs less but can add tracking error.

The gap between the ETF's return and the index return is the tracking difference. Tracking error is the variability of that gap (see 6.11). An **active** ETF has a manager who selects securities to beat a benchmark. It usually has a higher MER than an index ETF, and its holdings change with the manager's decisions. Other styles are smart beta, leveraged and inverse (see 5.6).

### The use of leverage

NI 81-102 restricts leverage in mutual funds, including ETFs. CSA guidance says the investment restrictions are "in part intended to prevent the use of specified derivatives for the purpose of leveraging the assets of a mutual fund".

A conventional mutual fund may borrow cash only as a temporary measure for one of these purposes:

- to meet redemptions while it sells assets in an orderly way;
- to settle portfolio trades.

Its total borrowing must not exceed **5% of NAV**.

An alternative mutual fund or a non-redeemable investment fund may borrow up to **50% of NAV**, if all of these apply:

- the lender is an entity that can act as a fund custodian (s. 6.2 or 6.3);
- if the lender is an affiliate or associate of the manager, the fund's independent review committee approved the agreement;
- the agreement follows normal industry practice, on standard commercial terms.

An alternative mutual fund's total exposure to borrowing, short sales and specified derivatives must not exceed **300% of NAV**. Leveraged and inverse ETFs use derivatives such as swaps and futures to get their exposure (see 5.6). For the investor, leverage magnifies both gains and losses.

### Advantages and disadvantages of ETFs over mutual funds

| | ETF | Conventional mutual fund |
|---|---|---|
| Trading | Intraday, on an exchange | Once a day, at the next NAV |
| Costs | Commissions, bid–ask spread, usually a lower MER | Any sales charge; trailing commission inside the MER |
| Tax | Fewer capital gains distributions | Redemptions may force taxable sales |
| Disclosure | ETF Facts, within 2 business days after the trade | Fund Facts, normally before the trade |

Small automatic purchases with no trade commission favour a mutual fund.

### Cost structures

An ETF investor "may have to pay a commission every time you buy and sell", plus the bid–ask spread. The ETF itself has an MER and a TER, and some ETFs pay a trailing commission.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Teodor places a market order for a thinly traded ETF one minute after the open. The ask is well above the bid. It is also above the value of the ETF's holdings at that time.
>
> **Question.** What went wrong, and what is the better method?
>
> **Resolution.** Prices can be more volatile at the start of the day, and a thin market has a wide spread. His market order paid the full ask, so he paid more than the holdings were worth. He should check the average spread in the ETF Facts. He should use a limit order, or trade later in the day.

## 5.6 Fund management styles

### Active and passive management

An **active** manager selects securities or market timing to beat a benchmark. The aim is alpha, at a higher cost and with a risk that the fund lags (see 5.8).

A **passive** fund holds an index's securities "in substantially the same proportion" as the index, or replicates its performance. The aim is the market return at a low cost, with low turnover and low tax. It does not try to beat its index. After costs, its return is usually a little below the index return.

### Smart beta and factor investing

A smart-beta index follows rules, like a passive index. But it weights securities by factors, not by market capitalization: value, size, momentum, low volatility, quality or dividend. The aim is transparent factor premiums at a mid-range cost, but a factor can lag for years. Smart-beta products track factor indices (see 4.9), and the factor models are in 6.7.

### Leveraged funds

Leveraged ETFs "seek to deliver multiples of the performance of the index or benchmark they track" (e.g. 2×), through swaps, futures and other derivatives. Their purpose is short-term tactical exposure. Most **reset daily**, so their objective is a *daily* one. Daily returns compound, so longer-period returns can differ sharply from the multiple. A 2× fund can fall in a year in which its index rises.

### Inverse funds

Inverse ETFs seek "the opposite of the performance of the index or benchmark they track", or a multiple of it. They serve as a short-term hedge or a bet on a decline. CIRO notes that they "are often marketed as a way for investors to profit from, or at least hedge their exposure to, downward-moving markets".

CIRO gives a two-day example in which the index ends where it started:

- if the index goes from 100 to 101 and back to 100, "the inverse ETF loses 0.02 per cent";
- if the index goes from 100 to 110 and back to 100, "the inverse ETF loses 1.82 per cent".

More volatility gives a larger loss, although the index is unchanged.

### Dealer obligations for leveraged and inverse ETFs

CIRO guidance says leveraged and inverse ETFs that reset daily "typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session, particularly in volatile markets". Dealers must:

- understand the product, including how volatility, leverage and the holding period affect its return;
- make a client-specific suitability determination;
- keep sales materials fair, and never imply that the fund tracks its index over a longer period;
- supervise recommendations and train staff.

Risk disclosure in a prospectus "does not cure otherwise deficient disclosure in sales material".

> **Exam trap:** A −1× inverse ETF held for a month will not return exactly minus the index's monthly return. Its objective is a *daily* one.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Elspeth expects a market decline over the next 6 months. She asks her RR to buy a −1× inverse ETF as a hedge and hold it.
>
> **Question.** What must the RR consider?
>
> **Resolution.** The ETF resets daily, so over 6 months its return can differ sharply from minus the index return, more so in volatile markets. CIRO guidance says such ETFs are typically unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them longer than one trading session. This applies particularly in volatile markets. The dealer, through the RR, must make a client-specific suitability determination. The RR must not imply that the ETF tracks the index over 6 months.

## 5.7 Information sources: Fund Facts and ETF Facts

### The Fund Facts document (Form 81-101F3)

Fund Facts is a short summary that "must be prepared using plain language". The fund files a Fund Facts document "for each class or series" of a mutual fund. Each of Part I and Part II is normally one page, and the document has four pages at most. It covers these headings:

- **Quick Facts** (including the MER), **What does the fund invest in?** and **How risky is it?** (see 5.4);
- **How has the fund performed?**: year-by-year returns (up to 10 calendar years), best and worst 3-month returns, average return;
- **Who is this fund for?** and **A word about tax**;
- **How much does it cost?**: sales charges, fund expenses, the trailing commission and other fees;
- **What if I change my mind?**: the investor's rights.

**Delivery rule.** Unless the dealer has previously done so, it must deliver the latest Fund Facts **before it accepts the purchase instruction**.

**Exception for an urgent purchase.** All of these conditions must apply:

1. The client instructs that the purchase "must be completed immediately or by a specified time".
2. Delivery before that time is not reasonably practicable.
3. Before it accepts the order, the dealer does all of these:
   - it explains the existence and purpose of Fund Facts and its own obligation to deliver it;
   - it gets the client's consent to later delivery for that specific purchase (a blanket consent is not valid);
   - it gives a verbal summary of the key sections.

The dealer must then deliver it "not later than midnight on the second business day after entering into the purchase".

**Exception for automatic plans.** A purchase under a pre-authorized purchase plan or a rebalancing plan needs no new Fund Facts if all of these apply:

- the purchase is not the first under the plan;
- the dealer has sent the required notice, which says that later plan purchases have no withdrawal right but the misrepresentation remedy stays;
- the dealer reminds the client in writing, at least annually, how to request the Fund Facts;
- the dealer sends it on request.

**Exception for managed accounts and some permitted clients.** A dealer need not deliver Fund Facts for a purchase in a managed account. The same applies if the purchaser is a permitted client that is not an individual.

> **Key rule:** "Under securities law in some provinces and territories", investors may "withdraw from an agreement to buy mutual funds within two business days after you receive a simplified prospectus or Fund Facts document", or "cancel your purchase within 48 hours after you receive confirmation of the purchase." (Form 81-101F3 Part II Item 2)

**Misrepresentation.** In some provinces and territories, an investor may also cancel a purchase or "claim damages, if the simplified prospectus, Fund Facts document or financial statements contain a misrepresentation".

### The ETF Facts document (Form 41-101F4)

The syllabus calls it the "EFT fact sheet". The ETF prepares it for each class and series, in plain language. Like Fund Facts, each Part is normally one page, and the document must "not exceed four pages in length". It must not incorporate anything by reference. It adds trading and pricing data:

- ticker symbol and exchange;
- average daily volume;
- market price and NAV;
- average bid–ask spread.

In some provinces and territories, its statement of rights gives a right to cancel within 48 hours after the confirmation. It gives no two-day withdrawal right. It also describes the remedy for a misrepresentation.

### Other information sources

The **simplified prospectus** incorporates by reference the Fund Facts, the financial statements and the management reports of fund performance (MRFPs). On request, the fund must send any of these "within three business days of receipt of the request and free of charge". The fund must make its NAVPS public "at no cost". Fund websites, the financial press and independent rating services add more information.

### Other managed products

- **Listed closed-end funds** are investment funds, so they file annual financial statements under NI 81-106.
- **REITs and income trusts** are reporting issuers. They file annual financial statements and MD&A under NI 51-102.
- **Pooled and hedge funds** sold under prospectus exemptions have no prospectus or Fund Facts. Investors rely on the offering memorandum or other offering documents and the manager's reports.

> **Remember:** Fund Facts: normally **before** the trade (client-requested immediate purchase: within 2 business days after). ETF Facts: within **two business days after**. Both documents: one page per Part (normally 2 pages), 4 pages at most.

## 5.8 Evaluating fund performance

### Measuring return: holding period return (HPR)

HPR is the total gain over the period, including income:

```
HPR = (Ending value − Beginning value + Income) ÷ Beginning value
```

For example, $10,000 that grows to $10,500 and pays $200 of income gives (10,500 − 10,000 + 200) ÷ 10,000 = 7%. HPR is not annualized. It assumes no deposits or withdrawals during the period, and it ignores when the investor received the income.

### Measuring return: money-weighted rate of return (MWRR)

MWRR is the internal rate of return of the investor's own cash flows, so it depends on the **timing and size of deposits and withdrawals**. If a client adds money just before a decline, the client's MWRR is below the fund's published return.

MWRR answers the client's question: "How did my own money do?" Clients see it on their annual performance report. IDPC 3810 requires returns "calculated net of charges using a money weighted rate of return calculation methodology generally accepted in the securities industry". If the client makes no deposits or withdrawals in the period, MWRR and TWRR give the same result.

### Measuring return: time-weighted rate of return (TWRR)

TWRR splits the period at each cash flow. It calculates the return for each sub-period, then links these returns by compounding. Cash flows do not affect the result, so TWRR measures the **manager**. The manager does not control when investors buy or sell. Convention: use TWRR for fund and manager performance, and MWRR for the client's own account.

### Comparing return: benchmark

A **benchmark** is an index that matches the fund's asset class, style and region (see 5.9). The difference between the two returns shows what the manager added or lost. A poor benchmark gives a false answer: a small-cap fund looks skilled against a large-cap index in a year when small caps lead.

### Comparing return: peer group

A **peer group** is a set of funds with similar objectives. Rating services often rank funds by quartile: a first-quartile fund is in the top 25% of its peers. Two problems can distort peer comparisons:

- **style drift**: a fund moves away from its stated style, so its peers no longer match it;
- **survivorship bias**: closed or merged funds drop out of the data, so the average of the survivors looks too good.

In every comparison:

1. Use the same periods.
2. Compare returns after fees.
3. Consider the risk that the fund took.

> **Exam trap:** A client's return can differ from the fund's published return. A common cause is the timing of the client's cash flows (MWRR vs TWRR). Others are sales charges and account fees, which published returns exclude, and a different series or period. None of these reflects on the manager.

*Sources: IDPC 3810(2)(v); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.9 Valuing funds and measuring their performance

### Pricing of mutual fund units: NAV and NAVPS

NAV is "the value of the total assets of the investment fund less the value of the total" liabilities. The fund measures it at fair value. It includes income and expenses accrued to the valuation date. Purchases and redemptions take place at the next-determined NAVPS.

```
NAVPS = (Total assets − Total liabilities) ÷ Units outstanding
Units issued = Net amount invested ÷ NAVPS
Value of a holding = Units held × NAVPS
Total return = (Ending NAVPS − Beginning NAVPS + Distributions per unit) ÷ Beginning NAVPS
```

The total-return formula is for a single period, with distributions received in cash.

> **Worked example**
> Birchwood Balanced Fund has assets of $50,500,000, liabilities of $500,000 and 4,000,000 units outstanding.
> 1. NAVPS = (50,500,000 − 500,000) ÷ 4,000,000 = **$12.50**
> 2. Anouk invests $10,000 and pays a 2% front-end charge of 0.02 × 10,000 = $200. Units issued = 9,800 ÷ 12.50 = **784 units** (with no charge: 10,000 ÷ 12.50 = 800 units).
> 3. One year later the NAVPS is $13.10, after $0.40 per unit of distributions paid in cash. Her holding is worth 784 × 13.10 = **$10,270.40**, and she has received 784 × 0.40 = **$313.60** of cash distributions.
> 4. Total return per unit = (13.10 − 12.50 + 0.40) ÷ 12.50 = 1.00 ÷ 12.50 = **8.0%**. Anouk's own return = (10,270.40 + 313.60 − 10,000) ÷ 10,000 = **5.84%**, lower than 8.0% because of the $200 charge.

### Standard performance data and total return

NI 81-102 sets standards for performance in sales communications:

- a sales communication must not "be untrue or misleading";
- it generally may show performance only after **12 consecutive months** of prospectus distribution (exceptions include communications sent only to the fund's investors);
- it must reflect "all elements of return";
- it must include standard performance data.

For funds other than money market funds, standard performance data means annual compounded total returns for **10, 5, 3 and 1 years**, as they apply. The returns assume reinvested distributions and exclude sales charges and taxes. If a mutual fund has offered securities by prospectus for more than 1 and less than 10 years, it also shows a return since inception. A money market fund instead reports a current yield (and optionally an effective yield) for the most recent **seven-day** period. The warning ends "past performance may not be repeated".

### Use of comparisons

If a sales communication compares a fund with a benchmark or another investment, it must:

- include "all facts that, if disclosed, would be likely to alter materially the conclusions" of the comparison;
- present data "for the same period or periods";
- explain any factors needed to make the comparison "fair and not misleading".

For a benchmark, it must also use one that existed and was widely recognized and available during the period. If the benchmark did not exist, a reconstruction on a consistent basis must be widely recognized and available. Advertisements may compare only with funds under common management, funds with similar objectives, or an index.

### Appropriate benchmarks

A valid benchmark has seven properties:

- **specified in advance**, before the evaluation period;
- **appropriate** to the manager's style and mandate;
- **measurable**, with a return that can be calculated regularly;
- **unambiguous**, with clearly defined constituents and weights;
- **reflective** of the manager's current investment universe;
- **accountable**, accepted by the manager as the fair yardstick;
- **investable**, so the investor could hold it passively instead.

A benchmark chosen after the period can make the fund look better than it is. One outside the manager's universe measures luck, not skill.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Bram, an RR, reviews an advertisement for the Cedarline Canadian Small-Cap Fund. It compares the fund's best year with the S&P 500 for a different year.
>
> **Question.** What is wrong with the comparison?
>
> **Resolution.** The periods differ, so the comparison rules are not met. An index is a permitted comparison, but it must be fair and not misleading. A U.S. large-cap index is also not appropriate or reflective for a Canadian small-cap mandate.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** One year ago, Clio held 1,200 units of a fund at a NAVPS of $20.00. Today the NAVPS is $21.30, after cash distributions of $0.50 per unit. The fund's benchmark returned 6.5% over the same year.
>
> **Question.** What is her holding worth, and did the fund beat its benchmark?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. Value of the holding = 1,200 × 21.30 = **$25,560**.
> 2. Total return = (21.30 − 20.00 + 0.50) ÷ 20.00 = 1.80 ÷ 20.00 = **9.0%**.
> 3. 9.0% − 6.5% = **2.5 percentage points** above the benchmark, before any account fees.

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 1.1 ("net asset value"), 9.3, 10.3, 15.2(1), 15.3(1), 15.4(6), 15.6, 15.7, 15.8, 15.10; NI 81-106 s. 14.2(1)–(1.1); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.10 Impact of costs on performance

### Loads and charges

**Loads and charges** reduce the amount invested or the return. They include front-end charges, switch fees, short-term trading fees and fee-based account fees. A front-end charge means fewer units from the start (see 5.9).

### Assessing expense ratios: MER and TER

| Measure | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **MER** | Management fee (including any trailing commission) plus operating expenses, as a % of average NAV |
| **TER** | The fund's trading costs (commissions and other portfolio transaction costs), which are **excluded** from the MER |
| **Fund expenses** | MER + TER: the total ongoing cost. Fund Facts states it as a % and as $ per $1,000 invested |

Investors do not pay these directly, but "they reduce the fund's returns". Published returns are net of them. A 2.0% MER costs $20 a year per $1,000 invested.

Compare expense ratios only between similar funds and series. A fee-based series has a lower MER because it pays no trailing commission, but the client pays an account fee instead.

### Turnover

High portfolio turnover means more trading. It raises the TER and the spread costs. It also realizes more capital gains, which the fund distributes as taxable income in non-registered accounts.

### Taxes on the fund

A trust pays tax on income that it keeps. So a mutual fund trust normally distributes all its net income and net capital gains each year. The investor, not the fund, then pays the tax.

A mutual fund corporation (see 5.3) pays income to investors only as dividends, including capital gains dividends. It cannot pass interest or foreign income through as such. So it can pay tax inside the fund on that income, unless its expenses offset it. That tax lowers the fund's return.

Foreign countries can also withhold tax on the fund's own foreign income. This tax lowers the fund's return, unless the investor can recover it (see the table below).

### Taxes on the investor

In a non-registered account, distributions (T3 or T5 slips) are taxable "whether you get them in cash or have them reinvested". The CRA considers that the investor received the amounts before the reinvestment. Redemptions can also realize capital gains (see 5.11).

### Withholding tax: recoverable and unrecoverable

Many foreign countries withhold tax on dividends paid to Canadian residents, and some also withhold tax on interest. Under the Canada–U.S. treaty, U.S. tax on dividends paid to a Canadian-resident individual must not exceed **15%**.

| Withholding tax | When it applies |
|---|---|
| **Recoverable** | Non-registered account: the foreign income is "reported on your Canadian tax return", so the investor claims a **foreign tax credit** (Form T2209). A fund trust passes foreign tax through (T3 box 34) |
| **Unrecoverable** | No foreign tax credit is possible, so the tax is a permanent cost (cases below) |

Withholding tax is unrecoverable in these cases:

- the income is in a TFSA, where income is "generally tax-free", or in an RESP or FHSA. The investor does not report the foreign income, so there is no credit;
- a fund held in any registered plan pays the tax on its own foreign holdings;
- a foreign-listed fund pays the tax on its own foreign holdings and does not pass it through.

The treaty also exempts some arrangements from U.S. tax on dividends and interest. The arrangement must meet all of these conditions:

- it is "a resident of a Contracting State";
- it is "generally exempt from income taxation in a taxable year in that State";
- it is "operated exclusively to administer or provide pension, retirement or employee benefits".

The exemption does not cover income from a trade or business or from a related person (Art. XXI(4)).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Emeka holds a Canadian-listed ETF of U.S. stocks. He holds one position in a non-registered account and one in his TFSA.
>
> **Question.** Can he recover the U.S. tax withheld on the dividends?
>
> **Resolution.** In the non-registered account, yes, if the ETF is a trust. The trust passes the foreign tax through on his T3 slip, and he claims a foreign tax credit. In the TFSA, no: he does not report the income, so he has no credit to claim.

*Sources: Form 81-101F3 Part I Items 5, 8, Part II Item 1.3; NI 81-106 s. 1.1, 15.1; CRA "Tax treatment of mutual funds"; CRA "Foreign tax credit"; CRA "Tax-free Savings Account"; Canada–U.S. Tax Convention Art. X(2), Art. XXI(2), (4); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.11 Implications of redemptions

### Tax consequences of a redemption

A redemption is a disposition. The capital gain or loss is the proceeds minus the ACB minus the selling costs. Selling costs include "redemption fees and commissions".

- **Average cost.** CRA treats units or shares in the same class or series of a fund as identical properties. So the ACB is the **average cost per unit** (ACB mechanics: 7.10).
- **Separate series.** An investor with several classes or series of one fund calculates the ACB "for each class or series separately".
- **Switches.** If only the fees or expenses differ, a switch between series of the same class of corporation shares is not a disposition. Other switches can be dispositions.

### Tax consequences of T-SWPs

A **T-SWP** (tax-efficient systematic withdrawal plan) uses T-class or T-series units. It pays monthly amounts that are largely **return of capital (ROC)**. The investor pays no tax on ROC when the investor receives it. But ROC **reduces the ACB**. This defers tax until the investor sells the units.

The CRA states the next rule for units of a mutual fund trust. If the ACB of the trust units falls below zero, "the negative amount is deemed to be a capital gain in the year". The ACB then resets to zero.

For shares of a mutual fund corporation, the T5 slip does not report ROC. The investor must keep track of it to calculate the ACB correctly.

> **Exam trap:** ROC is not tax-free income. It only defers tax, because it lowers the ACB. The investor pays the deferred tax as a capital gain at the sale. If the ACB of mutual fund trust units is already zero, any further ROC is a capital gain in the year of receipt.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Wanjiru holds T-series units of a mutual fund trust in a non-registered account. Her ACB is $2,000. Over several years she receives $2,600 of ROC.
>
> **Question.** What is the tax effect?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. She pays no tax on each ROC payment when she receives it. But each payment reduces her ACB.
> 2. The first $2,000 of ROC reduces her ACB to zero.
> 3. The other $600 would make the ACB negative. So it is a deemed capital gain in the year she receives it, and her ACB stays at zero.
> 4. If she sells later, her whole proceeds are a capital gain, because her ACB is zero.

### Withdrawal plans

A withdrawal plan redeems units on a schedule. There are three main types:

- **fixed dollar**: a set amount, e.g. $500 a month;
- **fixed period**: the plan pays out the holding over a set number of years;
- **ratio**: a set percentage of the holding's value each year.

If payouts are more than the fund's return, they reduce the capital. If the investor sells in a falling market, the losses become permanent. In a non-registered account, each redemption can cause a capital gain or loss. CIRO guidance says that planned withdrawals need no suitability determination before each withdrawal. But a Registered Individual "should make a suitability determination before establishing a plan and when a triggering event occurs".

### Suspension of redemptions: gating

A **gate** limits the redemptions that a fund pays in a period, for example to a set percentage of its assets each quarter. A **suspension** stops all redemptions for a time. In both cases, the investor cannot get all of the money on request. Requests can wait in a queue, and the investor may get only part of the amount, or get it late. Meanwhile, the value of the units can fall.

A mutual fund may suspend redemptions only while one of these applies:

- normal trading is suspended on an exchange that lists **more than 50% of the fund's assets** by value, with no practical alternative market; or
- for a clone fund, the fund it tracks has suspended redemptions.

Other suspensions need regulatory approval. During a suspension, the fund may postpone payment and must not accept purchases.

**Payment timing.** Conventional mutual funds pay within **two business days** of the NAV date. Alternative mutual funds and non-redeemable funds pay within **15 business days**. If its prospectus discloses it, an alternative mutual fund may also bar redemptions "for a period of up to 6 months" after the receipt for its initial prospectus. Hedge and pooled funds set their own gates, lock-ups and notice periods in their offering documents.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** The Kestrel Resource Fund holds 60% of its assets in securities listed on one foreign exchange. That exchange halts all trading for three days, and no other exchange trades the securities.
>
> **Question.** May the fund suspend redemptions?
>
> **Resolution.** Yes. Trading is suspended on an exchange that lists more than 50% of its assets, with no practical alternative. It may postpone payment and must not accept purchases. At only 30% of assets, the fund would need regulatory approval to suspend.

## 5.12 Managed vs non-managed products

### The investor's decisions

An investor who chooses between a managed product and direct holdings weighs:

- **Expertise and time.** A managed product delegates research and monitoring. Direct holdings need both.
- **Portfolio size.** A small account cannot diversify cheaply with individual securities.
- **Control.** A direct holder chooses each trade and when to realize gains or losses.
- **Cost.** Direct holdings have commissions but no MER. A fund has ongoing fees.
- **Transparency.** A direct holder always knows the holdings. A fund discloses them only periodically.
- **Values.** A direct holder can exclude any company. A fund applies its own screens.
- **Income or growth needs.** Both routes can meet either need.

### The Registered Representative's recommendation

The recommendation must meet the IDPC 3402(1) suitability standard. Under it, the dealer must determine that the action is suitable and **puts the client's interest first** (factors: 1.17). For this choice, two factors usually matter most:

- **cost**: "the potential and actual impact of costs on the retail client's returns";
- **alternatives**: "a consideration of a reasonable range of alternative actions available" through the dealer.

The RR compares the main routes on these two factors:

- **Mutual fund.** The MER pays for management and the trailing commission. Small automatic purchases cost nothing extra.
- **ETF.** The MER is usually lower, but each trade has a commission and a spread. Occasional larger purchases suit it best.
- **Direct securities.** There is no MER, but each trade has a commission. A large account can diversify directly at a lower total cost.
- **Managed account.** An account fee replaces the MER, and a portfolio manager manages individual securities for the client.

Each direction can go wrong. A small account in a few stocks is concentrated and costly to diversify. A large account in high-MER funds may pay much more than a reasonable alternative, and the costs reduce the client's returns. If the client insists on an unsuitable choice, the IDPC 3402(5) steps apply (1.18).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Thandiwe has $15,000 to invest, adds $200 a month and has little time or knowledge. She asks her RR whether to buy a few bank stocks.
>
> **Question.** What should the RR recommend, and why?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. A few stocks in one sector give her a concentrated account, which is a suitability factor.
> 2. Her small amounts cannot buy a diversified portfolio of stocks at a low cost.
> 3. A diversified fund or ETF portfolio gives her diversification and delegation.
> 4. The dealer, through the RR, compares costs and reasonable alternatives. The RR then recommends the choice that puts her interest first.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Desmond has $1.5 million in a non-registered account, all in high-MER equity funds. He follows markets closely. He wants to choose his holdings and decide when to realize capital gains.
>
> **Question.** What alternatives should the RR consider?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. His account is large enough to diversify with individual securities or ETFs.
> 2. The MERs apply to all $1.5 million each year, so costs have a large impact on his returns.
> 3. Direct holdings give him control of trades and of the timing of gains and losses. A managed account suits him if he wants professional management of individual securities.
> 4. A sale of the funds can realize capital gains, so the RR also considers the tax cost of a change.
> 5. The RR recommends the alternative that puts his interest first, which is likely direct holdings or a managed account.

*Sources: IDPC 3402(1)(i)(c)–(e), (ii), 3402(5); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.13 Alternative strategy funds and alternative assets

### Hedge funds

A hedge fund is a private pool that uses leverage, short sales and derivatives. Strategies include long/short, market-neutral, macro, event-driven and arbitrage. The aim is an absolute return: a positive return whether markets rise or fall. Dealers usually sell hedge funds under prospectus exemptions.

### Structured products

A structured product is a note or deposit whose payoff is linked to an index or a reference asset. A common example is the **principal-protected note (PPN)**. Under the *Bank Act*, a PPN pays amounts linked to "an index or reference point" and repays at least the amount paid. Before issuance, the bank must disclose the product's risks, "including, if applicable, the risk that no interest may accrue". It must also disclose any lack of CDIC coverage.

Other structured notes are **not principal-protected**. Their return and the repayment of principal both depend on the reference asset, so the investor can lose part of the principal. All structured notes carry the issuer's credit risk, and they can be hard to sell before maturity.

### Alternative investment funds

An **alternative mutual fund** ("liquid alt") is a public mutual fund with a prospectus. It is a mutual fund "other than a precious metals fund". Its fundamental investment objectives permit it to do these things "in a manner not permitted for other mutual funds":

- invest in physical commodities, crypto assets or specified derivatives;
- borrow cash;
- sell short.

NI 81-102 sets no naming rule. But the CSA would generally expect only alternative mutual funds to use "alternative" in their name.

### Crypto-assets

The CSA generally views a crypto asset as "any digital representation of value that uses cryptography and distributed ledger technology". Investors hold them directly or through funds. CSA guidance describes a "general prohibition on mutual funds investing in crypto assets". These are the exceptions:

- an alternative mutual fund may invest in a crypto asset if "the crypto asset is fungible" and one of these applies:
  - it trades on an exchange that a Canadian securities regulatory authority recognizes;
  - it underlies a specified derivative that trades on such an exchange;
- a conventional mutual fund may enter into a specified derivative on a crypto asset, if the derivative trades on such an exchange. After the purchase, these derivatives, commodity derivatives and permitted precious metals must not exceed **10% of NAV**;
- a non-redeemable investment fund has a similar exception.

### Private equity

Private equity funds buy or invest in private companies, often to improve them and sell them later. Investors usually commit money as limited partners, and the fund calls the money when it invests (see 5.14). Access is usually through a prospectus exemption, for example for accredited investors. The fund locks up the money for many years, often about 10, and the units have no market. The main risks are illiquidity, debt in the companies, and valuations that are estimates, not market prices.

### Venture capital

Venture capital is private equity for early-stage start-ups. Investors usually get access through a venture capital fund under a prospectus exemption, or through a labour-sponsored fund. The horizon is long, and the units are illiquid. Many of the companies fail, and a few large winners provide most of the return. So a venture capital fund needs many investments to spread this risk.

## 5.14 Characteristics of alternative funds and other investments

### Structure

Hedge, private equity and venture funds are usually **limited partnerships** (the general partner manages; investors are limited partners) or trusts. Liquid alts are prospectus mutual funds. Structured notes are the issuer's debt.

### Features

Typical features are:

- leverage;
- short positions and derivatives;
- illiquid holdings;
- lock-ups (periods with no redemption);
- capital calls (demands for committed money);
- limited transparency.

### Fees: management, performance, hurdle rates

- **Management fee:** a fee on assets, e.g. 1–2% a year.
- **Performance fee:** a share of profits, e.g. 20%.
- **Hurdle rate:** the minimum return before the manager earns any performance fee.
- **High-water mark:** no performance fee until the fund recovers its earlier losses.

With a hurdle and a high-water mark, a manager gets no fee for a weak return and no second fee on the same gain.

NI 81-102 also limits performance fees in mutual funds, including alternative mutual funds:

- for a conventional mutual fund, the fee must use a **total-return benchmark or index**. The index must reflect the fund's sectors and be available to persons other than the fund and its service providers. The fund measures the fee cumulatively since the last fee;
- an alternative mutual fund's fee is based on its own **cumulative total return** since the last fee;
- the prospectus must describe the method of calculation. For a conventional fund, it must also describe the composition of the benchmark.

### Advantages and disadvantages to the investor and to the provider

| | Investor | Provider |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Low correlation, absolute-return potential, access to private markets | Two-layer fees; locked-in capital; flexible mandates |
| **Disadvantages** | High fees, illiquidity, leverage, complexity, limited disclosure | Complex operations and compliance; redemption pressure in a crisis |

### Sources of risk and potential returns

The main sources of return are:

- manager skill;
- leverage;
- factor premiums and illiquidity premiums.

The main risks are:

- leverage, which magnifies losses;
- counterparty risk in derivatives;
- liquidity risk;
- valuation risk, because illiquid assets have no market price;
- issuer credit risk (structured notes);
- extreme volatility (crypto assets).

By contrast, a mutual fund, including a liquid alt, has limits on illiquid assets. It must not buy one if more than **10% of its NAV** would then be illiquid. It must not hold more than **15%** of its NAV in illiquid assets for 90 days or more. A non-redeemable investment fund has higher limits: 20% at purchase and 25% for 90 days or more.

### Impact of the costs of acquiring and holding other investments

These costs reduce the return on other investments:

- commissions and wide bid–ask spreads, paid on each trade;
- structuring fees inside notes, which lower the note's payoff;
- custody and storage fees for commodities and crypto assets, paid each year;
- penalties for early redemption, which reduce the amount paid back;
- the low return on money that the investor has committed but the fund has not yet called. The investor must keep that money available, often in low-return assets.

### Accredited investor requirement

Dealers sell most hedge, private equity and pooled funds without a prospectus, to **accredited investors** who buy as principal. The tests use income or wealth as a sign that the investor can accept risk with less disclosure. The rule sets four individual tests: (j), (j.1), (k) and (l) in NI 45-106 s. 1.1. Section 3.1 gives each test and its threshold. An individual under test (j), (k) or (l) signs Form 45-106F9. An individual under test (j.1) does not sign it (see 3.1).

The form summarizes tests (j), (k) and (l) in four statements. The investor must meet at least one:

- net income before taxes over $200,000 in each of the 2 most recent calendar years, and expected in the current year (test (k));
- the same income test with a spouse, at $300,000 (test (k));
- alone or with a spouse, "more than $1 million in cash and securities, after subtracting any debt related to the cash and securities" (test (j));
- alone or with a spouse, net assets worth "more than $5 million", real estate included (test (l)).

The form says "more than $5 million" for test (l). The rule says net assets "of at least $5 000 000" (NI 45-106 s. 1.1(l)). The rule is the authority, so net assets of exactly $5,000,000 meet test (l) (see 3.1).

On the form, the investor must "Initial the statement that applies". The same form is also a risk acknowledgement. It warns: "Don't invest unless you can afford to lose all the money you pay for this investment", and the investor signs it. Initials on the form are not enough: the dealer must take reasonable steps to verify status (more detail: 3.1).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Zofia invests in a hedge fund with an 8% hurdle rate, a 20% performance fee and a high-water mark. Last year the fund lost money. This year it gained, but its NAVPS is still below its previous peak.
>
> **Question.** Does the manager earn a performance fee this year?
>
> **Resolution.** No. Under the high-water mark, the manager earns no performance fee until the fund recovers its earlier losses. The NAVPS is still below the previous peak. The hurdle rate matters only above the high-water mark.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ingrid is single and has no debts. She owns a home worth $3 million. She also has $600,000 in cash and securities and earns $150,000 a year. A dealer offers her a private equity fund and asks her to initial the accredited investor box.
>
> **Question.** Is she an accredited investor?
>
> **Resolution.**
> 1. Income: $150,000 is below $200,000. She has no spouse, so the $300,000 combined test does not apply.
> 2. Cash and securities: $600,000 is below $1 million, and her home does not count.
> 3. Net assets: $3 million + $600,000 = $3.6 million, with no debt to subtract. This is below $5 million.
> 4. She meets none of the four statements on Form 45-106F9. She also fails test (j.1), which needs financial assets over $5,000,000. So the dealer cannot rely on the accredited investor exemption for her on these facts. Her initials do not change that, and the dealer must take reasonable steps to verify status.

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 2.4, 7.1; NI 45-106 s. 2.3; 45-106CP s. 1.9(3); Form 45-106F9; standard finance concept (verified)*
