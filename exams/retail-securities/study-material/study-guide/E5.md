# Element 5: Managed products and other investments

This element covers pooled, professionally managed products: mutual funds, ETFs, closed-end funds, income trusts and REITs, wraps and pooled funds, and alternatives such as hedge funds, structured products, private equity and crypto-asset funds. It carries about **16 of the 120 questions**.

Expect comparisons (trust or corporation? ETF or mutual fund? which document, delivered when?) and short scenarios on costs, redemptions and suitability. Calculations are confined to 5.9: NAV per unit, units issued, the value of a holding, and simple total return.

## 5.1 Types of managed products

The statutory test for a **mutual fund** is redeemability. It is an issuer "whose primary purpose is to invest money provided by its security holders and whose securities entitle the holder to receive on demand" an amount based on its net assets (Ontario *Securities Act*). A **non-redeemable investment fund** (closed-end fund) also pools money but "is not a mutual fund".

| Product | Key features |
|---|---|
| Mutual fund trust | Investors buy **units**; income flows through to them (T3 slips) |
| Mutual fund corporation | Investors buy **shares**, often in several classes of one corporation (T5 slips) |
| Income trust | Listed trust holding an operating business; pays out most cash flow (not guaranteed) |
| Closed-end fund | Units issued mainly at the IPO (no continuous offering); investors usually exit by selling on an exchange, at a **discount** (or premium) to NAV. Some offer periodic redemption at NAV |
| REIT | Listed trust owning income-producing real estate |
| ETF | Fund listed on an exchange and traded intraday; most track an index, some are active. An "index participation unit" is the narrow legal term for a unit that holds an index's securities "in substantially the same proportion" |
| Wrap / fund of funds / ETF wrap | One account or fund holds several funds or ETFs at a target mix for one fee (NI 81-102 s. 2.5 governs fund-on-fund holdings) |
| Pooled fund | No public prospectus: sold under exemptions (e.g. accredited investors, 5.14) or inside discretionary programs |

> **Exam trap:** Closed-end funds and ETFs both trade on exchanges, but only an ETF has a creation/redemption mechanism (5.5) that keeps its price close to NAV. A closed-end fund can trade at a persistent discount.

*Sources: Securities Act (Ontario) s. 1(1); NI 81-106 s. 1.1; NI 81-102 s. 1.1, s. 2.5; NI 45-106 s. 2.3; CRA "Tax treatment of mutual funds"*

## 5.2 Considerations for investors in managed products

**Range of exposures.** One purchase can deliver **income** (money market, bond, dividend funds) or **growth** (equity, small-cap); any **asset class** (cash, bonds, equities, real estate, commodities); a **sector**; a **geography** (Canadian, U.S., international, emerging, global); and **ethical/ESG** mandates (screened, integrated or impact; labels vary, so read the objectives). Broad funds give **diversification**; sector or thematic funds add **concentration**. A conventional mutual fund cannot buy if more than **10% of its NAV** would be "invested in securities of any one issuer" (20% for an alternative mutual fund or a non-redeemable investment fund). Government securities, other investment funds bought under s. 2.5 and index participation units are exempt from the limit.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Professional management | Ongoing fees (MER) reduce returns every year |
| Diversification with a small investment | No control over holdings or the timing of gains |
| Liquidity: daily redemption or exchange trading | Taxable distributions the investor did not choose |
| Automatic plans, reinvestment, record-keeping | Active funds may lag their benchmark |
| Access to hard-to-reach markets | Closed-end funds and REITs can trade below NAV |

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 2.1(1), (1.1), (2); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.3 Main features of mutual funds

**Access.** Mutual funds are sold through registered dealers. An **investment dealer** may deal in "any security". A **mutual fund dealer** may deal only in mutual funds and labour-sponsored funds. The dealer must normally deliver the latest **Fund Facts** before accepting the purchase instruction, with limited exceptions (5.7).

**Structure.** In a **trust**, a trustee holds legal title to the assets for the unitholders (the custodian has physical custody), and income and gains flow through to them. A **corporation** issues shares and pays dividends and capital gains dividends.

| Participant | Role |
|---|---|
| Trustee | Holds legal title for unitholders (trusts only) |
| Manager | Directs the fund's business and hires the portfolio adviser. The term excludes "others, such as trustees" who do not direct it |
| Distributor | Sells units: a principal distributor and/or participating dealers |
| Custodian | Holds the portfolio assets: they are held under the custodianship of **one custodian**, which may use sub-custodians (limited exceptions for margin and collateral deposits) |

**Fee structures.** The investor may pay a **front-end sales charge** (usually negotiable, deducted from the amount invested), switch fees and short-term trading fees. The fund pays the **management fee plus operating expenses (MER)**. The management fee includes the **trailing commission**, "paid for as long as you own the fund". NI 81-102 bars a manager from charging a securityholder a fee for a redemption, so new purchases can no longer carry a manager-charged deferred sales charge (DSC). Short-term trading fees, where a fund imposes them, are usually paid to the fund rather than the manager. **Fee-based series** pay no trailer and are held in fee-based accounts.

**Daily pricing.** Orders are filled at the NAV per unit "next determined after the receipt" of the order (**forward pricing**), and orders after the cut-off may be treated as received the next business day. NI 81-106 requires NAV at least **weekly**, or **every business day** if the fund uses specified derivatives or sells short. Daily pricing is industry practice, not a universal rule.

*Sources: NI 31-103 s. 7.1(2); 31-103CP s. 7.1; NI 81-101 s. 3.2.01; 81-102CP s. 2.9; NI 81-102 s. 1.1 ("custodian", "participating dealer"), 6.1, 9.3, 10.2.1, 10.2(7), 10.3; Form 81-101F3 Part II Item 1; NI 81-106 s. 14.2(3)*

## 5.4 Mutual fund considerations and risk ranking

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Investor** | Diversification, management, low minimums, redemption at NAV, automatic plans, plain-language Fund Facts | Fees, forced taxable distributions, no intraday trading, possible underperformance |
| **Provider** | Asset-based fee income, scale, recurring contributions, dealer distribution | Redemptions force asset sales, disclosure and compliance costs, fee pressure from ETFs |

**Return** comes from income distributions (interest, dividends, foreign income), capital gains distributions and NAV growth. **Risk** comes from the market, interest rates, credit, currency, concentration, liquidity and the manager.

**Risk-ranking methodology.** Each mutual fund must set its investment risk level "at least annually" under NI 81-102 Appendix F. The method uses the **annualized standard deviation** of the fund's monthly returns over the most recent 10 years (n = 120 months), computed from NAV with distributions reinvested and expressed in %. The monthly standard deviation is multiplied by √12, so a 4% monthly figure annualizes to about 13.9% (Medium). The result maps to five levels:

| Annualized standard deviation (%) | Risk level |
|---|---|
| 0 to less than 6 | Low |
| 6 to less than 11 | Low to medium |
| 11 to less than 16 | Medium |
| 16 to less than 20 | Medium to high |
| 20 or greater | High |

A fund with less than 10 years of history fills in the missing years. It uses the returns of its underlying fund (for a clone fund) or of another fund with the same manager, portfolio manager, objectives and strategies; failing that, it uses a reference index that "reasonably approximates" its standard deviation. A series with a different attribute, such as currency hedging, uses its own returns. The manager may raise the level if that is "reasonable in the circumstances", and must keep records of how the level was set.

> **Exam trap:** The rating measures past **volatility**, not the chance of loss. Fund Facts must say it "doesn't tell you how volatile the fund will be in the future" and that a low-rated fund "can still lose money".

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 15.1.1, Appendix F Items 1–5; Form 81-101F3 Part I Item 4*

## 5.5 ETFs

**Access.** ETFs are bought and sold on an exchange through any brokerage account during trading hours. A dealer acting as agent for the purchaser must deliver the **ETF Facts** (unless it has previously done so) "not later than midnight on the second business day after" the purchase. The obligation to deliver a prospectus does not apply to ETFs.

**Creation and redemption.** Authorized participants (designated brokers) deliver a basket of securities to the ETF for new units (*creation*), or return units for the basket (*redemption*). When the price rises above NAV they create and sell units; when it falls below, they buy and redeem. This arbitrage keeps the price near NAV, and in-kind redemptions mean fewer taxable sales inside the fund.

**Pricing.** "ETFs have two sets of prices: market price and net asset value (NAV)." Market price moves intraday; NAV is calculated after each close. A smaller **bid–ask spread** generally means a more liquid ETF; prices can be more volatile near the open and close.

**Management styles:** mostly passive indexing, plus active, smart-beta, leveraged and inverse ETFs (5.6).

**Leverage.** A conventional fund may borrow only temporarily, up to **5% of NAV**. An alternative mutual fund may borrow up to **50% of NAV**, with total exposure to borrowing, short sales and specified derivatives capped at **300% of NAV**.

| | ETF | Conventional mutual fund |
|---|---|---|
| Trading | Intraday, on an exchange | Once a day, at the next NAV |
| Costs | Commissions, bid–ask spread, usually a lower MER | Any sales charge; trailer inside the MER |
| Tax | Fewer capital gains distributions | Redemptions may force taxable sales |
| Disclosure | ETF Facts, within 2 business days after the trade | Fund Facts, normally before the trade |

**Cost structures.** An investor "may have to pay a commission every time you buy and sell". The ETF itself has an MER and a TER, and some ETFs pay a trailing commission.

*Sources: NI 41-101 s. 3C.2; Form 41-101F4 Part I Items 2 and 6, Part II Item 1; NI 81-102 s. 2.6(1)(a)(i), 2.6(2)(c), 2.9.1(1)*

## 5.6 Fund management styles

| Style | Feature | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** | Manager selects securities or timing to beat a benchmark | Alpha, at higher cost and risk of lagging |
| **Passive** | Holds an index's securities in index proportions, or replicates its performance | Market return; low cost, turnover and tax |
| **Smart beta / factor** | Rules-based index weighted by factors (value, size, momentum, low volatility, quality, dividend) instead of market cap | Transparent factor premiums at mid-range cost; can lag for years |
| **Leveraged** | Seeks a **multiple** (e.g. 2×) of an index's daily return, via swaps and futures | Short-term tactical exposure |
| **Inverse** | Seeks the **opposite** (or a multiple of it) of the daily return | Short-term hedge or bet on a decline |

Smart-beta products track factor indices (4.9); the factor models behind them are in 6.7.

Most leveraged and inverse ETFs **reset daily**. Because daily returns compound, longer-period returns can differ sharply from the stated multiple; a 2× fund can fall in a year its index rises. CIRO guidance says they "typically are unsuitable for retail investors who plan to hold them for longer than one trading session". Dealers must understand the product, make a client-specific suitability determination, keep sales materials fair (never implying long-term tracking), and supervise and train staff.

> **Exam trap:** A −1× inverse ETF held for a month will not return exactly minus the index's monthly return. Its objective is a *daily* one.

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 1.1 ("index mutual fund"); CIRO GN-3400-21-001; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.7 Information sources: Fund Facts and ETF Facts

**Fund Facts (Form 81-101F3).** A Fund Facts document is filed "for each class or series" of a mutual fund. Unless it has previously done so, the dealer must deliver the latest one **before accepting the purchase instruction**. Exception (all conditions must apply): the client instructs that the purchase "must be completed immediately or by a specified time"; pre-sale delivery is not reasonably practicable; and, before accepting the order, the dealer explains the Fund Facts, gets the client's consent for that specific purchase (no blanket consent) and gives a verbal summary of the key sections. The dealer may then deliver it "not later than midnight on the second business day after entering into the purchase". For purchases after the first under a pre-authorized purchase or rebalancing plan, the dealer need not deliver it once it has sent the required notice, reminds the client in writing at least annually how to request it, and sends it on request. Part I and Part II are one page each (four pages at most). It covers quick facts (including the MER), what the fund invests in, how risky it is, past performance (up to 10 calendar years), who the fund is for, tax, costs (sales charges, fund expenses, trailer, other fees) and the investor's rights.

> **Key rule:** "Under securities law in some provinces and territories", investors may "withdraw from an agreement to buy mutual funds within two business days after you receive a simplified prospectus or Fund Facts document", or "cancel your purchase within 48 hours after you receive confirmation of the purchase." (Form 81-101F3 Part II Item 2)

**ETF Facts (Form 41-101F4).** Prepared for each class and series in plain language. It must "not exceed four pages in length" and must not incorporate anything by reference. It adds trading and pricing data: ticker, exchange, average daily volume, market price, NAV and average bid–ask spread. It is posted on the ETF's website within 10 days of filing.

**Other sources.** The **simplified prospectus** incorporates by reference the Fund Facts, financial statements and management reports of fund performance (MRFPs); the fund must send any of these "within three business days of receipt of the request and free of charge". NAV per unit must be made public "at no cost". Fund websites, the financial press and independent rating services complete the picture.

**Other managed products.** Listed closed-end funds, as investment funds, file annual financial statements under NI 81-106. REITs and income trusts are reporting issuers that file annual financial statements and MD&A under NI 51-102. Pooled and hedge funds sold under prospectus exemptions have no prospectus or Fund Facts; investors rely on the offering memorandum or other offering documents and the manager's reports.

> **Remember:** Fund Facts: normally **before** the trade (client-requested immediate purchase: within 2 business days after). ETF Facts: within **two business days after**. Fund Facts is normally 2 pages (4 at most); ETF Facts at most 4.

*Sources: NI 81-101 s. 2.1, 3.1, 3.2.01, 3.2.02, 3.2.03, 3.3(3); Form 81-101F3 General Instructions (14), Part I Items 2–5, 7–8, Part II Items 1–2; Form 41-101F4 Part I Item 2; NI 41-101 s. 3B.2, 3B.4, 3C.2; NI 81-102 s. 1.1 ("mutual fund rating entity"); NI 81-106 s. 1.1, 2.1(1), 14.2(6.1); NI 51-102 s. 4.1(1), 5.1(1)*

## 5.8 Evaluating fund performance

**Holding period return (HPR).** The total gain over the period, including income:

```
HPR = (Ending value − Beginning value + Income) ÷ Beginning value
```

For example, $10,000 that grows to $10,500 and pays $200 of income gives (10,500 − 10,000 + 200) ÷ 10,000 = 7%. HPR is not annualized; it assumes no deposits or withdrawals during the period and ignores when the income was received.

**Money-weighted rate of return (MWRR).** The internal rate of return of the investor's own cash flows, so it depends on the **timing and size of deposits and withdrawals**. A client who adds money just before a decline has an MWRR below the fund's published return.

**Time-weighted rate of return (TWRR).** The period is split at each cash flow and the sub-period returns are chained:

```
TWRR = (1 + r₁)(1 + r₂)…(1 + rₙ) − 1
```

Here rᵢ is the return for sub-period i. Cash flows do not affect the result, so TWRR measures the **manager**, who does not control when investors buy or sell. Convention: TWRR is used for fund and manager performance; MWRR for the client's own account.

**Comparing returns.** Against a **benchmark**: an index matching the fund's asset class, style and region (5.9). Against a **peer group**: funds with similar objectives, often ranked by quartile (style drift and survivorship bias can distort it). Use the same periods, after fees, and weigh the risk taken.

> **Exam trap:** If a client's return differs from the fund's published return, a common cause is the timing of the client's cash flows (MWRR vs TWRR). Others are sales charges and account fees, which published returns exclude, and a different series or period. None of these reflects on the manager.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.9 Valuing funds and measuring their performance

**Pricing units.** NAV is "the value of the total assets of the investment fund less the value of the total" liabilities. It is measured at fair value and includes income and expenses accrued to the valuation date. Purchases and redemptions take place at the next-determined NAV per unit.

```
NAVPS = (Total assets − Total liabilities) ÷ Units outstanding
Units issued = Net amount invested ÷ NAVPS
Value of a holding = Units held × NAVPS
Total return = (Ending NAVPS − Beginning NAVPS + Distributions per unit) ÷ Beginning NAVPS
```

The total-return formula is for a single period, with distributions received in cash.

> **Worked example**
> Maple Balanced Fund has assets of $50,500,000, liabilities of $500,000 and 4,000,000 units outstanding.
> 1. NAVPS = (50,500,000 − 500,000) ÷ 4,000,000 = **$12.50**
> 2. Priya invests $10,000 and pays a 2% front-end charge of 0.02 × 10,000 = $200. Units issued = 9,800 ÷ 12.50 = **784 units** (with no charge: 10,000 ÷ 12.50 = 800 units).
> 3. One year later the NAVPS is $13.10, after $0.40 per unit of distributions paid in cash. Her holding is worth 784 × 13.10 = **$10,270.40**, and she has received 784 × 0.40 = **$313.60** of cash distributions.
> 4. Total return per unit = (13.10 − 12.50 + 0.40) ÷ 12.50 = 1.00 ÷ 12.50 = **8.0%**. Priya's own return = (10,270.40 + 313.60 − 10,000) ÷ 10,000 = **5.84%**, lower than 8.0% because of the $200 charge.

**Standard performance data.** Sales communications may show performance only after **12 consecutive months** of prospectus distribution, must reflect "all elements of return", and must include standard performance data. For funds other than money market funds, that means annual compounded total returns (distributions reinvested, before sales charges and taxes) for **10, 5, 3 and 1 years**, or since inception for funds 1–10 years old. A money market fund instead reports a current yield (and optionally an effective yield) for the most recent **seven-day** period. The warning ends "past performance may not be repeated". Advertisements may compare only with funds under common management, funds with similar objectives, or an index.

**Appropriate benchmarks.** A valid benchmark is:

- **specified in advance**, before the evaluation period;
- **appropriate** to the manager's style and mandate;
- **measurable**, with a return that can be calculated regularly;
- **unambiguous**, with clearly defined constituents and weights;
- **reflective** of the manager's current investment universe;
- **accountable**, accepted by the manager as the fair yardstick;
- **investable**, so the investor could hold it passively instead.

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 1.1 ("net asset value"), 9.3, 10.3, 15.4(6), 15.6, 15.7, 15.8, 15.10; NI 81-106 s. 14.2(1)–(1.1); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.10 Impact of costs on performance

**Loads and charges** (front-end, switch, short-term trading, fee-based account fees) reduce the amount invested or the return.

| Measure | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **MER** | Management fee (including any trailer) plus operating expenses, as a % of average NAV |
| **TER** | The fund's trading costs (commissions and other portfolio transaction costs), which are **excluded** from the MER |
| **Fund expenses** | MER + TER: the total ongoing cost. Fund Facts states it as a % and as $ per $1,000 invested |

Investors do not pay these directly, but "they reduce the fund's returns" (published returns are net of them). A 2.0% MER costs $20 a year per $1,000 invested.

**Turnover.** High portfolio turnover means more trading. That raises the TER and spread costs, and it realizes more capital gains, which are distributed as taxable income in non-registered accounts.

**Taxes.** *On the fund:* a mutual fund trust normally distributes its net income and gains, so the investor, not the fund, pays the tax. *On the investor:* in a non-registered account, distributions (T3 or T5 slips) are taxable "whether you get them in cash or have them reinvested", and redemptions can realize gains. *Withholding tax:* foreign countries withhold tax on dividends and interest; under the Canada–U.S. treaty, U.S. tax on dividends to a Canadian resident generally may not exceed **15%**.

| Withholding tax | When it applies |
|---|---|
| **Recoverable** | Non-registered account: the foreign income is "reported on your Canadian tax return", so the tax withheld can be claimed as a **foreign tax credit** (Form T2209). Fund trusts pass through foreign tax paid (T3 box 34) so investors can claim it |
| **Unrecoverable** | The income is not reported on a Canadian return, so there is nothing to claim against: e.g. in a TFSA, where income is "generally tax-free", or another registered plan. Also tax withheld inside a fund when the fund is held in an RRSP, TFSA or other registered plan (e.g. a Canadian fund or ETF holding U.S. stocks), and tax withheld inside a foreign-listed fund on its own foreign holdings (e.g. a U.S.-listed ETF holding international stocks), which is not passed through to the investor. A permanent drag on return |

The treaty exempts dividends and interest earned by an arrangement "operated exclusively to administer or provide pension, retirement or employee benefits". RRSPs and RRIFs are generally treated as qualifying, so U.S. securities held directly in them (including U.S.-listed ETFs) are usually exempt from U.S. withholding.

*Sources: Form 81-101F3 Part I Items 5, 8, Part II Item 1.3; NI 81-106 s. 1.1, 15.1; CRA "Tax treatment of mutual funds"; CRA "Foreign tax credit"; CRA "Tax-free Savings Account"; Canada–U.S. Tax Convention Art. X(2), Art. XXI(2)*

## 5.11 Implications of redemptions

**Tax consequences.** A redemption is a disposition: proceeds minus ACB minus selling costs gives the capital gain or loss. CRA treats units or shares in the same class or series of a fund as identical properties, so the ACB is the **average cost per unit** (ACB mechanics: 7.10).

A **T-SWP** (tax-efficient systematic withdrawal plan, through T-class or T-series units) pays monthly amounts that are largely **return of capital (ROC)**. ROC is not taxed when received, but it **reduces the ACB**, which defers tax until the units are sold. If the ACB falls below zero, "the negative amount is deemed to be a capital gain in the year" and the ACB resets to zero.

**Withdrawal plans** pay a **fixed dollar** amount (e.g. $500 a month), pay out the holding over a **fixed period**, or pay a **ratio** (a set percentage of value each year). Payouts above the fund's return erode capital, selling in a falling market locks in losses, and in a non-registered account each redemption can trigger a gain or loss.

**Suspension of redemptions (gating).** A fund may suspend redemptions only while:

- normal trading is suspended on an exchange holding **more than 50% of the fund's assets** by value, and no practical alternative market exists; or
- for a clone fund, the fund it tracks has suspended redemptions.

Other suspensions need regulatory approval. During a suspension the fund may postpone payment and must not accept purchases. **Payment timing:** conventional mutual funds pay within **two business days** of the NAV date; alternative mutual funds and non-redeemable funds within **15 business days**. Hedge and pooled funds set their own gates, lock-ups and notice periods in their offering documents.

> **Exam trap:** ROC is not tax-free income. It defers tax by lowering the ACB, and the deferred amount is taxed as a capital gain when the units are sold. Once the ACB reaches zero, any further ROC is deemed a capital gain in the year it is received.

*Sources: CRA "Tax treatment of mutual funds"; NI 81-102 s. 10.4(1)–(1.2), 10.6; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.12 Managed vs non-managed products

**The investor's decisions** turn on expertise and time, portfolio size (a small account cannot diversify cheaply with individual securities), the wish for control (direct holders choose trades and when to realize gains), cost (commissions with no MER versus ongoing fund fees), transparency, values, and income or growth needs.

**The Registered Representative's recommendation** must meet the IDPC 3402(1) suitability standard, which **puts the client's interest first** (factors: 1.17). For this choice, the cost and reasonable-alternatives factors usually matter most. In practice, the RR weighs a fund against an ETF, direct securities or a managed account: small or growing accounts, delegation and specialized exposure favour managed products; large accounts and a wish for control or tax timing favour direct holdings.

If the client insists on an unsuitable choice, the IDPC 3402(5) steps apply (1.18).

*Sources: IDPC 3402(1), 3402(5); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.13 Alternative strategy funds and alternative assets

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Hedge fund** | Private pool using leverage, short sales and derivatives (long/short, market-neutral, macro, event-driven, arbitrage) for absolute return; sold under exemptions |
| **Structured product** | Note or deposit whose payoff is linked to an index or reference asset, e.g. principal-protected notes (PPNs) |
| **Alternative investment fund** | Public **alternative mutual fund** ("liquid alt"): may invest in physical commodities, crypto assets or specified derivatives, or "borrow cash or engage in short selling in a manner not permitted for other mutual funds" |
| **Crypto-assets** | "any digital representation of value that uses cryptography and distributed ledger technology" (CSA view); held directly or via funds |
| **Private equity** | Funds buying or investing in private companies; multi-year lock-ups |
| **Venture capital** | Early-stage start-up financing: many failures, a few large winners |

NI 81-102 sets no naming rule, but the CSA would generally expect only alternative mutual funds to use "alternative" in their name. A **PPN** (Bank Act) pays amounts linked to "an index or reference point" and repays at least the amount paid; before issuance the institution must disclose its risks, "including, if applicable, the risk that no interest may accrue", and any lack of CDIC coverage.

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 1.1 ("alternative mutual fund"); 81-102CP s. 2.01; Bank Act s. 627.01, 627.78; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 5.14 Characteristics of alternative funds and other investments

**Structure and features.** Hedge, private equity and venture funds are usually **limited partnerships** (general partner manages; investors are limited partners) or trusts; liquid alts are prospectus mutual funds; structured notes are the issuer's debt. Features: leverage, short positions, derivatives, illiquid holdings, lock-ups, capital calls and limited transparency.

**Fees.** A **management fee** (e.g. 1–2% of assets) plus a **performance fee** (e.g. 20% of profits). A **hurdle rate** is the minimum return before any performance fee is paid; a **high-water mark** means no fee until earlier losses are recovered. Under NI 81-102, a conventional mutual fund's performance fee must be measured against a **total-return benchmark or index** reflecting its sectors, cumulatively since the last fee was paid. An alternative mutual fund's fee is based on its own **cumulative total return** since the last fee. Either way, the method must be in the prospectus.

| | Investor | Provider |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Low correlation, absolute-return potential, access to private markets | Two-layer fees; locked-in capital; flexible mandates |
| **Disadvantages** | High fees, illiquidity, leverage, complexity, limited disclosure | Complex operations and compliance; redemption pressure in a crisis |

**Risks and returns.** Returns come from manager skill, leverage, and factor and illiquidity premiums. Risks: leverage, counterparty, liquidity, valuation, issuer credit (notes) and extreme volatility (crypto). By contrast, a mutual fund (liquid alts included) may not buy illiquid assets above **10% of NAV** or hold over **15%** for 90 days or more.

**Costs of acquiring and holding:** commissions and wide spreads, structuring fees embedded in notes, custody and storage (commodities, crypto), early-redemption penalties, and the drag of committed but uncalled capital.

**Accredited investor requirement.** Most hedge, private equity and pooled funds are sold without a prospectus to **accredited investors** buying as principal. The individual tests, the Form 45-106F9 acknowledgement and the financial-assets-versus-net-assets trap are in 3.1. A ticked box is not enough: the seller must take reasonable steps to verify status.

*Sources: NI 81-102 s. 2.4, 7.1; NI 45-106 s. 2.3; 45-106CP s. 1.9(3); standard finance concept (verified)*
