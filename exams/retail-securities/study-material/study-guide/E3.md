# Element 3: Equities

This element covers how shares reach the market (prospectus and exemptions), the rights attached to common shares, preferred shares and CDRs, what each means for investor and issuer, corporate actions, and equity valuation. It carries about **12 of the 120 questions**: mostly scenario questions (which security suits this client or issuer?) plus short valuation calculations done step by step on the basic/scientific calculator.

## 3.1 Prospectus requirement and exemptions

**The requirement.** Securities legislation bars a distribution without a receipted prospectus unless an exemption applies. In Ontario, for example:

> **Key rule:** No one may trade "if the trade would be a distribution of the security, unless a preliminary prospectus and a prospectus have been filed and receipts have been issued for them by the Director." (Securities Act (Ontario) s. 53(1))

**Purpose: comprehensive disclosure.** NI 41-101 is the primary prospectus instrument. A prospectus must give "full, true and plain disclosure of all material facts relating to the securities being distributed"; statutory liability gives investors a remedy if it does not (41-101CP s. 2.6).

**Primary and secondary distributions.** A "distribution" includes a trade in securities "that have not been previously issued" (a **primary**, treasury offering) and a trade "from the holdings of any control person" (a **secondary** offering of a control block). Ordinary exchange trading is neither.

**Advertising and marketing.** Marketing meant to promote a distribution is "conduct directly or indirectly in furtherance" of a trade, so it is caught (41-101CP s. 6.2). In the **waiting period** (preliminary to final receipt), communications carry a legend that no sale will occur until the final receipt. Standard term sheets and marketing materials follow their own rules instead: their content must be disclosed in, or derived from, the preliminary prospectus (NI 41-101 ss. 13.1(3), 13.5, 13.7).

**Timely disclosure.** Reporting issuers must also make timely disclosure of material changes (NI 51-102 s. 7.1; see 4.1). Bad news goes out as promptly as good news (NP 51-201).

**Exempt market and private placements.** **Exempt-market securities** are sold under a prospectus exemption. Some exemptions depend on the security, which then trades freely: debt "issued by or guaranteed by the Government of Canada or the government of a jurisdiction of Canada" (NI 45-106 s. 2.34; similar exemption in Ontario's Act) or commercial paper maturing within one year that carries a prescribed high short-term rating (at or above R-1(low) from DBRS or the listed equivalent) and is not a securitized product (s. 2.35). Others depend on the buyer: these **private placements** give less disclosure and carry resale restrictions.

| Exemption | Key condition |
|---|---|
| Accredited investor (s. 2.3; s. 73.3 of the Ontario Act in Ontario) | Purchaser buys as principal and is an accredited investor |
| Private issuer (s. 2.4) | Not a reporting issuer or investment fund; transfer restrictions; ≤ 50 beneficial owners (excluding employees and former employees). Buyer must be in a listed category: directors, officers, employees, founders or control persons; family, close personal friends and close business associates of a director, executive officer, founder or control person; existing holders; accredited investors; or "a person that is not the public" |
| Offering memorandum (s. 2.9) | OM plus signed risk acknowledgement before purchase; conditions vary by province |
| Minimum amount (s. 2.10) | Purchaser is **not an individual** and pays at least $150,000 in cash |

**Accredited investor: individual tests (NI 45-106 s. 1.1)**

| Test | Threshold | Spouse combined? |
|---|---|---|
| (j) Financial assets, net of related liabilities | exceeds $1,000,000 | Yes |
| (j.1) Financial assets, net of related liabilities | exceeds $5,000,000 | No (individual alone) |
| (k) Pre-tax net income, each of the last 2 years and expected this year | exceeds $200,000 ($300,000 with spouse) | Yes |
| (l) Net assets | at least $5,000,000 | Yes |

Individuals under (j), (k) or (l) sign a Form 45-106F9 risk acknowledgement, kept for 8 years. Whoever relies on an exemption must confirm its conditions are met.

> **Exam trap:** Financial assets (cash, securities, insurance contracts, deposits) **exclude** the home; net assets **include** the home and its mortgage. A $900,000 portfolio plus a debt-free $3M house fails (j), and fails (l) too ($3.9M is below $5M).

**Resale.** Accredited-investor purchases face a **restricted period**. Among other conditions (including a legend or legend notice, and no control distribution), the issuer must have been a reporting issuer for the four months before the trade and "at least four months have elapsed from the distribution date" (NI 45-102 s. 2.5).

**Application to takeovers.** A take-over bid is an offer that, with the offeror's securities, reaches 20% or more of a class (NI 62-104). Shares issued as bid consideration need no prospectus: "The prospectus requirement does not apply to a distribution of a security in connection with a take-over bid" (NI 45-106 s. 2.16).

*Sources: Securities Act (Ontario) ss. 1(1), 53(1), 73.3; NI 41-101 ss. 1.1, 13.1, 13.5, 13.7; 41-101CP ss. 1.2, 2.6, 6.2; NI 45-106 ss. 1.1, 2.3, 2.4, 2.9, 2.10, 2.16, 2.34, 2.35; 45-106CP ss. 1.9, 3.5; NI 45-102 s. 2.5; NI 51-102 s. 7.1; NP 51-201 s. 2.1; NI 62-104 s. 1.1*

## 3.2 Classes of common shares

The articles set out share classes. For a federal corporation, three rights must exist: to vote, to declared dividends, and to remaining property on dissolution. "If there are more than one class of shares, each of the three rights have to be assigned to at least one class of shares, but one class does not need to have all three" (Corporations Canada). Each share carries one vote unless the articles say otherwise.

| Class | Voting rights | Dividend rights | Rights on dissolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary common | One per share | Pro rata, when declared | Residual, after creditors and preferred |
| Multiple-voting | Several per share (often founders) | Usually equal | Usually equal |
| Subordinate-voting | Fewer per share than another class | Usually equal | Usually equal |
| Restricted-voting | Capped (limit on how many may be voted or owned) | Usually equal | Usually equal |
| Non-voting | None, except certain fundamental issues | Equal or higher | Usually equal |

**Restricted shares.** A "restricted security" is an equity security, other than a preferred, where another class "carries a greater number of votes per security", where its voting rights are nullified or significantly restricted, or where another class shares more in earnings or assets per security (NI 41-101 s. 1.1). Subordinate-voting, restricted-voting and non-voting classes are therefore restricted shares; the multiple-voting class is not. **Coattail** provisions let the inferior class convert or join a bid made only for the superior-voting class.

> **Exam trap:** Lower voting power does not mean lower economic rights; subordinate-voting shares often share equally in dividends and liquidation. The difference is control.

*Sources: Corporations Canada (ISED), "Share structure and shareholders"; NI 41-101 s. 1.1; otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.3 Canadian depositary receipts (CDRs)

A **CDR** is a Canadian-dollar, Canadian-listed security representing a fraction of a share of a (usually U.S.) company held by a Canadian financial institution acting as depositary.

**Rights of the holder.** Economic exposure to the share (price changes and dividends, paid in CAD). Voting is exercised through the depositary (holders typically give it voting instructions); exact rights depend on the CDR's terms. Most CDRs carry a **notional currency hedge**: the CDR's C$ value moves roughly with the share's home-currency return, largely neutralizing USD/CAD moves, with the hedge cost built in.

**CDR ratio.** How much of one share each CDR represents, set so a CDR trades at an affordable price. The hedge works through ongoing adjustment of this ratio as the exchange rate moves, so the ratio is not fixed. Simplified illustration: at 20 CDRs per share, each CDR represents 1/20 of a share, so a US$300 share with the hedge locking in about 1.40 CAD/USD gives roughly C$21 per CDR.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.4 Investing in common shares and depositary receipts

**Return** = dividends + capital gain (or loss); neither is promised.

**Sources of risk**

| Risk | Common shares | Depositary receipts (add-ons) |
|---|---|---|
| Business/earnings | Profits fall, so dividends and price fall | Same as the underlying company |
| Financial (leverage) | Heavy debt magnifies swings in earnings | Same |
| Market (systematic) | Broad sell-offs hit most stocks | Same |
| Liquidity | Thin trading means wide spreads | DR volume may be below the underlying's |
| Control | Restricted shares give little say | Depend on the depositary's terms |
| Currency | None for Canadian shares | Unhedged DRs carry FX risk; hedged CDRs mostly remove it |

**Costs:** commissions, bid-ask spreads, account/custody fees, and currency-conversion spreads when buying foreign shares directly (CDRs avoid these but embed a hedge cost). Flat fees bite hardest on small trades.

> **Worked example**
> Mia buys 500 shares at $20 and pays a $25 commission. She receives $0.40 per share in dividends and sells at $22, paying another $25.
> ```
> Cost = 10,000 + 25 = 10,025;  proceeds = 11,000 − 25 = 10,975;  dividends = 200
> Return = (10,975 + 200 − 10,025) ÷ 10,025 = 1,150 ÷ 10,025 = 11.47%
> ```
> Without fees: 1,200 ÷ 10,000 = 12.00%. The two $25 fees cost about 0.53 percentage points.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.5 Common share ownership: advantages and disadvantages

**For the owner**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Unlimited upside: shares growth in profits and value | Last claim on earnings and assets; can lose the whole investment |
| Dividends from taxable Canadian corporations may qualify for the dividend tax credit (3.6) | Dividends are discretionary and can be cut |
| Votes on directors and fundamental changes (voting classes) | Price volatility |
| Liquidity if listed | Restricted classes carry little or no control |
| Limited liability: "limited to the amount they paid for their shares" (Corporations Canada) | |

**For the issuer**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Permanent capital, never repaid | Dilutes existing owners' control and earnings per share (EPS) |
| No obligation to pay dividends | Equity costs more than debt, because investors demand higher returns |
| Strengthens the balance sheet (lower debt-to-equity) | Dividends come out of after-tax profit, whereas interest is generally a deductible expense |
| | Prospectus, underwriting and continuous-disclosure costs (3.1) |

*Sources: Corporations Canada (ISED), "Share structure and shareholders"; otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.6 Corporate actions

**Declaring and claiming dividends.** The board **declares** a dividend, setting a **record date** and **payment date**; holders of record are paid. "Current SRO and marketplace rules mandate a standard T+1 settlement cycle period for most transactions in equity and long term debt securities" (CP 24-101). So a buyer must trade at least one business day before the record date, and the **ex-dividend date** (first day trading without the dividend, when the price typically drops by about the dividend) normally equals the record date. Street-name clients are credited through their dealer; DRIPs reinvest dividends in more shares.

**Taxation.** Dividends from taxable Canadian corporations are grossed up into income, then offset by a dividend tax credit (DTC). "Eligible" dividends are those the payer designates as eligible.

| Federal treatment (ITA ss. 82, 121) | Eligible | Other than eligible |
|---|---|---|
| Gross-up | 38%, so 138% is taxable | 15%, so 115% is taxable |
| Federal dividend tax credit | 6/11 of the gross-up | 9/13 of the gross-up |

These ITA rates apply for years ending after 2011 (eligible) and after 2018 (other); CRA's 2025 pages use 138%/115%. E.g. $100 eligible → $138 taxable, federal DTC 6/11 × $38 = $20.73. Provinces add their own credits.

> **Exam trap:** Foreign dividends "do not qualify for this credit." Dividends on U.S. shares also get no gross-up, which applies only to dividends received "from corporations resident in Canada" (ITA s. 82(1)).

**Splits and consolidations.** A split multiplies shares and divides the price proportionally; value and percentage ownership are unchanged. Used to bring a high price into a popular trading range. A **consolidation** (reverse split) does the opposite, lifting a low price.

For example, after a 3-for-1 split, 300 shares at $90 become 900 shares at $30; after a 1-for-5 consolidation, 1,000 shares at $0.80 become 200 shares at $4.00. The holding is worth the same before and after.

**Share buybacks.** An issuer repurchasing its shares makes an **issuer bid** (NI 62-104). A normal-course issuer bid through a designated exchange (TSX, TSX Venture, Cboe Canada) is exempt from the formal-bid rules "if the bid is made in accordance with the bylaws, rules, regulations and policies of that exchange" (s. 4.8(1)–(2)). Buybacks return cash, reduce the share count, which tends to raise EPS (if earnings hold up), and may signal management thinks the shares are undervalued.

*Sources: ITA ss. 82(1), 89(1), 121; CRA, Lines 12000/12010 and Line 40425 (2025); CRA, "Eligible dividends"; CP 24-101 s. 5.1; NI 62-104 ss. 1.1, 4.8*

## 3.7 Classes of preferred shares

Preferred shares rank ahead of common for dividends and on dissolution, behind all debt, usually with a fixed dividend.

| Feature | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Cumulative** | Missed dividends accumulate as arrears, which must be paid before any common dividend |
| **Non-cumulative** | A skipped dividend is lost for good |
| **Redeemable / callable** | Issuer may buy back at a set price (often par plus a premium) |
| **Retractable** | Holder may sell back to the issuer at a set price on set dates |
| **Convertible** | Holder may exchange for common shares at a set ratio |
| **Participating** | Extra dividends beyond the fixed rate, alongside common |
| **Floating / adjustable-rate** | The dividend moves with a reference interest rate |
| **Fixed-reset** | Rate resets periodically (commonly every 5 years) to a Government of Canada yield plus a fixed spread; holder can usually switch to a floating series |
| **Perpetual (straight)** | No maturity or retraction date |

**Voting rights:** usually none, often arising only after a set number of unpaid dividends. **Rights on dissolution:** generally a stated amount (often par, e.g. $25) plus any unpaid cumulative dividends (arrears), ahead of common but with no share in any surplus.

> **Remember:** Cumulative protects the **dividend**, retractable protects the **price** (a put for the holder), and callable caps the **price** (a call for the issuer).

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.8 Investing in preferred shares

**Return** comes mainly from the fixed dividend, which qualifies for the dividend tax credit (the enhanced rate if designated eligible; see 3.6 and 7.11); price gains are limited unless convertible.

**Sources of risk**

| Risk | Most exposed |
|---|---|
| **Interest-rate risk:** when rates rise, fixed-dividend prices fall | Straight perpetuals (no maturity to anchor the price) |
| **Credit / dividend risk:** dividends can be suspended without a legal default | Non-cumulative issues and weaker issuers |
| **Call risk:** shares are redeemed when rates fall, capping gains | Callable issues trading above the call price |
| **Reset risk:** the new rate may be lower if Government of Canada yields fall | Fixed-reset issues |
| **Liquidity risk:** small issues trade thinly with wide spreads | Most preferreds compared with common shares |
| **Inflation risk:** a fixed dividend loses purchasing power | Fixed-rate issues |

Retractable and floating-rate issues carry the least interest-rate risk.

**Costs.** Commissions and especially **bid-ask spreads** are large relative to a modest expected return; on a thin issue the spread can absorb much of a year's dividend. Use limit orders; avoid frequent trading.

> **Exam trap:** A preferred dividend is not interest. Missing one is not a default, and arrears on cumulative shares are only a priority claim ahead of the common shareholders.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.9 Common versus preferred ownership in practice

| | Owner of preferred shares | Issuer of preferred shares |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Priority over common for dividends and assets; steady, often tax-advantaged income; cumulative and retractable features add protection | Raises equity without diluting voting control; skipping a dividend is not a default; improves debt-to-equity compared with borrowing |
| **Disadvantages** | Little or no capital growth; usually no vote; ranks behind all creditors; interest-rate and call risk | Dividends come out of after-tax profit, so preferreds cost more than debt; arrears block common dividends; fixed dividends reduce flexibility |

**Applying it**

- *Retiree wanting steady, tax-efficient income, moderate risk:* preferreds (retractable or floating-rate if rates may rise; fixed-reset reprices only at each reset).
- *Young investor seeking growth:* common shares.
- *Issuer whose founders want control without more debt:* preferreds rather than new common or bonds.
- *Investor wanting priority income plus some upside:* convertible preferreds.

> **Exam trap:** A preferred is legally equity but behaves like fixed income: its price follows interest rates more than company growth.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.10 Valuing equities with time value of money

A share is worth the **present value of its expected cash flows**, discounted at the required return (PV mechanics: 2.9).

**Discounted cash flow (DCF)**

```
P₀ = D₁ ÷ (1 + r) + D₂ ÷ (1 + r)^2 + … + (Dₙ + Pₙ) ÷ (1 + r)^n
P₀ = value today;  Dₜ = dividend in year t;  Pₙ = expected selling price in year n;  r = required return
n = number of years the share is held; dividends and the sale price are assumed received at year-end, with annual discounting
```

> **Worked example**
> Sam expects dividends of $1.00 and $1.10, then to sell at $30 at the end of year 2. He requires 10%.
> ```
> P₀ = 1.00 ÷ 1.10 + 1.10 ÷ 1.10^2 + 30 ÷ 1.10^2
>    = 0.91 + 0.91 + 24.79 = $26.61
> ```

**Dividend discount models**

```
Zero growth (e.g. a straight preferred):  P₀ = D ÷ r
Constant growth (Gordon):                 P₀ = D₁ ÷ (r − g),  where D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g) and r > g
D = constant annual dividend, paid forever (a perpetuity);  D₀ = dividend just paid;  g = constant annual dividend growth rate
```

> **Worked example**
> A preferred share pays $1.25 a year and investors require 5%: P₀ = 1.25 ÷ 0.05 = **$25.00**.
> A common share just paid $2.00, with dividends growing 4% a year and a required return of 9%:
> ```
> D₁ = 2.00 × 1.04 = 2.08;   P₀ = 2.08 ÷ (0.09 − 0.04) = 2.08 ÷ 0.05 = $41.60
> ```

**Price–earnings growth models.** Value = expected EPS × an appropriate P/E. The constant-growth model gives the "justified" forward P/E:

```
P₀ ÷ E₁ = payout ratio ÷ (r − g)      →      P₀ = E₁ × P/E
E₁ = next year's EPS;  payout ratio = D₁ ÷ E₁
```

> **Worked example**
> Next year's EPS is forecast at $3.00, the payout ratio is 40%, r = 10% and g = 6%.
> ```
> Justified P/E = 0.40 ÷ (0.10 − 0.06) = 10;   P₀ = 3.00 × 10 = $30.00
> Check: D₁ = 0.40 × 3.00 = 1.20;   1.20 ÷ 0.04 = $30.00
> ```

Higher growth or a lower required return justifies a higher P/E. The PEG ratio (P/E ÷ expected EPS growth in %) also relates P/E to growth.

> **Exam trap:** Gordon uses **next** year's dividend (D₁), not D₀, and fails when g ≥ r. Market price below model value → the share looks undervalued.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*
