# Element 3: Equities

This element covers these equity topics:

- the prospectus and its exemptions;
- the rights and risks of common shares, preferred shares and CDRs;
- corporate actions;
- valuation.

It carries about **12 of the 120 questions**. Most are scenarios: which security suits this client or this issuer? Some are short valuation calculations (3.10) on the basic/scientific calculator.

## 3.1 Prospectus requirement and exemptions

A **prospectus** is the disclosure document for a distribution of securities. NI 41-101 gives the general prospectus requirements. NI 45-106 gives the exemptions. The exam tests their purpose, their general requirements and the result of a breach.

### The requirement

Unless an exemption applies, securities legislation prohibits a distribution without a receipted prospectus. Each province's Securities Act sets this requirement, for example in Ontario:

> **Key rule:** No one may trade "if the trade would be a distribution of the security, unless a preliminary prospectus and a prospectus have been filed and receipts have been issued for them by the Director." (Securities Act (Ontario) s. 53(1))

The **waiting period** runs from the receipt for the preliminary prospectus to the receipt for the final prospectus (NI 41-101 s. 1.1). So no sale can occur until the final receipt (s. 53(1); NI 41-101 s. 13.1(1)). The regulator can refuse a receipt "where it is not in the public interest to issue the receipt" (41-101CP s. 2.6(1)).

### Application to primary and secondary distributions

A **distribution** includes two main kinds of trade (Securities Act (Ontario) s. 1(1)):

- A **primary distribution** (treasury offering) is a trade in securities "that have not been previously issued". The issuer receives the money.
- A **secondary distribution** is a trade in previously issued securities "from the holdings of any control person". The control person receives the money.

Both kinds need a prospectus or an exemption. Ordinary exchange trading between investors is neither kind. One way to understand the control-person limb: a control block can reach the public in the same way as a new issue.

### Comprehensive disclosure

The purpose of a prospectus is to give investors all the important facts before they decide. Securities law requires that the final prospectus "contain full, true and plain disclosure of all material facts" (41-101CP s. 6.12(7)). A **material fact** is "a fact that would reasonably be expected to have a significant effect on the market price or value of the securities" (Securities Act (Ontario) s. 1(1)).

A **misrepresentation** is "an untrue statement of material fact". It also covers a failure to state a material fact in two cases (s. 1(1)):

- the law requires the issuer to state the fact;
- without the fact, a statement is misleading in its context.

Statutory liability gives investors "a remedy" if a prospectus does not contain full, true and plain disclosure. It also helps "to protect the integrity of the Canadian public markets" (41-101CP s. 2.6(1)).

### Advertising and marketing

The prospectus requirement also controls how issuers and dealers promote an offering. The regulators have concerns about "equal access to information, conditioning of the market, tipping and insider trading" (41-101CP s. 6.2(9)). Conditioning of the market means that promotion creates demand before investors have the prospectus.

A "trade" includes any "advertisement" or other act "in furtherance" of a sale (Securities Act (Ontario) s. 1(1)). So marketing that promotes a distribution is a trade (41-101CP s. 6.2(5)). Without a receipt or an exemption, it breaches the prospectus requirement (s. 6.3(1)). If an activity does not promote a distribution, the prospectus requirement generally does not apply to it (s. 6.3(2)). Examples are:

- product advertising;
- regular factual news about the business;
- filings that the law requires.

| Stage | What the rules allow (NI 41-101 Part 13) |
|---|---|
| Before the preliminary prospectus | Generally no marketing in furtherance of the distribution. Narrow exceptions exist, e.g. testing of the waters for IPO issuers (s. 13.4) and bought-deal pre-marketing (41-101CP s. 6.4) |
| Waiting period | Communications carry a legend: no sale until the final receipt (s. 13.1). Standard term sheets and marketing materials follow their own rules (ss. 13.5, 13.7) |
| After the final receipt | Communications carry a legend that the offering "is only made by prospectus" (s. 13.2) |

### Timely disclosure requirements

After the offering, a reporting issuer must keep the market informed. The reason: "It is fundamental that everyone investing in securities have equal access to information that may affect their investment decisions" (NP 51-201 s. 1.1(1)).

If a material change occurs, the issuer must immediately issue and file a news release. It must also file a material change report as soon as practicable (NI 51-102 s. 7.1(1)). The latest date is 10 days after the change. For the definition of a material change and the confidential-filing exception, see 4.1.

### Exempt market securities

The **exempt market** is the market for securities sold under a prospectus exemption. Some exemptions depend on the security itself. Securities under these two exemptions are free trading (NI 45-106 notes to ss. 2.34, 2.35):

- debt "issued by or guaranteed by the Government of Canada or the government of a jurisdiction of Canada" (s. 2.34; Ontario's Act has a similar exemption);
- short-term (one year or less), highly rated commercial paper that meets the conditions of s. 2.35.

### Private placements

A **private placement** is a sale under a prospectus exemption. The buyer qualifies in one of these ways:

- who the buyer is (accredited investor, private issuer list);
- how much the buyer invests (minimum amount);
- what disclosure the buyer gets (offering memorandum).

The buyer gets less disclosure than under a prospectus. The securities carry resale restrictions.

| Exemption | Main conditions |
|---|---|
| Accredited investor (s. 2.3; s. 73.3 of the Ontario Act in Ontario) | Purchaser buys as principal and is an accredited investor |
| Private issuer (s. 2.4) | Issuer is a private issuer; buyer buys as principal and is in a listed category (see the lists below) |
| Offering memorandum (s. 2.9) | OM plus signed risk acknowledgement before purchase; conditions vary by province |
| Minimum amount (s. 2.10) | Purchaser is **not an individual**, buys as principal and pays at least $150,000 in cash at the time of the distribution, for securities of a single issuer. Not available to a person created or used only to use this exemption |

**Private issuer exemption (s. 2.4).** The issuer must meet all of these conditions:

- It is not a reporting issuer or an investment fund.
- Not counting non-convertible debt securities, its securities have restrictions on transfer. The issuer's constating documents or security holders' agreements contain these restrictions.
- Not counting non-convertible debt securities, no more than 50 persons beneficially own its securities. Employees and former employees of the issuer or its affiliates do not count.
- Each person counts as one beneficial owner. Exception: someone created or used the person only to buy or hold the issuer's securities. Then each beneficial owner or beneficiary of that person counts separately.
- It distributed its securities only to persons in the listed categories. Alternative: after a completed transaction, only those persons owned its securities, and later distributions went only to them.

The buyer must buy as principal and be in a listed category. Examples of listed categories:

- directors, officers, employees, founders or control persons of the issuer;
- a spouse, parent, grandparent, brother, sister, child or grandchild of a director, executive officer, founder or control person;
- close personal friends and close business associates of those persons;
- existing security holders;
- accredited investors;
- "a person that is not the public".

Except on a distribution to an accredited investor, no director, officer, founder or control person can get a commission or finder's fee (s. 2.4(3)).

**Resale.** A restricted period applies to first trades after these exemptions:

- accredited investor;
- offering memorandum;
- minimum amount.

A **seasoning period** applies to private issuer securities (NI 45-106 notes to ss. 2.3–2.10). Under the restricted period, a first trade "is a distribution" unless it meets all the conditions in NI 45-102 s. 2.5(2):

- The issuer has been a reporting issuer for the four months immediately before the trade.
- "At least four months have elapsed from the distribution date."
- In most cases, the certificate carries a legend, or the purchaser gets written notice of it.
- The trade is not a control distribution.
- No unusual effort is made to prepare the market or create demand.
- No extraordinary commission or consideration is paid for the trade.
- If the seller is an insider or officer, the seller has no reasonable grounds to believe that the issuer is in default of securities legislation.

So a sale that is too early is itself a distribution.

**Who checks.** "The person relying on a prospectus exemption is responsible for determining whether the terms and conditions of the prospectus exemption are met" (45-106CP s. 1.9(1)). Unless the seller "has taken reasonable steps to verify the representations", standard representations or an initial on a form are not enough (s. 1.9(3)). An exemption also does not remove suitability: the dealer "must still assess whether the investment is suitable for the purchaser" (s. 1.9(2); see 1.17).

### Accredited investor

The exemption applies if "the purchaser purchases the security as principal and is an accredited investor" (NI 45-106 s. 2.3(1)). The definition also covers many non-individuals. For example, it includes "a person, other than an individual or investment fund, that has net assets of at least $5 000 000 as shown on its most recently prepared financial statements" (s. 1.1, (m)). For individuals, these are the tests:

| Individual test (NI 45-106 s. 1.1) | Threshold | Spouse combined? |
|---|---|---|
| (j) Financial assets, net of related liabilities | exceeds $1,000,000 | Yes |
| (j.1) Financial assets, net of related liabilities | exceeds $5,000,000 | No (individual alone) |
| (k) Pre-tax net income, each of the last 2 years and expected this year | exceeds $200,000 ($300,000 with spouse) | Yes |
| (l) Net assets | at least $5,000,000 | Yes |

The thresholds are "bright-line" standards: below a threshold, the investor does not qualify under that test (45-106CP s. 3.5(2)). Financial assets are "cash, securities, or a contract of insurance, a deposit or an evidence of a deposit that is not a security" (s. 3.5(1)). They exclude the personal residence. Net assets include the home and its mortgage.

Individuals under (j), (k) or (l) sign a Form 45-106F9 risk acknowledgement at or before the purchase agreement (s. 2.3(6)). An individual under (j.1) does not need Form 45-106F9 (45-106CP s. 3.5(1)).

> **Exam trap:** Financial assets (cash, securities, insurance contracts, deposits) **exclude** the home. Net assets **include** the home and its mortgage. A $900,000 portfolio plus a debt-free $3M house fails (j). It fails (l) too, because $3.9M is below $5M.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Colette Brun's financial assets are $700,000, and her spouse's are $400,000, with no related liabilities. Their house is worth $2,000,000. She wants to buy shares under the accredited investor exemption.
>
> **Question.** Does she qualify as an accredited investor, and can she resell the shares freely?
>
> **Resolution.** Test (j) combines spouses: $1,100,000 exceeds $1,000,000, so she qualifies. The house does not count. She signs Form 45-106F9 at or before the purchase agreement. The seller verifies her status. Her Registered Representative (RR) still assesses suitability.
>
> A restricted period applies to her resale (NI 45-102 s. 2.5). She must wait four months from the distribution date. The issuer must also have been a reporting issuer for the four months before her trade. The other s. 2.5(2) conditions also apply. If the issuer never becomes a reporting issuer, she may be unable to resell under s. 2.5.

### Application to takeovers

A **take-over bid** is an offer to acquire outstanding voting or equity securities of a class that, "together with the offeror's securities, constitute in the aggregate 20% or more of the outstanding securities of that class" (NI 62-104 s. 1.1). An offeror that pays with its own new shares makes a distribution. NI 45-106 s. 2.16 exempts it: "The prospectus requirement does not apply to a distribution of a security in connection with a take-over bid" (or an issuer bid).

The target's shareholders usually still get disclosure. If the bid is not exempt from Part 2 of NI 62-104, the offeror "must prepare and send" a bid circular (ss. 2.1, 2.10(1)). A bid that meets a Part 4 exemption is "exempt from Part 2", so this circular duty does not apply (s. 4.1).

*Sources: Securities Act (Ontario) ss. 1(1), 53(1), 73.3; NI 41-101 ss. 1.1, 13.1, 13.2, 13.4, 13.5, 13.7; 41-101CP ss. 2.6, 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, 6.12; NI 45-106 ss. 1.1, 2.3, 2.4, 2.9, 2.10, 2.16, 2.34, 2.35; 45-106CP ss. 1.9, 3.5; NI 45-102 s. 2.5; NI 51-102 s. 7.1; NP 51-201 ss. 1.1, 2.1; NI 62-104 ss. 1.1, 2.1, 2.10, 4.1*

## 3.2 Classes of common shares

The articles of a corporation state its share classes and the rights of each class. For a federal corporation, three rights must exist (Corporations Canada):

- a vote at shareholder meetings;
- dividends, but only when the board declares them;
- a share of what is left after the corporation is dissolved.

"If there are more than one class of shares, each of the three rights have to be assigned to at least one class of shares, but one class does not need to have all three" (Corporations Canada).

| Class | Voting rights | Dividend rights | Rights on dissolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary common | One per share | Pro rata, when declared | Residual, after creditors and preferred |
| Multiple-voting | Several per share (often founders) | Usually equal | Usually equal |
| Subordinate-voting | Fewer per share than another class | Usually equal | Usually equal |
| Restricted-voting | Capped (limit on how many may be voted or owned) | Usually equal | Usually equal |
| Non-voting | None, except certain fundamental issues | Usually equal | Usually equal |

### Dividend rights

A common shareholder has no right to a dividend until the board declares one (see 3.6). Each share of the class then gets the same amount, so the payment is pro rata. In most dual-class companies, each class gets the same dividend per share. Common dividends rank after preferred dividends (3.7).

### Voting rights

Each share carries one vote "unless your articles provide otherwise" (Corporations Canada). Voting shareholders do these things:

- elect and dismiss directors;
- approve by-laws;
- appoint the auditor;
- approve fundamental changes.

Ordinary resolutions need "a simple majority (50 percent plus 1) of votes cast". Special resolutions, for example for fundamental changes, need "two thirds of the votes cast".

Under NI 41-101 s. 1.1, an equity security that is not a preferred security is a "restricted security" if any of these apply:

- another class "carries a greater number of votes per security";
- its class terms, another class's terms or the issuer's constating documents nullify or significantly restrict its voting rights;
- another class has a greater right to earnings or assets per security;
- outside Ontario and British Columbia, the regulator determines that it is a restricted security.

Usually, subordinate-voting, restricted-voting and non-voting classes are restricted securities. A multiple-voting class is not, unless another class has greater economic rights per share (limb (c)). Non-voting holders can still vote "on certain fundamental issues" under the CBCA (Corporations Canada). **Coattail** provisions let the inferior class convert or join a bid made only for the superior-voting class. Without a coattail, a buyer could pay a control premium to the superior class only.

### Rights to surplus on dissolution

On dissolution, common shareholders get what remains after creditors and preferred shareholders. If little remains, they can get nothing. Each common class usually gets the same surplus per share, whatever its voting power. The articles state each class's rights, so they can give one class a different claim.

> **Exam trap:** Lower voting power does not mean lower economic rights. Subordinate-voting shares often get equal dividends and an equal amount on dissolution. The difference is control.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** At Lumen Harbour Media Inc., founders hold Class A shares with 10 votes each. Class B subordinate-voting shares have one vote each. Per share, both classes get equal dividends and equal amounts on dissolution. Siobhan Kerr, a Class B holder, asks if her dividends are smaller.
>
> **Question.** Are her dividends smaller than the dividends on the Class A shares?
>
> **Resolution.** No. Her dividends and her claim on dissolution are equal per share. Her class has less control, so her shares are restricted securities under NI 41-101. She should check for a coattail provision, which protects her class in a take-over.

*Sources: Corporations Canada (ISED), "Share structure and shareholders"; NI 41-101 s. 1.1; otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.3 Canadian depositary receipts (CDRs)

A **CDR** is a Canadian-dollar security, listed in Canada, that represents a fraction of a share of a (usually U.S.) company. A Canadian financial institution acts as **depositary** and holds the underlying shares. CDRs let a Canadian investor own part of a high-priced foreign share, in CAD, on a Canadian exchange. Features vary, and the terms of each CDR govern the details.

### Rights to holder

- **Economic exposure.** The holder gets the price changes of the underlying share and its dividends, paid in CAD.
- **Voting.** The holder votes through the depositary, usually with voting instructions. The exact rights depend on the CDR's terms.
- **Currency hedge.** Most CDRs carry a notional currency hedge. The CDR's C$ value changes about as much as the share's return in its home currency. So USD/CAD changes have little effect on it. The CDR price includes the hedge cost.

**What the holder does not have.** The CDR holder is not a registered shareholder of the foreign company. The depositary holds the shares, and the holder's rights come through it, under the CDR terms. Read the terms for voting, dividends and corporate actions.

### CDR ratio

The **CDR ratio** shows how much of one share each CDR represents. The ratio aims to give each CDR an affordable price. The ratio changes when the exchange rate changes. This is how the hedge works, so the ratio is not fixed.

The ratio sets the holder's proportional exposure. The percentage gain or loss on a CDR is about the same as the share's home-currency return, less the hedge cost. So the CDR price alone does not show if the share is cheap or expensive.

Simplified illustration: at 20 CDRs per share, each CDR represents 1/20 of a share. A US$300 share, with the hedge at about 1.40 CAD/USD, gives roughly C$21 per CDR. A C$21 CDR is not a cheap share; it is a small part of an expensive share.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ysolde Quenneville has $600. She wants exposure to a U.S. technology company whose shares trade near US$300, but she does not want to convert her dollars to USD.
>
> **Question.** Which product meets her need, and what are its disadvantages?
>
> **Resolution.** A CDR matches her stated need. It trades in CAD, so she needs no currency conversion. Each CDR is a fraction of a share, so $600 buys several CDRs.
>
> The hedge removes most of the USD/CAD effect, but the CDR price includes its cost. She votes through the depositary. Suitability still applies (1.17).

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.4 Investing in common shares and depositary receipts

### Sources of risks and potential returns

**Return** has two parts: dividends and a capital gain (or loss). The issuer promises neither part. Dividends depend on the board (3.6). Price gains depend on future profits. A depositary receipt gives the return of the underlying share, adjusted for currency effects and costs.

| Risk | Common shares | Depositary receipts (add-ons) |
|---|---|---|
| Business/earnings | Profits fall, so dividends and price fall | Same as the underlying company |
| Financial (leverage) | Heavy debt makes earnings change more | Same |
| Market (systematic) | Broad sell-offs affect most stocks | Same |
| Liquidity | Thin trading means wide spreads | DR volume may be below the underlying's |
| Control | Restricted shares give little say | Depend on the depositary's terms |
| Currency | No direct FX risk (priced in CAD); indirect FX risk if the company earns or spends in foreign currency | Unhedged DRs carry FX risk; hedged CDRs mostly remove it |

Interest payments do not change. So debt makes the profit for shareholders increase and decrease more. Market risk affects most shares at the same time, and diversification cannot remove it. Diversification can reduce company-specific risk (see 6.3 and 6.4). A Canadian company with foreign revenue, costs or debt has indirect currency risk. For example, a stronger CAD can reduce a Canadian exporter's profit and share price.

### Impact of the costs of acquiring and holding

The main costs are commissions, bid-ask spreads and account or custody fees. A direct purchase of foreign shares adds currency-conversion spreads. CDRs have no conversion spread, but their price includes a hedge cost. A flat fee is a larger percentage of a small trade. Each new trade adds the costs again.

Tax is a cost too. "Foreign dividends do not qualify for the dividend tax credit" (CRA). If the investor paid foreign tax on foreign dividends, a foreign tax credit may be available (CRA, Line 40500).

> **Worked example**
> Mia buys 500 shares at $20 and pays a $25 commission. She receives $0.40 per share in dividends and sells at $22, with another $25 commission.
> ```
> Cost = 10,000 + 25 = 10,025;  proceeds = 11,000 − 25 = 10,975;  dividends = 200
> Return = (10,975 + 200 − 10,025) ÷ 10,025 = 1,150 ÷ 10,025 = 11.47%
> ```
> Without fees: 1,200 ÷ 10,000 = 12.00%. The two $25 fees cost about 0.53 percentage points.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Kofi Amankwah plans to buy five positions of $300 each and to trade often. His dealer charges a flat $9.95 per trade.
>
> **Question.** How much do the fees cost him, as a part of each trade?
>
> **Resolution.** Each purchase fee is about 3.3% of the trade, and the sale costs the same again. So a share must rise about 6.6% just to cover the two fees, before the spread. Fewer, larger trades reduce the cost per dollar invested.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Rosalind Ferraz can buy a U.S. company's shares directly in USD, or buy its hedged CDR in Canada.
>
> **Question.** What does she gain and lose with each choice?
>
> **Resolution.** The direct purchase adds a conversion spread and full USD/CAD risk. In exchange, she holds the voting shares herself, through her dealer. The CDR trades in CAD and removes most currency risk. In exchange, the CDR has these disadvantages:
> - its price includes a hedge cost;
> - she votes through the depositary;
> - its volume can be lower.

*Sources: CRA, "Foreign income" and Line 40500 (federal foreign tax credit) (2025); otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.5 Common share ownership: advantages and disadvantages

A common shareholder owns the residual interest in the company. This position brings the highest potential reward in the capital structure, and the highest risk.

### Advantages and disadvantages of common share ownership

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| No limit on gains: gets part of all growth in profits and value | Last claim on earnings and assets; can lose the whole investment |
| Dividends from taxable Canadian corporations may qualify for the dividend tax credit (3.6) | Dividends are discretionary and can be cut |
| Votes on directors and fundamental changes (voting classes) | Price volatility |
| Liquidity if listed | Restricted classes carry little or no control |
| Limited liability: "limited to the amount they paid for their shares" (Corporations Canada) | |

Creditors and preferred shareholders get fixed amounts. Common shareholders get everything above that. So the possible gain has no limit. But if the company fails, the common shareholder gets money last. Liability is limited: "shareholders are usually not liable for the corporation's debts" (Corporations Canada).

### Advantages and disadvantages to the issuer of common shares

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Permanent capital: no maturity date and no obligation to repay | Dilutes existing owners' control and earnings per share (EPS) |
| No obligation to pay dividends | Equity costs more than debt, because investors demand higher returns |
| Strengthens the balance sheet (lower debt-to-equity) | Dividends come out of after-tax profit, whereas interest is generally a deductible expense |
| | Prospectus, underwriting and continuous-disclosure costs (3.1) |

Common shares have no repayment date and no fixed payment, so a bad year does not cause a default. The new equity also gives creditors a larger cushion. In exchange, new shares spread earnings and votes over more shares (EPS: see 4.7).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Tenzin Dorje is 30 and has a long time horizon. He wants growth and a vote in the companies he owns.
>
> **Question.** Which type of share matches his objective, and which risks must he accept?
>
> **Resolution.** Common shares match his objective. They get part of all profit growth, and voting classes give a vote. He must accept these risks:
> - the board can cut dividends;
> - prices are volatile;
> - he ranks last on dissolution.
>
> Diversification reduces company-specific risk. Suitability still applies (1.17).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Kettleby Brewing Ltd. is private, and its founders own all the shares. It needs $20 million for a new plant and can borrow or issue common shares.
>
> **Question.** What are the effects of an issue of new common shares?
>
> **Resolution.** New common shares give permanent capital with no fixed payment, and debt-to-equity improves. The founders lose part of their control and future earnings. Also, equity costs more than debt. A public issue adds prospectus and disclosure costs (3.1). A loan keeps control but adds a fixed obligation.

*Sources: Corporations Canada (ISED), "Share structure and shareholders"; CRA, Lines 12000/12010 (2025); otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.6 Corporate actions

### How dividends are declared, claimed and taxed

**Declared.** A shareholder has a right to a dividend only "if the board of directors has declared any" (Corporations Canada). The board sets a **record date** and a **payment date**. The company pays the holders of record.

**Claimed.** "Current SRO and marketplace rules mandate a standard T+1 settlement cycle period for most transactions in equity and long term debt securities" (24-101CP s. 5.1). So a buyer must trade at least one business day before the record date. The **ex-dividend date** is the first day the share trades without the dividend. It is normally the same as the record date. On that date, the price usually decreases by about the amount of the dividend.

Street-name clients get the dividend through their dealer. A dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) uses it to buy more shares.

**Taxed.** Dividends from taxable Canadian corporations go into income at a grossed-up amount. A dividend tax credit (DTC) then reduces the tax. The idea is integration: the corporation already paid tax on its profit. "Eligible" dividends are those the payer designates as eligible.

| Federal treatment (ITA ss. 82, 121) | Eligible | Other than eligible |
|---|---|---|
| Gross-up | 38%, so 138% is taxable | 15%, so 115% is taxable |
| Federal dividend tax credit | 6/11 of the gross-up | 9/13 of the gross-up |

These ITA rates apply for years that end after 2011 (eligible) and after 2018 (other). CRA's 2025 pages use 138% and 115%. For example, $100 of eligible dividends gives $138 of taxable income and a federal DTC of 6/11 × $38 = $20.73. Provinces add their own credits. For full tax calculations, see 7.11.

> **Exam trap:** Foreign dividends "do not qualify for this credit." Dividends on U.S. shares also get no gross-up, which applies only to dividends "from corporations resident in Canada" (ITA s. 82(1)).

### The impact of stock splits and consolidations

A **split** multiplies the number of shares and divides the price in the same proportion. The total value and the percentage ownership do not change. A company uses a split to bring a high share price into a popular trading range. A **consolidation** (reverse split) does the opposite and increases a low price. For example, after a 3-for-1 split, 300 shares at $90 become 900 shares at $30. After a 1-for-5 consolidation, 1,000 shares at $0.80 become 200 shares at $4.00.

For tax, "no disposition or acquisition is considered to have occurred and the event is not taxable" (CRA T4037). The shareholder must recalculate the adjusted cost base (ACB) per share (see 7.10).

### Share buyback

An issuer that repurchases its own shares makes an **issuer bid** (NI 62-104 s. 1.1). A normal-course issuer bid through a designated exchange is exempt from the formal-bid rules "if the bid is made in accordance with the bylaws, rules, regulations and policies of that exchange" (s. 4.8(2)). Designated exchanges are TSX, TSX Venture, Cboe Canada and any other exchange that the regulators recognize or designate (s. 4.8(1)).

A buyback returns cash to the shareholders who sell. It reduces the number of shares. If earnings do not decrease, EPS usually increases. The remaining shareholders own a larger percentage. It may signal that management thinks the shares are undervalued. A buyback at a high price can reduce value for the shareholders who stay.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Idris Kalfa wants a dividend whose record date is Friday. He also holds 400 shares of another company that does a 2-for-1 split.
>
> **Question.** When must he buy to get the dividend, and what does the split change?
>
> **Resolution.** Settlement is T+1, so he must buy by Thursday; a Friday trade settles too late. After the split he has 800 shares at half the price. His value and percentage ownership do not change, and the split is not taxable. His ACB per share halves.

*Sources: Corporations Canada (ISED), "Share structure and shareholders"; ITA ss. 82(1), 121; CRA, Lines 12000/12010, Line 40425 and "Eligible dividends" (2025); CRA T4037 (2025); 24-101CP s. 5.1; NI 62-104 ss. 1.1, 4.8*

## 3.7 Classes of preferred shares

Preferred shares rank ahead of common for dividends and on dissolution, and behind all debt. They usually pay a fixed dividend, and the issue terms define each class.

### Dividend rights

| Feature | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Cumulative** | Missed dividends accumulate as arrears, which must be paid before any common dividend |
| **Non-cumulative** | A skipped dividend is lost for good |
| **Participating** | Extra dividends beyond the fixed rate, alongside common |
| **Floating / adjustable-rate** | The dividend moves with a reference interest rate |
| **Fixed-reset** | Rate resets periodically (commonly every 5 years) to a Government of Canada yield plus a fixed spread; holder can usually switch to a floating series |

The board must still declare each preferred dividend. A missed dividend is not a default. The cumulative feature is the main protection: common shareholders get no dividend while preferred arrears are unpaid.

### Other features

| Feature | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Perpetual (straight)** | No maturity or retraction date |
| **Redeemable / callable** | Issuer may buy back at a set price (often par plus a premium) |
| **Retractable** | Holder may sell back to the issuer at a set price on set dates |
| **Convertible** | Holder may exchange for common shares at a set ratio |

### Voting rights

Preferred shares usually have no vote at ordinary shareholder meetings. Many issues give a vote only after the issuer misses a set number of dividends, for example eight quarterly dividends. The class can then often elect some directors. The vote usually ends when the issuer pays the arrears. These triggers are issue terms, so read the terms of each issue.

A non-voting preferred also has a statutory right. Under the CBCA, holders of non-voting shares can "vote on certain fundamental issues" (Corporations Canada; see 3.2). This right is separate from any vote that the issue terms give.

### Rights on dissolution

The holder generally gets a stated amount (often par, e.g. $25) plus any unpaid cumulative dividends (arrears). This claim ranks ahead of common shares but behind creditors. Unless the terms say otherwise, the holder gets no part of any surplus.

> **Remember:** Cumulative protects the **dividend**, retractable protects the **price** (a put for the holder), and callable caps the **price** (a call for the issuer).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Northgate Rail Ltd. has a cumulative preferred that pays $0.25 a quarter. Its terms give the class a vote after eight missed quarterly dividends. Northgate misses six dividends. Halina Duarte holds 1,000 shares.
>
> **Question.** What are her arrears, and which rights does she have now?
>
> **Resolution.** Her arrears are 6 × $0.25 × 1,000 = $1,500. Northgate cannot pay a common dividend until it pays these arrears. Halina has no vote yet, because the terms need eight missed dividends. If Northgate misses two more, her class gets the vote. She cannot claim a default, because a missed preferred dividend is not a default.

*Sources: Corporations Canada (ISED), "Share structure and shareholders"; otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.8 Investing in preferred shares

### Sources of risks and potential returns

**Return** comes mainly from the fixed dividend. Dividends from taxable Canadian corporations may qualify for the dividend tax credit. Eligible dividends get a higher gross-up and a larger federal credit per dollar of dividend (3.6, 7.11). Unless the share is convertible, price gains are usually small.

| Risk | Most exposed |
|---|---|
| **Interest-rate risk:** when rates rise, fixed-dividend prices fall | Straight perpetuals (no maturity to anchor the price) |
| **Credit / dividend risk:** dividends can be suspended without a legal default | Non-cumulative issues and weaker issuers |
| **Call risk:** shares are redeemed when rates fall, capping gains | Callable issues trading above the call price |
| **Reset risk:** the new rate may be lower if Government of Canada yields fall | Fixed-reset issues |
| **Liquidity risk:** small issues trade thinly with wide spreads | Most preferreds compared with common shares |
| **Inflation risk:** a fixed dividend loses purchasing power | Fixed-rate issues |

When new issues pay more, a straight preferred's price falls until its yield matches the market. A perpetual has no maturity to pull its price back to par (compare 2.7). Retractable and floating-rate issues carry the least interest-rate risk. A call feature caps the gain, because the price rarely rises far above the call price.

Credit risk is higher for a preferred than for the same issuer's bonds. The preferred ranks behind all debt, and the board can suspend its dividend without a default. A cumulative feature keeps the arrears, but the holder cannot force payment.

A fixed-reset issue gives partial protection. If yields rise, the next reset rate also rises. Between resets, its price can still fall when rates rise.

### Impact of the costs of acquiring and holding

Commissions and especially **bid-ask spreads** are large compared with a modest expected return. On a thin issue, the spread can cost a large part of one year's dividend. For example, a $25 preferred pays $1.25 a year and has a $0.25 spread. A purchase and a sale at the same quotes lose $0.25, which is 20% of one year's dividend. Use limit orders, and avoid frequent trading.

> **Exam trap:** A preferred dividend is not interest. A missed dividend is not a default. Arrears on cumulative shares are only a priority claim ahead of the common shareholders.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Hollis Nakashima holds a straight perpetual preferred with a fixed 5% dividend. He expects interest rates to rise.
>
> **Question.** How do higher rates affect his share, and which features reduce that risk?
>
> **Resolution.** If rates rise, new preferreds pay more, so his share's price falls. A retractable preferred lets him sell back at a set price on set dates. A floating-rate preferred raises its dividend with rates. Both carry less interest-rate risk.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Joaquim Barros owns a thinly traded fixed-reset preferred. At the reset, the Government of Canada yield is lower than at issue. He wants to sell with a market order.
>
> **Question.** What happens to his dividend at the reset, and how should he sell?
>
> **Resolution.** The new rate is the lower yield plus the fixed spread, so his dividend falls: this is reset risk. The thin market means a wide spread, so a market order can fill far below the last price. A limit order controls the price.

*Sources: CRA, Lines 12000/12010 and Line 40425 (2025); otherwise standard finance concept (verified)*

## 3.9 Common versus preferred ownership in practice

This outcome applies 3.5 to 3.8 to real choices: which security fits this owner or this issuer?

### Advantages and disadvantages to the owners of preferred shares

The owner accepts less growth in exchange for priority and steady income. Each advantage has a reason:

- **Priority.** The preferred dividend comes before any common dividend. On dissolution, the stated amount comes before the common shareholders. Creditors still rank ahead.
- **Tax.** In a taxable account, dividends from taxable Canadian corporations may qualify for the dividend tax credit (3.6). So the after-tax income can be higher than from the same amount of interest.
- **Features.** Cumulative, retractable and convertible features add protection or growth. Each feature usually costs the owner some yield.

The disadvantages come from the fixed dividend. When rates rise, the price falls (3.8). When rates fall, the issuer can call the shares, so the gain has a limit. The owner usually has no vote. Common shares are better for a young investor who wants growth (3.5).

### Advantages and disadvantages to the issuer of preferred shares

The issuer gets equity without new votes, so the common shareholders keep control. A skipped dividend is not a default, so the issuer keeps flexibility in a bad year. Compared with borrowing, the new equity improves debt-to-equity.

The costs are real. Dividends come out of after-tax profit, but interest is generally a deductible expense. So preferreds usually cost more than debt. If the issue is cumulative, arrears block common dividends until the issuer pays them. The market also expects the fixed dividend. So the fixed dividend reduces the issuer's flexibility.

A callable feature gives back some flexibility: the issuer can redeem the shares if rates fall.

> **Exam trap:** A preferred is legally equity but behaves like fixed income: its price follows interest rates more than company growth.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Margaux Vantongeren is retired, has a taxable account and wants steady income with moderate risk. She thinks interest rates may rise.
>
> **Question.** Do preferred shares suit her, and which features help if rates rise?
>
> **Resolution.** Preferred shares match her income goal better than common shares. Their dividends have priority.
>
> In her taxable account, dividends from taxable Canadian corporations may qualify for the dividend tax credit (3.6). If rates rise, retractable or floating-rate preferreds limit her price risk. A fixed-reset issue changes its rate only at each reset. Suitability still applies (1.17).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Sedgewick Ridge Energy Inc. needs new capital. Its founders want to keep control, and its debt is already high.
>
> **Question.** Do preferred shares fit the company's needs?
>
> **Resolution.** Preferred shares fit. They usually carry no vote, so the founders keep control. They add equity, so debt-to-equity improves. Also, a skipped dividend is not a default. The cost is higher than debt, and cumulative arrears would block common dividends.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Casimir Ostrowski wants priority income and some part of the company's growth.
>
> **Question.** Which type of preferred share matches both wishes?
>
> **Resolution.** A convertible preferred matches both wishes. He gets the preferred dividend and priority over common. If the common price rises, he can convert at the set ratio. In exchange, a convertible usually pays a lower dividend than a similar straight preferred.

## 3.10 Valuing equities with time value of money

A share is worth the **present value of its expected cash flows**, discounted at the required return (PV mechanics: 2.9). The required return shows the share's risk. CAPM is one method to estimate it (6.8). A higher required return gives a lower value.

### Discounted cash flow (DCF)

```
P₀ = D₁ ÷ (1 + r) + D₂ ÷ (1 + r)^2 + … + (Dₙ + Pₙ) ÷ (1 + r)^n
P₀ = value today;  Dₜ = dividend in year t;  Pₙ = expected selling price in year n;  r = required return
n = number of years the share is held; dividends and the sale price are assumed received at year-end, with annual discounting
```

> **Worked example**
> Sam expects dividends of $1.00 and $1.10, then to sell at $30 at the end of year 2. He requires 10%.
> ```
> P₀ = 1.00 ÷ 1.10 + 1.10 ÷ 1.10^2 + 30 ÷ 1.10^2
>    = 0.91 + 0.91 + 24.79 = $26.61
> ```

### Dividend discount models

```
Zero growth (e.g. a straight preferred):  P₀ = D ÷ r
Constant growth (Gordon):                 P₀ = D₁ ÷ (r − g),  where D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g) and r > g
D = constant annual dividend, paid forever (a perpetuity);  D₀ = dividend just paid;  g = constant annual dividend growth rate
```

> **Worked example**
> A preferred share pays $1.25 a year and investors require 5%: P₀ = 1.25 ÷ 0.05 = **$25.00**.
> A common share just paid $2.00, with dividends that grow 4% a year and a required return of 9%:
> ```
> D₁ = 2.00 × 1.04 = 2.08;   P₀ = 2.08 ÷ (0.09 − 0.04) = 2.08 ÷ 0.05 = $41.60
> ```

The Gordon model suits a stable company with steady dividend growth. It does not work for a company that pays no dividend. It is very sensitive to the gap between r and g.

### Price–earnings growth models

Value = expected EPS × an appropriate P/E. The constant-growth model gives the "justified" forward P/E:

```
P₀ ÷ E₁ = payout ratio ÷ (r − g)      →      P₀ = E₁ × P/E
E₁ = next year's EPS;  payout ratio = D₁ ÷ E₁
```

> **Worked example**
> Next year's EPS is forecast at $3.00, the payout ratio is 40%, r = 10% and g = 6%.
> ```
> Justified P/E = 0.40 ÷ (0.10 − 0.06) = 10;   P₀ = 3.00 × 10 = $30.00
> Check: D₁ = 0.40 × 3.00 = 1.20;   1.20 ÷ 0.04 = $30.00
> ```

Higher growth or a lower required return justifies a higher P/E. The PEG ratio (P/E ÷ expected EPS growth in %) also relates P/E to growth. For P/E as a value ratio, see 4.7.

> **Exam trap:** Gordon uses **next** year's dividend (D₁), not D₀, and fails when g ≥ r. Market price below model value → the share looks undervalued.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Leilani Kahale looks at the common share from the Gordon example. It trades at $36.00.
>
> **Question.** Is the share undervalued or overvalued?
>
> **Resolution.**
> ```
> At r = 9%:   P₀ = 2.08 ÷ (0.09 − 0.04) = $41.60   → above $36.00: looks undervalued
> At r = 10%:  P₀ = 2.08 ÷ (0.10 − 0.04) = $34.67   → below $36.00: looks overvalued
> ```
> A one-point change in r reverses the answer, so treat a model value as an estimate.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Saltmarsh Foods Inc. trades at $42.00. Harjit Gill uses the inputs from the P/E example: E₁ = $3.00, payout 40%, r = 10%, g = 6%.
>
> **Question.** Does the market price fit the model, and what growth does the price imply?
>
> **Resolution.**
> ```
> Market P/E = 42.00 ÷ 3.00 = 14;   justified P/E = 10;   model value = $30.00
> At g = 7%:  justified P/E = 0.40 ÷ (0.10 − 0.07) = 13.33;   P₀ = 1.20 ÷ 0.03 = $40.00
> Implied g:  0.40 ÷ (0.10 − g) = 14   →   0.10 − g = 0.0286   →   g ≈ 7.1%
> ```
> At 6% growth, the share looks overvalued. Even at 7% growth, the model value ($40.00) is below $42.00. The market price fits the model only with different inputs. For example, the market can expect growth of about 7.1% or more, a lower required return or higher EPS.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*
