# Element 6: Portfolio construction

This element moves from single securities to whole portfolios. It covers these topics:

- how to set and maintain the asset mix;
- how to measure and manage risk;
- short sales and margin;
- the theories behind portfolio recommendations (MPT, CAPM, APT, factor models, EMH);
- active and passive techniques for equity and fixed-income portfolios.

The element carries about **13 of the 120 questions**.

Expect theory questions and short scenarios. A typical question asks which strategy or risk applies, or what happens to a margin requirement when the price moves. The only calculations are in 6.8: CAPM expected return and portfolio beta. The exam tests risk measures, portfolio risk and margin conceptually.

Rule content is mainly in 6.2 (financial crime risk) and 6.5 (short sales and margin). "Manager" means the portfolio manager, and "RR" means the Registered Representative in client scenarios.

## 6.1 Asset allocation process

Asset allocation decides how much of a portfolio goes into each asset class. It is the first and most important decision in portfolio construction. The input is the client's KYC profile (see 1.2 and 1.3). The output goes into the recommendation (see 7.5).

### Principles of portfolio construction; process and importance of asset allocation

Portfolio construction follows a fixed order:

1. Start from the client's objectives: safety, income and growth.
2. Identify the client's constraints:
   - time horizon;
   - liquidity;
   - tax;
   - legal or unique circumstances;
   - risk tolerance.
3. Choose the asset mix.
4. Select the securities within each asset class.
5. Monitor the portfolio and rebalance it.

Three principles guide each step:

- **Fit:** each holding serves the client's objectives and constraints.
- **Diversification:** no single loss decides the result (see 6.4).
- **Cost control:** fees, spreads and taxes reduce the net return every year.

In a diversified portfolio, the mix explains most of the variation in returns, more than security selection does. So careful security selection cannot repair a mix that does not fit the client.

### Asset mix categories and strategies for setting the asset mix

There are three main categories:

- cash and equivalents (liquidity, safety);
- fixed income (income, stability);
- equities (long-term growth).

Some portfolios also add alternatives (see 5.13). Conservative clients usually hold more cash and bonds. Growth clients usually hold more equities.

An RR can use these strategies to set the mix (see 7.5):

- **Model portfolios:** the RR matches the client's profile to one of the dealer's model mixes, from conservative to aggressive.
- **Time horizon or life stage:** a longer horizon permits more equities. The equity weight usually falls as a goal comes near.
- **Ranges around targets:** each asset class has a target weight and a permitted range.

### Types and key aspects of asset allocation

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| Strategic | Long-run "policy" mix with target weights and ranges, set from the client's profile; may change on a schedule over the client's life (e.g. a target-date glide path) |
| Tactical | Temporary, deliberate tilts away from the strategic mix to exploit expected market conditions (an active forecast) |
| Dynamic | A rule-based mix that changes automatically as market values change (e.g. portfolio insurance: more equities after gains, fewer after losses) |
| Rebalancing | Restoring drifted weights to fixed targets, on a calendar or when a band (e.g. ±5 percentage points) is breached |

The strategic mix is the base. Tactical and dynamic allocation make changes from that base. A tactical tilt is a forecast. If the forecast is wrong, the portfolio does worse than the strategic mix. A dynamic rule needs no forecast, but it can cause many trades when markets move fast.

### Benefits of different stock selection techniques

- **Quantitative screens** are cheap and consistent.
- **Fundamental analysis, company by company,** can find mispriced stocks, but it costs more.
- **Top-down and bottom-up approaches** start from the economy or from the company (see 4.1 and 6.10).
- **Indexing** is the passive alternative. The manager does not select stocks and gets market exposure at low cost (see 6.11).

### Benefits of a rebalancing strategy

Rebalancing keeps the risk level that the client agreed to. Without it, the best performer grows into a larger share, and risk drifts from the target. When equity markets rise, the drift usually means more risk than the client agreed to. Rebalancing also makes the manager sell assets that rose and buy assets that fell. For example, a 60/40 portfolio whose equities rise 20% (bonds flat) drifts to about 64/36. To rebalance, the manager sells equities and buys bonds.

A band can be absolute or relative. On a 60% target, ±5 percentage points gives 55% to 65%, but ±5% of the target gives 57% to 63%.

### Costs of implementation and rebalancing

Each trade costs the bid–ask **spread** and a **commission**. It also takes **time**. In a large or illiquid trade, the price can move against the client before the trade is complete. In a taxable account, a sale can also realize a capital gain (see 7.10). These costs are the reason for tolerance bands rather than constant rebalancing.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ilse Brandvold has an advisory account. Her strategic mix is 60% equities and 40% bonds, with a band of ±5 percentage points. After a strong year for equities, her mix is 66/34.
>
> **Question.** Should her Registered Representative (RR) rebalance?
>
> **Resolution.** The equity band is 55% to 65%, so 66% is outside it. The RR recommends a sale of equities and a purchase of bonds to return to 60/40. In an advisory account, "the client is responsible for all investment decisions" (IDPC 1201). So the RR gets her instruction before the trade (see 8.5). In a taxable account, the RR first checks the capital gain that the sale will realize.

*Sources: IDPC 1201 ("advisory account"); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.2 Types of risk

Risk is the chance that the actual result differs from the expected result. Each type of risk has a different cause and affects different assets. So match each risk to the client's situation and time horizon.

**Interest rate risk.** Bond prices fall when interest rates rise. New bonds pay the higher rate, so the price of an older bond must fall to give a buyer a competitive yield. Longer terms and lower coupons make the price move more (see 2.7). Long-term bonds and preferred shares are most exposed. If the issuer does not default, a client who holds a bond to maturity still gets par back.

**Inflation (purchasing-power) risk.** If a return is lower than inflation, the client's real wealth shrinks. Cash and fixed-coupon bonds are most exposed, because their payments do not increase with prices.

**Liquidity risk.** The client cannot sell the asset quickly at a price near its fair value. Thinly traded and exempt-market securities are the usual examples. This risk matters most when the client may need cash at short notice.

**Capital risk.** The value can fall below the amount invested. Equities and high-yield debt carry the most. A client with a short time horizon has little time to recover from a loss, so this risk is more serious for that client.

**Income risk.** The income from a portfolio can fall. A company can cut its dividend. Or, when a bond matures, the client must reinvest the money at a lower rate (reinvestment risk). Retirees who live on portfolio income are most exposed.

**Issuer (credit/business) risk.** Problems at one issuer can cause a default, a credit downgrade or a fall in profits. Single-issuer holdings and lower-rated bonds carry the most. Diversification across issuers reduces it (see 6.4).

> **Exam trap:** A GIC has no price risk, yet its fixed return can still lose to inflation. A bond held to maturity avoids price risk but keeps reinvestment (income) risk.

### Financial crime risk

Every account is exposed to financial crime risk. This risk has two sides:

1. **The client is the victim** of investment fraud or of financial exploitation. IDPC 3201 defines financial exploitation as "the use or control of, or deprivation of the use or control of, a financial asset of an individual by a person through undue influence, unlawful conduct or another wrongful act". For the trusted contact person and temporary holds, see 1.6.
2. **The account is the tool** for crime, mainly money laundering or market abuse. Money laundering makes money from crime look legal.

One object of the PCMLTFA is "to detect and deter money laundering and the financing of terrorist activities" (s. 3(a)). Part 1 of the Act applies to persons "authorized under provincial legislation to engage in the business of dealing in securities" (s. 5(g)). CIRO's guidance states: "The PCMLTFA requires that Investment Dealers report attempted and completed suspicious transactions." (GN-3200-23-001, s. 6.1)

The report goes to FINTRAC when there are "reasonable grounds to suspect" a listed offence, for example money laundering (PCMLTFA s. 7). Section 8.2 gives the full list of offences and the reporting steps.

CIRO's AML guidance (GN-3200-23-001) lists signs that need more investigation. Examples:

- **Client indicators:**
  - ownership structures that hide the true beneficial owner;
  - an account used for undeclared purposes.
- **Trading indicators:**
  - OTC securities (little required disclosure);
  - stock manipulation;
  - frequent trading in low-priced securities, especially with immediate liquidation or transfer of proceeds, or with no reasonable rationale;
  - shell companies or inactive issuers.
- **Non-trading indicators:**
  - cash deposits or withdrawals directly with the dealer;
  - deposits of physical certificates;
  - early redemptions that cause fees, when the client gives no reasonable explanation;
  - proceeds paid as bank drafts or certified cheques;
  - accounts used only to move funds.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Dariusz Marchetti is a new client. He deposits physical share certificates of an inactive issuer that trades over the counter. He sells all the shares two days later and asks for the proceeds as a certified cheque.
>
> **Question.** Which type of risk does this activity show, and what does the RR do?
>
> **Resolution.** This is financial crime risk, with the account as the tool. Four separate indicators from GN-3200-23-001 occur together:
> - physical certificates (s. 5.3.2);
> - an OTC security (s. 5.2.1);
> - an inactive issuer (s. 5.2.4);
> - proceeds as a certified cheque (s. 5.3.4).
>
> The quick sale adds to the concern. But it is one sale, not the frequent trading in low-priced securities that s. 5.2.3 describes. Under the guidance, employees "must escalate and report any suspicious activity" (s. 6.2). So the RR escalates the pattern under the dealer's AML procedures. If there are reasonable grounds to suspect, the dealer must report the transaction (PCMLTFA s. 7; see 8.2).

*Sources: IDPC 3201 ("financial exploitation"); CIRO GN-3200-23-001 (AML Compliance Guidance), ss. 5, 6.1, 6.2; PCMLTFA ss. 3, 5(g), 7; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.3 Measures of risk

Each risk measure answers a different question, so choose the measure that fits the decision.

### Standard deviation and variance

**Variance and standard deviation** measure total risk: how widely returns scatter around their mean. Variance is the average squared deviation of returns from the mean. Standard deviation (σ) is its square root. It is in return units, so it is the figure that people usually quote.

For roughly normal returns, about 68% of outcomes fall within ±1σ of the mean, and about 95% within ±2σ. For example, a fund with an average return of 8% and σ = 10% returns between −2% and 18% in about two years out of three. A limit of σ: it treats a large gain as "risk" in the same way as a large loss.

### Beta

**Beta (β)** measures systematic (market) risk: how sensitive a security is to market movements. The market, usually a broad index, has β = 1.

- A stock with β = 1.3 tends to move about 1.3% for each 1% market move.
- A stock with β = 0.7 is defensive.
- A stock with β < 0 tends to move against the market.

Beta says nothing about company-specific risk. In a diversified portfolio, diversification removes most of that risk, so beta is the risk that remains (see 6.4 and 6.8). Beta comes from past data and one chosen index, so it can change.

### Multi-factor measures

**Multi-factor measures** describe an asset's exposure ("loading") to several risk sources at once (see 6.7). Examples of these sources:

- the market;
- company size;
- value;
- interest rates;
- inflation.

A loading works like a separate beta for each factor. Two funds with the same market beta can react very differently to a change in rates. A single beta hides that difference.

### Drawdown

**Drawdown** is the decline from a previous peak to a later trough, in percent. Maximum drawdown, the largest such fall in a period, shows the loss that a client actually lives through. For example, a fall from $120,000 to $90,000 is a 25% drawdown. After that fall, the portfolio needs a gain of about 33% to return to its peak. A deep drawdown can make a client sell at the bottom. The loss then becomes permanent.

> **Exam trap:** Standard deviation is *total* risk (systematic + unsystematic). Beta is *systematic* risk only. A single stock can have a high σ and a low β.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Benedikta Varga holds a broad, diversified portfolio. She can add Fund A (σ 24%, β 0.6, maximum drawdown 40%) or Fund B (σ 18%, β 1.1, maximum drawdown 30%).
>
> **Question.** Which fund adds more market risk to her portfolio?
>
> **Resolution.** Inside a diversified portfolio, beta is the relevant measure, so Fund B adds more systematic risk. Much of Fund A's high σ is unsystematic. Her other holdings remove most of it by diversification. Alone, Fund A has larger swings in value.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.4 Risk management: risk, return, hedging and diversification

Risk management does not mean that the client avoids risk. It means that the client takes the right risks, in the right amounts, for the expected return.

### The role of risk in asset selection

Investors demand a higher expected return for more risk. This is the risk–return trade-off. So judge every asset on its return *per unit of risk*. Also judge how the asset changes the risk of the whole portfolio, not only its risk alone. An asset that is risky alone can lower portfolio risk if its returns move differently from the rest. The total risk must stay within the client's risk profile (see 1.3).

### Diversification

**Diversification** combines assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated. Gains on some assets then partly offset losses on others. It reduces unsystematic (company-specific) risk at little cost. It cannot remove systematic (market) risk, because all stocks share exposure to the economy. Each added holding gives a smaller extra reduction in risk. How differently the holdings behave matters more than their number (see 6.6).

### Hedging

**Hedging** takes an offsetting position to reduce a specific risk. Examples:

- a put option on a stock that the client holds;
- a short futures contract against a portfolio;
- a currency hedge on foreign investments.

A hedge has a cost: option premiums or lost upside. Unlike diversification, a hedge targets one named risk, and it can reduce systematic risk.

### Factors that affect the expected return and risk of a portfolio

Four inputs drive a portfolio's expected return and risk:

- the weights of the assets;
- each asset's expected return;
- each asset's standard deviation;
- the correlations (ρ, from −1 to +1) between the assets.

Expected return is simply the weighted average of the assets' expected returns. Risk is *not* a weighted average. When ρ is below +1, portfolio σ is *less* than the weighted-average σ. The lower the correlation, the bigger the reduction.

For example, take a 60/40 mix of equities (σ 16%) and bonds (σ 6%). Its weighted-average σ is 12%. When the correlation is low, its portfolio σ is well below 12%. The gap is the diversification benefit. You do not need to calculate portfolio σ from correlations.

> **Remember:** ρ = +1 → no risk reduction; ρ = 0 → substantial reduction; ρ = −1 → risk can, in theory, be eliminated.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Freya Tanaka holds only the shares of four large Canadian banks. Her RR suggests that she replace part of them with bonds and with shares from other sectors.
>
> **Question.** How does the change affect her expected return and her risk?
>
> **Resolution.** The same economic and rate news drives all four banks, so their returns are highly correlated. Her portfolio therefore keeps most of the risk of one industry. Bonds and other sectors have lower correlations with banks, so her portfolio σ falls by more than the weights alone suggest. Her new expected return is the weighted average of the expected returns of all her holdings. If bonds have a lower expected return than bank shares, her expected return falls a little.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Torsten Achterberg holds a large position in Copperline Energy shares. He has a large unrealized capital gain and does not want to sell this year. He fears a sharp fall in the share price.
>
> **Question.** Should he hedge or diversify?
>
> **Resolution.** A sale would remove the issuer risk, but it would realize the capital gain (see 7.10). A protective put gives him the right to sell at the strike price until expiry. So it sets a minimum value for his shares: the strike price, less the premium. If the price rises, he keeps all the upside, less the premium. Options also need their own account documents, including a signed derivatives trading agreement (IDPC 3251; see 8.5).
>
> A hedge costs money and expires. So it suits a temporary concern when he has a tax reason not to sell. If he wants to remove the issuer risk permanently, he diversifies by selling over time.

*Sources: IDPC 3251 (derivatives account, cross-reference); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.5 Short selling: process, risks and margin

Short selling lets a client profit from a fall in price, but it has extra risks. UMIR controls short-sale orders. IDPC margin rules control the credit that the dealer gives the client.

### The process of short selling

1. The client sells shares that the client does not own, and expects to buy them back later at a lower price.
2. The dealer borrows shares to deliver to the buyer.
3. Later, the client buys the shares back ("covers"), and the dealer returns them to the lender.

A UMIR short sale "means a sale of a security, other than a derivative instrument, which the seller does not own". Borrowed shares do not count as owned. But a seller who bought the security and has not yet received it is deemed to own it.

Under UMIR 3.2(1), a Participant or Access Person must not enter a short-sale order on a marketplace (order type: 8.3) in these cases:

- if the order is not **marked short** (short-marking exempt orders are excepted, UMIR 3.2(2));
- if the security is a **Short Sale Ineligible Security** at the time of entry (limited exceptions in UMIR 3.2(3));
- if the security is a **Pre-Borrow Security**, unless the Participant or Access Person arranged the borrowing before it entered the order.

The Market Regulator designates these two kinds of security (UMIR Policy 1.1, Parts 2.1 and 4).

> **Key rule:** "a Participant or Access Person must have a reasonable expectation to settle any resulting trade on the date contemplated on the execution of the trade." (UMIR 3.3(1))

For a seller deemed to own the security, the duty applies unless the Participant is reasonably informed of the seller's expected delivery (UMIR 3.3(2)). The information must show that the seller expects to deliver the security for settlement:

- as soon as all delivery restrictions are removed; and
- no later than 35 consecutive calendar days after the trade date.

IDPC 4782 puts the same duty, with the same exception, on a dealer that is not a Participant.

### The associated risks and how to manage them

- **Unlimited loss.** The price can rise without limit, but the gain cannot exceed the sale price.
- **Recall.** The lender can ask for the shares back, and force a cover at a bad time.
- **Short squeeze.** Many short sellers cover at once, and their purchases push the price up further.
- **Carry costs.** The client pays any dividends and borrowing fees on the borrowed shares while the position is open.
- **Margin calls.** As the price rises, the margin requirement increases.

The client can limit these risks with:

- a stop-buy order (8.3). If the stock opens much higher, the loss can be larger than the stop price suggests;
- a call option, which sets a maximum loss but costs a premium;
- a small position.

The margin agreement must describe the dealer's right to "sell assets in the client’s account and make purchases to cover short sales" (IDPC 3247(2)(v)). If the client requires prior notice, the agreement must also describe that notice and what the client must do to fix a deficiency. If the dealer "considers it necessary for its credit risk protection", it may "buy securities held short for an indebted client" (IDPC 5116).

### Margin requirements for long and short positions

Short positions are carried in a margin account. Before the dealer lets a client trade on margin, it must ensure that the client "is aware of the risks and benefits associated with trading on margin" (IDPC 3246). Before it opens a margin account, the dealer must also "obtain a copy of the margin account agreement signed by the client" (IDPC 3247(1)).

IDPC 5113 says that the loan value is "generally calculated" by these formulas (other methods for special cases: 5113(2)–(3)):

```
Long:  loan value = (100% − margin rate) × market value
Short: loan value = (100% + margin rate) × (negative) market value
```

The dealer totals the positive and negative loan values in the account (IDPC 5113(4)):

- A net positive loan value permits a debit balance no larger than that value.
- A net negative loan value requires a credit balance at least equal to that value.

So at a 50% rate, $20,000 of stock held long supports a $10,000 loan. A short position needs a credit of 150% of the shares' value, and this amount rises with the price.

Minimum client rates for Canada and U.S. equities eligible for margin, listed or unlisted (IDPC 5310, applied by 5311). Long and short rates are the same, except below $0.25:

| Price per share | Margin rate |
|---|---|
| ≥ $2.00 and qualifying for inclusion on CIRO's List of Securities Eligible for Reduced Margin | 30% |
| ≥ $2.00, other | 50% |
| $1.75–$1.99 | 60% |
| $1.50–$1.74 | 80% |
| $0.25–$1.49 | 100% |
| below $0.25 | long 100%; short $0.25 per share |

If the account becomes undermargined, these rules apply (Form 1, Schedule 4, Note 5):

- Within 20 business days, the dealer must restrict the account to trades that reduce the deficiency, until the account is fully margined.
- The dealer must not advance funds or deliver securities while the account is undermargined, or if that step would make it undermargined.

If the client does not bring the account into good standing, the dealer itself must "provide margin against the shortfall" (IDPC 5111(2); see 8.5).

> **Exam trap:** The short-sale proceeds stay in the account as part of the required credit. They are not free cash.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Bastien Leclair bought 1,000 shares of Ashgrove Tech at $2.40 with the maximum margin loan, at a 50% rate. The price falls to $1.80.
>
> **Question.** What happens to his margin position?
>
> **Resolution.** The market value falls by 25%, and at $1.80 the minimum rate rises from 50% to 60%. So his loan value falls below his debit balance, and the account is undermargined. He can deposit cash or marginable securities, or he can sell shares. Until he corrects the deficiency, he cannot withdraw funds or securities (Form 1, Schedule 4, Note 5). If the dealer considers it necessary for its credit risk protection, it may sell his shares (IDPC 5116).

*Sources: UMIR 1.1 "short sale", 3.2(1)–(3), 3.3(1)–(2), Policy 1.1 Parts 2.1 and 4; IDPC 3246, 3247, 4782, 5111, 5113, 5116, 5310–5311, Form 1 Schedule 4 Note 5; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.6 Portfolio recommendation concepts

### Modern portfolio theory (mean–variance theory)

**Modern portfolio theory (MPT), or mean–variance theory** (Markowitz), assumes that investors are risk averse. It also assumes that they judge portfolios only by expected return and variance. Combinations of imperfectly correlated assets give an **efficient frontier**: the set of portfolios with the highest expected return for each level of risk.

Investors select the point on the frontier that matches their risk tolerance. A portfolio below the frontier is inefficient, because another portfolio gives more return for the same risk. For every asset, MPT needs three estimates:

- expected return;
- standard deviation;
- correlation with the other assets.

Small errors in these estimates can change the weights a lot.

### Efficient diversification, naïve diversification and concentration

**Efficient diversification** chooses weights from the same three estimates. It looks for assets with low correlations to each other. So it gets more risk reduction per holding than equal weights do. Its goal is a portfolio on the efficient frontier, or close to it.

**Naïve diversification** simply spreads money across many holdings, often equally (the "1/N" approach), with no regard to correlations. It still reduces unsystematic risk as the number of holdings increases. But each new holding adds less benefit than the one before. It can also leave hidden concentration:

- **Industry concentration:** many correlated names, such as ten bank stocks, react to the same news. An efficient mix with the same number of holdings, spread across sectors and bonds, has a lower σ (see the 6.4 scenario).
- **Issuer concentration:** one oversized holding makes the portfolio depend on one company (issuer risk: see 6.2).

### Black–Litterman model

Plain mean–variance optimization is very sensitive to its return estimates. It often gives extreme, concentrated weights. The **Black–Litterman model** has two steps:

1. Start from the market's equilibrium (implied) returns. These come from market-capitalization weights.
2. Blend in the manager's views, and weight each view by the manager's confidence in it.

The result is a more stable allocation. It differs from market weights only where the manager holds views. Its limit: the result depends on the views and confidence levels that the manager enters. Poor views still give a poor portfolio.

### Monte Carlo simulation

**Monte Carlo simulation** runs thousands of random return paths from three assumed inputs:

- expected return;
- volatility;
- correlation.

It reports a *distribution* of outcomes, not one straight-line projection. For example, it can report "78% probability the savings last to age 95". That result also means that the savings run out in about 22% of the paths (financial goals: see 7.8). The simulation is only as good as its assumptions.

> **Exam trap:** Black–Litterman starts from *market equilibrium returns*, not historical averages. Monte Carlo gives probabilities, not a guaranteed result.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.7 Asset pricing models

An asset pricing model estimates the return that investors require for the risk of an asset. The models differ in the risks that they count.

**Purpose.** Managers use asset pricing models for three main tasks:

- to set discount rates for valuation;
- to judge whether a security is fairly priced;
- to evaluate whether a manager's alpha comes from skill or simply from exposure to known factors.

### Capital asset pricing model (CAPM)

The **CAPM** explains expected return with one factor: beta to the market portfolio. It rewards only systematic risk, because investors can remove unsystematic risk by diversification at no cost.

- **Advantages:** simple and intuitive; the standard tool for required return.
- **Disadvantages:**
  - unrealistic assumptions (e.g. borrow and lend at the risk-free rate, no taxes or costs);
  - the true market portfolio cannot be observed;
  - a single beta often explains returns poorly.

The calculation is in 6.8.

### Arbitrage pricing theory (APT)

**Arbitrage pricing theory (APT)** (Ross) explains expected return with several systematic factors. Examples:

- inflation;
- GDP growth;
- interest rates;
- credit spreads.

It rests on no-arbitrage. If two portfolios have the same factor exposures, they must have the same expected return. Otherwise, traders could earn a riskless profit.

- **Advantages:** fewer assumptions than the CAPM; flexible.
- **Disadvantages:** it does not name the factors or how many there are, so the choice of factors is subjective.

### Multi-factor models

Researchers found return patterns that the CAPM misses. On average, small companies and cheap ("value") companies earned more than their betas predicted. Multi-factor models add such patterns as factors:

- **Size (SMB, small minus big):** small companies minus large companies.
- **Value (HML, high minus low):** high book-to-market companies minus low book-to-market companies.
- **Profitability (RMW, robust minus weak):** robust profitability minus weak profitability.
- **Investment (CMA, conservative minus aggressive):** conservative investment minus aggressive investment.
- **Momentum (winners minus losers):** stocks with high recent returns minus stocks with low recent returns.

| Model | Factors | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fama–French five-factor | Market, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA | Explains more cross-sectional return variation than CAPM | More data and estimation; factors are empirical, and their premiums can fade |
| Carhart four-factor | Fama–French three factors (market, SMB, HML) + momentum | Captures momentum; widely used to judge fund managers | Momentum is volatile and costly to trade |
| FF5 + momentum | Five factors + momentum (six in all) | Broadest coverage of known anomalies | Most complex; risk of data-mining |

> **Exam trap:** Carhart = *three* Fama–French factors + momentum (4). The Fama–French five-factor model adds profitability and investment, *not* momentum. Momentum added to the five gives six.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Kwame Wolstenholme reviews a small-cap value fund. It beat the S&P/TSX Composite by about 3% a year, and its CAPM alpha is positive. With a Fama–French model, its alpha is close to zero.
>
> **Question.** What does the difference tell him?
>
> **Resolution.** The CAPM counts only market risk, so it labels the extra return from small-cap and value stocks as "alpha". The Fama–French model counts size and value as factors, and then little extra return remains. So the results came mostly from factor exposure, not from skill in stock selection. A lower-cost factor fund could give him similar exposure.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.8 Applying the CAPM

The CAPM gives the return that an investor should require for a security's systematic risk. Plotted against beta, it is the **security market line (SML)**.

```
E(Rᵢ) = R_f + βᵢ × (E(Rₘ) − R_f)
R_f = risk-free rate (e.g. T-bill yield); E(Rₘ) = expected market return;
(E(Rₘ) − R_f) = market risk premium; βᵢ = the asset's beta
Portfolio beta: βₚ = Σ wᵢ × βᵢ   (wᵢ = weight of asset i in the portfolio)
```

### Expected return of an asset

1. Find the market risk premium: E(Rₘ) − R_f.
2. Multiply the premium by the asset's beta.
3. Add the risk-free rate.
4. Compare the result with the forecast return. A forecast above the CAPM return plots above the SML (undervalued). A forecast below it plots below the SML (overvalued).

> **Worked example**
> R_f = 3%, E(Rₘ) = 8%, so the market risk premium = 5%. Eastbrook Ltd. has β = 1.2.
>
> E(R) = 3 + 1.2 × 5 = **9%**.
>
> An analyst forecasts 10% for Eastbrook. The forecast is more than the required return, so the stock plots *above* the SML. It is undervalued, with expected (ex-ante) alpha = 10 − 9 = +1%. Measured after the fact, the same comparison is Jensen's alpha (9.3). Buy. A forecast below 9% would plot below the SML (overvalued).
>
> **Portfolio:** 50% Eastbrook (β 1.2), 30% a utility (β 0.8), 20% T-bills (β 0).
>
> βₚ = 0.5(1.2) + 0.3(0.8) + 0.2(0) = 0.84; E(Rₚ) = 3 + 0.84 × 5 = **7.2%**.

### Expected return and risk of a portfolio

In the CAPM, beta is the measure of risk. A portfolio's beta is the weighted average of its assets' betas. β = 0.84 means that the portfolio should move about 84% as much as the market. To raise expected return, the manager must accept more beta, which means more market risk. To lower risk, the manager moves money to lower-beta assets or to T-bills (β = 0), and the expected return falls too.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Yevgenia Ruiz holds a gold-mining fund with a high standard deviation and a beta of about 0.3. She asks why its CAPM required return is so low when the fund is so volatile.
>
> **Question.** How does the CAPM explain this result?
>
> **Resolution.** The CAPM rewards only systematic risk. Most of the fund's volatility is unsystematic, and diversification can remove it. Only the low beta enters the formula, so the required return is close to the risk-free rate. Inside a diversified portfolio, the fund adds little market risk. Alone, it is still very volatile (see 6.3).

> **Exam trap:** Multiply beta by the *premium* (E(Rₘ) − R_f), not by the market return itself. Then add back R_f.

## 6.9 The efficient markets hypothesis

The **efficient markets hypothesis (EMH)** says that prices reflect available information. So no one can consistently beat the market with that information, after costs and risk adjustment. The logic is competition. Many analysts search for mispriced securities, and their trades quickly move prices to fair value. New information arrives at random, so price changes are hard to predict. In this section, **abnormal returns** are returns above what the risk justifies (see 4.11).

| Form | Prices reflect | Strategy that cannot earn abnormal returns |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | All past price and volume data | Technical analysis |
| Semi-strong | All publicly available information (includes weak) | Fundamental analysis of public data |
| Strong | All information, public and private (includes both) | Even insider information |

### Implications for portfolio management

Each form has a different result for strategy:

- **Weak form.** Technical analysis cannot give consistent abnormal returns. Fundamental analysis and private information can still give an edge.
- **Semi-strong form.** Fundamental analysis of public data also fails, because prices adjust quickly to news. Only private information can still give an edge.
- **Strong form.** No information gives an edge, not even private information.

The more efficient the market, the stronger the case for **passive, low-cost indexing** (see 6.11). The extra return from active research is then unlikely to be more than its cost. The manager adds value in other ways. The manager can:

- set the asset mix (see 6.1);
- manage risk (see 6.4);
- control costs and taxes;
- fit the portfolio to the client.

A client who thinks that the market is easy to beat often trades too much and buys recent winners. Each trade adds costs, and in a taxable account a sale can realize a gain.

### Limits of the EMH

The evidence for the EMH is mixed. Researchers found anomalies, such as the size, value and momentum effects (see 6.7). Some anomalies can be rewards for extra risk, and some disappear after publication. Behavioural finance also shows that investors can overreact or underreact to news (see 7.3). Most observers see large, heavily followed markets as close to semi-strong efficient. Less-followed segments (small caps, some bonds) may leave more room for active managers (see 6.10).

> **Exam trap:** Weak-form efficiency does not rule out abnormal returns from fundamental analysis. The semi-strong form rules them out. So does the strong form, because it includes the semi-strong form.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Chiamaka Vasquez pays for a newsletter that sells chart patterns as a way to beat the S&P/TSX Composite.
>
> **Question.** What does the EMH imply for her plan?
>
> **Resolution.** Chart patterns use past prices and volume. If the market is at least weak-form efficient, prices already reflect those data. So the newsletter cannot give her consistent abnormal returns, and her extra trades add spreads and commissions (see 6.1). A low-cost index fund is the alternative that fits the EMH.

## 6.10 Active equity management techniques

Active management tries to beat a benchmark after costs. Each technique uses a different forecast of what the market has not yet priced. Analyze-level questions ask three things:

- which technique a manager uses;
- when it fits;
- what risks it has.

### Top-down vs. bottom-up

A top-down manager starts with the economy, then chooses sectors, then companies (macroeconomic factors: see 4.13). A bottom-up manager studies company fundamentals first and pays little attention to the economy (company analysis: see 4.1). Top-down captures macro and sector trends, but it can miss good companies in the "wrong" sectors. Bottom-up finds individual mispricings, but it can overlook sector or macro risk. Growth and value are *styles*, and a manager can run either style top-down or bottom-up.

### Sector rotation

Sector rotation overweights the sectors that the manager expects to lead in the next phase of the business cycle. A common textbook pattern is below, but actual leadership varies:

- early expansion: cyclicals such as consumer discretionary and financials;
- late expansion: energy and materials;
- contraction: defensives (staples, utilities, health care).

Sector traits and the cycle are in 4.12 and 4.14. Rotation can add value at turning points. If the cycle forecast is wrong, the portfolio holds the wrong sectors. It also pays for high turnover.

### Growth investing

Growth managers buy companies with above-average earnings growth. These stocks often have high P/E ratios and low dividends, because the price already includes the expected growth. The manager pays a premium for that growth. Gains are large when the growth delivers. If growth slows, both the earnings and the P/E multiple can fall, so the price drops sharply.

### Value investing

Value managers buy companies that look cheap on fundamentals (ratios: see 4.7):

- low P/E;
- low price-to-book;
- high dividend yield.

Low expectations give a margin of safety. The main danger is a "value trap": a stock that is cheap because its business is in permanent decline. Value can also lag for long periods.

### Market timing

Market timers move money between equities and cash or bonds on market forecasts. Of all the techniques, market timing depends most on forecasts. If the forecast is right, the timer avoids some declines. But a timer must be right twice: when to exit and when to re-enter. Out of the market, the timer can miss a few very strong days. Those few days give a large part of long-run returns.

Market timing is the extreme form of tactical allocation (see 6.1). The timer makes large, frequent switches between equities and cash on market forecasts. A tactical tilt is a smaller move inside set ranges around the strategic mix.

> **Exam trap:** A manager who picks stocks for low P/B and high yield regardless of sector is a *bottom-up value* manager, not a sector rotator.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Three fund managers describe their methods:
> - Ignatius Dumont forecasts lower interest rates, then overweights the sectors that gain most from lower rates.
> - Florentine Oduya buys any stock with a low price-to-book ratio and a high dividend yield, in any sector.
> - Zubair Kingsley cuts his equity weight from 90% to 40% because he expects a recession.
>
> **Question.** Which technique does each manager use?
>
> **Resolution.** Dumont is top-down, and his sector shifts are sector rotation. Oduya is a bottom-up value manager, because company ratios drive her choices. Kingsley is a market timer, because he makes a large switch out of equities on a forecast. He must be right about the recession and also about when to return to equities.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Delphine Okonedo holds only a growth fund. Its stocks trade at high P/E multiples, and economists forecast slower earnings growth.
>
> **Question.** What risk does her style concentration create?
>
> **Resolution.** If earnings disappoint, the price can fall from two sources: lower earnings and a lower P/E multiple. Her whole portfolio carries this one style risk. Her RR can add a value fund or a broad index fund, so that one style does not decide all of her results.

## 6.11 Passive equity management techniques

Passive management accepts the market return and does not try to beat it. It rests on two ideas: markets are hard to beat (see 6.9), and low costs add directly to the client's net return.

### Buy and hold

The manager selects a portfolio and keeps it, with few trades. Costs and taxable turnover are low. But the mix drifts and the manager does not rebalance it, so the risk changes over time (see 6.1). A buy-and-hold portfolio is not the same as an index portfolio, because the manager still selected the original securities.

### Tracking/indexing

An index manager copies a benchmark, such as the S&P/TSX Composite. There are three methods:

- **Full replication:** hold every stock in the index at its index weight.
- **Sampling:** hold a representative subset.
- **Optimization:** use a model to minimize the deviation from the index.

The gap between the fund's return and the index's return is the **tracking difference**. **Tracking error** is the variability (standard deviation) of that gap. Fees cause most of the tracking difference, because they reduce the return by about the same amount each year. Sampling, cash drag and the timing of trades cause most of the tracking error, because their effect changes from period to period. Other items, such as securities-lending income and withholding tax, also change the gap. Index funds and ETFs are the common vehicles (index fund and ETF styles: see 5.5 and 5.6).

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages:**

- low fees and turnover;
- tax efficiency;
- broad diversification and transparency;
- little risk of underperformance from poor security selection: costs and tracking error limit the underperformance.

**Disadvantages:**

- the index return less costs is the ceiling;
- there is no protection when the market falls;
- cap-weighted indices concentrate in the largest (sometimes overvalued) stocks or sectors.

> **Exam trap:** Index funds are not risk-free. They carry full market (systematic) risk. Passive management largely removes *manager (security-selection)* risk, not market risk.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Saoirse Andreou compares two ETFs on the same Canadian index. ETF X has a tracking difference of −0.10% and a tracking error of 0.05%. ETF Y has a tracking difference of −0.40% and a tracking error of 0.30%.
>
> **Question.** Which ETF does a better indexing job?
>
> **Resolution.** ETF X lags the index by less, probably because of lower costs. Its smaller tracking error also means that it follows the index more consistently. For a passive client, ETF X is the better tool.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Lorcan Valdane bought 15 stocks in his non-registered account twenty years ago and never traded them. One stock now makes up 40% of the portfolio.
>
> **Question.** What problem does pure buy-and-hold create here?
>
> **Resolution.** Without rebalancing, the best performer grew into an issuer concentration (see 6.6). His risk now depends heavily on one company. He can reduce the position and reinvest in an index fund. First, he must consider the trading costs and the taxable capital gain on the sale (see 7.10).

## 6.12 Fixed-income portfolio management techniques

Passive bond techniques aim at predictable cash flows or index returns. Active techniques try to gain from forecasts of:

- interest rates;
- yield spreads;
- price differences between bonds.

### Passive techniques

**Buy and hold.** The manager buys bonds and holds them to maturity, often in a ladder of staggered maturities. Costs are low and cash flows are predictable.

A ladder balances interest-rate risk and reinvestment risk. Some reinvestment risk remains, because a part matures each year. The ladder also keeps issuer (credit) risk. If an issuer defaults, the client does not get par back (see 6.2).

**Index matching.** The manager copies a bond index at low cost. The index holds too many issues to buy them all. So the manager usually holds a sample that matches the index in:

- duration;
- sector;
- credit quality.

The main risk is tracking error (see 6.11).

**Immunization.** The manager tries to secure a known return over a known horizon. The method sets the portfolio's (Macaulay) duration equal to the investment horizon (duration: see 2.7–2.8). If rates rise, higher reinvestment income offsets the lower prices. If rates fall, higher prices offset the lower reinvestment income. Duration drifts over time, so the manager must rebalance the portfolio periodically.

### Active techniques

**Duration management (interest rate anticipation).** If the manager expects rates to fall, the manager lengthens duration to gain from higher prices. If the manager expects rates to rise, the manager shortens duration. If the forecast is wrong, the portfolio loses more. For example, if rates rise after the manager lengthens duration, prices fall more than at the old duration.

**Bond swaps.** The manager sells one bond and buys another. Common types:

- *substitution swap:* a near-identical bond at a better yield;
- *intermarket (yield-spread) swap:* a move between sectors when a spread looks too wide or too narrow;
- *rate anticipation swap:* a change in maturity or duration to match a rate forecast;
- *tax-loss swap:* a sale that realizes a capital loss while the client keeps similar exposure.

A tax-loss swap buys a similar, not identical, bond, so that the superficial loss rule does not deny the loss (see 7.9).

**Sector rotation.** The manager moves between bond sectors: for example, from government to corporate or high-yield bonds. If the manager expects credit spreads to narrow, the manager moves toward corporate bonds. If the manager expects spreads to widen, the manager moves toward government bonds. If the spread forecast is wrong, the portfolio holds more credit risk at the wrong time.

> **Exam trap:** Immunization matches *duration*, not maturity, to the horizon. Expect rates to rise → *shorten* duration.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Obinna Duval must pay a fixed sum in seven years. He plans to buy one seven-year coupon bond to lock in his return.
>
> **Question.** Does this plan immunize him?
>
> **Resolution.** No. A coupon bond's Macaulay duration is shorter than its maturity, because coupons arrive before the final payment. To immunize, the duration must equal seven years. The simplest way is a seven-year strip (zero-coupon) bond, whose duration equals its maturity. A strip bond has no coupons to reinvest, so it needs no rebalancing. With coupon bonds, he needs a portfolio duration of seven years and must rebalance as it drifts.
