# Element 6: Portfolio construction

This element moves from single securities to whole portfolios: setting and maintaining the asset mix, measuring and managing risk, short sales and margin, the theories behind portfolio recommendations (MPT, CAPM, APT, factor models, EMH), and active and passive equity and fixed-income techniques. It carries about **13 of the 120 questions**.

Expect theory items and short scenarios (which strategy or risk applies? what happens to a margin requirement when the price moves?). The only calculations are in 6.8: CAPM expected return and portfolio beta, done step by step on the on-screen calculator. Risk measures, portfolio risk and margin are tested conceptually. Rule content sits in 6.2 (financial crime risk) and 6.5 (short sales and margin).

## 6.1 Asset allocation process

**Principles of portfolio construction.** Start from the client's objectives (safety, income, growth) and constraints (time horizon, liquidity, tax, legal or unique circumstances, risk tolerance); choose the asset mix, then securities within each class; monitor and rebalance. Asset allocation comes first because the mix, more than security selection, explains most of the variation in a diversified portfolio's returns.

**Asset mix categories.** Cash and equivalents (liquidity, safety), fixed income (income, stability) and equities (long-term growth), sometimes plus alternatives. Conservative clients lean to cash and bonds; growth clients to equity.

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| Strategic | Long-run "policy" mix with target weights and ranges, set from the client's profile; may change on a schedule over the client's life (e.g. a target-date glide path) |
| Tactical | Temporary, deliberate tilts away from the strategic mix to exploit expected market conditions (an active bet) |
| Dynamic | The mix is adjusted continuously by a rule as market values change (e.g. constant-mix or portfolio-insurance strategies) |
| Rebalancing | Restoring drifted weights to target, on a calendar or when a band (e.g. ±5%) is breached |

**Stock selection techniques.** Quantitative screening is cheap and consistent; company-by-company fundamental analysis can find mispricing but is costly; top-down and bottom-up approaches start from the economy or from the company (see 4.1, 6.10). The passive alternative is to forgo selection and index, gaining market exposure at low cost.

**Why rebalance.** Rebalancing keeps the risk level the client agreed to. Without it, the best performer grows into a larger share and risk drifts away from target; in rising equity markets that usually means more risk than agreed. It also forces selling high and buying low: a 60/40 portfolio whose equities rise 20% (bonds flat) drifts to about 64/36, so equity is sold and bonds bought.

**Implementation costs.** Each trade costs the bid–ask spread and commissions and takes time; in a taxable account it can also realize capital gains. Hence tolerance bands rather than constant rebalancing.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.2 Types of risk

| Risk | Meaning | Most exposed |
|---|---|---|
| Interest rate risk | Bond prices fall when rates rise; longer term and lower coupon mean bigger moves | Long-term bonds, preferreds |
| Inflation (purchasing-power) risk | Returns lag rising prices, so real wealth shrinks | Cash, fixed-coupon bonds |
| Liquidity risk | An asset cannot be sold quickly near fair value | Thinly traded or exempt-market securities |
| Capital risk | Value falls below the principal invested | Equities, high-yield debt |
| Income risk | Income falls: dividend cuts, or reinvestment at lower rates | Retirees living on income |
| Issuer (credit/business) risk | Problems specific to the issuer: default, downgrade, falling profits | Single-issuer holdings, lower-rated bonds |
| Financial crime risk | Exposure to fraud, exploitation, money laundering or market abuse | All accounts; see below |

> **Exam trap:** A GIC has no price risk, yet its fixed return can still lose to inflation; a bond held to maturity avoids price risk but keeps reinvestment (income) risk.

**Financial crime risk** has two sides. The investor can be the *victim* of investment fraud or of financial exploitation, which IDPC 3201 defines as "the use or control of, or deprivation of the use or control of, a financial asset of an individual by a person through undue influence, unlawful conduct or another wrongful act" (TCP and temporary holds: 1.6). Or the account can be *used* for crime, mainly money laundering. CIRO's AML guidance (GN-3200-23-001) lists indicators that warrant further investigation, for example:

- **Client:** ownership structures that hide the true beneficial owner; an account used for undeclared purposes.
- **Trading:** OTC securities (little required disclosure); stock manipulation; frequent trading in low-priced securities, especially with immediate liquidation or transfer of proceeds or no reasonable rationale; shell companies or inactive issuers.
- **Non-trading:** cash deposits or withdrawals directly with the dealer, physical certificate deposits, unexplained fee-incurring early redemptions, proceeds as bank drafts or certified cheques, accounts used only to move funds.

> **Key rule:** "The PCMLTFA requires that Investment Dealers report attempted and completed suspicious transactions." (GN-3200-23-001, s. 6.1) (Reporting: 8.2.)

*Sources: IDPC 3201 ("financial exploitation"); CIRO GN-3200-23-001 (AML Compliance Guidance), ss. 5, 6.1; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.3 Measures of risk

**Variance and standard deviation** measure total risk: how widely returns scatter around their mean. Variance is the average squared deviation of returns from the mean; standard deviation (σ), its square root, is in return units and is the figure usually quoted. For roughly normal returns, about 68% of outcomes fall within ±1σ of the mean and about 95% within ±2σ: a fund averaging 8% with σ = 10% returns between −2% and 18% in about two years out of three.

**Beta (β)** measures systematic (market) risk: how sensitive a security is to market movements. The market has β = 1. A stock with β = 1.3 tends to move about 1.3% for each 1% market move; β = 0.7 is defensive; β < 0 moves against the market. Beta says nothing about company-specific risk.

**Multi-factor measures** describe an asset's exposure ("loading") to several risk sources at once, such as market, size, value, interest rates or inflation (see 6.7).

**Drawdown** is the decline from a previous peak to a later trough, in percent. Maximum drawdown, the largest such fall in a period, shows the loss a client actually lives through: a fall from $120,000 to $90,000 is a 25% drawdown.

> **Exam trap:** Standard deviation is *total* risk (systematic + unsystematic); beta is *systematic* risk only. A single stock can have a high σ and a low β.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.4 Risk management: risk, return, hedging and diversification

**Risk in asset selection.** Investors demand a higher expected return for bearing more risk, so every asset is judged on return *per unit of risk* and on how it changes the risk of the whole portfolio, not on its own.

**Diversification** combines assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated. It reduces unsystematic (company-specific) risk at little cost but cannot remove systematic (market) risk. **Hedging** takes an offsetting position (a put on a stock held, a short futures contract, a currency hedge) to reduce a specific risk, at a cost in premiums or foregone upside.

**What drives a portfolio's expected return and risk:** the weights, each asset's expected return and standard deviation, and the correlations (ρ, from −1 to +1) between assets. Expected return is simply the weighted average. Risk is *not*: it is *less* than the weighted-average σ whenever ρ is below +1, and the lower the correlation, the bigger the reduction. Illustration: with ρ = 0.2, a 60/40 mix of equities (σ 16%) and bonds (σ 6%) has a σ of about 10.4%, versus a 12% weighted average; the gap is the diversification benefit.

> **Remember:** ρ = +1 → no risk reduction; ρ = 0 → substantial reduction; ρ = −1 → risk can, in theory, be eliminated.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.5 Short selling: process, risks and margin

**Process.** The client sells shares it does not own, expecting to buy them back cheaper; the dealer borrows shares to deliver; the client later buys ("covers") and returns them, paying any dividends and borrowing fees meanwhile. Profit = sale price − repurchase price − costs.

A UMIR short sale "means a sale of a security, other than a derivative instrument, which the seller does not own". Borrowed shares do not count as owned unless the seller is otherwise deemed to own the security (e.g. bought but not yet received). Under UMIR 3.2(1), a Participant or Access Person may not enter an order on a marketplace that would be a short sale (order type: 8.3):

- unless it is **marked short** (short-marking exempt orders excepted);
- if the security is a **Short Sale Ineligible Security** at the time of entry (limited exceptions in UMIR 3.2(3));
- for a **Pre-Borrow Security**, unless borrowing arrangements have been made before the order is entered.

> **Key rule:** "a Participant or Access Person must have a reasonable expectation to settle any resulting trade on the date contemplated on the execution of the trade." (UMIR 3.3(1))

**Risks.** Unlimited loss (the price can rise without limit; the gain is capped at the sale price); recall of the borrowed shares; a short squeeze; dividend and borrowing costs; margin calls as the price rises.

**Margin.** Before letting a client trade on margin, the dealer must ensure the client "is aware of the risks and benefits associated with trading on margin" (IDPC 3246). IDPC 5113 sets the loan value formulas:

```
Long:  loan value = (100% − margin rate) × market value
Short: loan value = (100% + margin rate) × (negative) market value
```

A net positive loan value permits a debit no larger than it; a net negative loan value requires a credit at least equal to it. So at a 50% rate, $20,000 of stock held long supports a $10,000 loan, while a short needs a credit of 150% of the shares' value, which rises as the price rises.

Minimum client rates for Canada and U.S. equities eligible for margin, listed or unlisted (IDPC 5310, applied by 5311); the same for long and short positions except below $0.25:

| Price per share | Margin rate |
|---|---|
| ≥ $2.00 and qualifying for inclusion on CIRO's List of Securities Eligible for Reduced Margin | 30% |
| ≥ $2.00, other | 50% |
| $1.75–$1.99 | 60% |
| $1.50–$1.74 | 80% |
| $0.25–$1.49 | 100% |
| below $0.25 | long 100%; short $0.25 per share |

Within 20 business days an undermargined account must be restricted to trades that reduce the deficiency, until it is fully margined. No funds or securities may be advanced while it is undermargined, or if the advance would make it so (Form 1, Schedule 4, Note 5).

> **Exam trap:** The short-sale proceeds stay in the account as part of the required credit; they are not free cash.

*Sources: UMIR 1.1 "short sale"; UMIR 3.2(1)–(3); UMIR 3.3; IDPC 3246; IDPC 5113; IDPC 5310–5311; IDPC Form 1, Schedule 4, Note 5; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.6 Portfolio recommendation concepts

**Modern portfolio theory (MPT), or mean–variance theory** (Markowitz), assumes investors are risk averse and judge portfolios by expected return and variance. Combining imperfectly correlated assets yields an **efficient frontier**: the set of portfolios with the highest expected return for each level of risk. Investors pick the frontier point that matches their risk tolerance; anything below it is inefficient.

**Efficient vs naïve diversification.** Efficient diversification chooses weights using expected returns, standard deviations and correlations. Naïve diversification simply spreads money across many holdings, often equally (the "1/N" approach), without regard to correlations. It still cuts unsystematic risk as holdings increase (with diminishing benefit), but many correlated names (ten bank stocks) leave **industry concentration**, and one oversized holding leaves **issuer concentration**.

**Black–Litterman model.** Plain mean–variance optimization is very sensitive to its return estimates and often produces extreme, concentrated weights. Black–Litterman starts from the market's equilibrium (implied) returns, backed out of market-capitalization weights, then blends in the manager's views, each weighted by confidence. The result is a more stable allocation that departs from market weights only where the manager holds views.

**Monte Carlo simulation** runs thousands of random return paths from assumed return, volatility and correlation inputs and reports a *distribution* of outcomes (e.g. "78% probability the savings last to age 95") rather than one straight-line projection. It is only as good as its assumptions.

> **Exam trap:** Black–Litterman's starting point is *market equilibrium returns*, not historical averages; Monte Carlo produces probabilities, not a guaranteed result.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.7 Asset pricing models

| Model | Explains expected return by | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| CAPM | One factor: beta to the market portfolio | Simple, intuitive; the standard for required return | Unrealistic assumptions (borrow/lend at risk-free rate, no taxes or costs); true market portfolio unobservable; single beta often explains returns poorly |
| APT (Ross) | Several systematic factors (e.g. inflation, GDP growth, interest rates, credit spreads), via no-arbitrage | Fewer assumptions; flexible | Does not name the factors or how many; factor choice is subjective |
| Fama–French five-factor | Market, size (SMB), value (HML), profitability (RMW: robust minus weak), investment (CMA: conservative minus aggressive) | Explains more cross-sectional return variation than CAPM | More data and estimation; factors are empirical, and their premiums can fade |
| Carhart four-factor | Fama–French three factors (market, SMB, HML) + momentum (winners minus losers) | Captures momentum; widely used to judge fund managers | Momentum is volatile and costly to trade |
| FF5 + momentum | Five factors + momentum (six in all) | Broadest coverage of known anomalies | Most complex; risk of data-mining |

**Purpose.** All of these models estimate a required or expected return. Managers use them to set discount rates, judge whether a security is fairly priced, and evaluate whether a manager's excess return ("alpha") is skill or simply exposure to known factors.

> **Exam trap:** Carhart = *three* Fama–French factors + momentum (4). Fama–French five-factor adds profitability and investment, *not* momentum. Adding momentum to five gives six.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.8 Applying the CAPM

The CAPM gives the return an investor should require for a security's systematic risk. Plotted against beta, it is the **security market line (SML)**.

```
E(Rᵢ) = R_f + βᵢ × (E(Rₘ) − R_f)
R_f = risk-free rate (e.g. T-bill yield); E(Rₘ) = expected market return;
(E(Rₘ) − R_f) = market risk premium; βᵢ = the asset's beta
Portfolio beta: βₚ = Σ wᵢ × βᵢ
```

> **Worked example**
> R_f = 3%, E(Rₘ) = 8%, so the market risk premium = 5%. Northfield Ltd. has β = 1.2.
> E(R) = 3 + 1.2 × 5 = **9%**.
> An analyst forecasts 10% for Northfield. Forecast > required, so the stock plots *above* the SML: undervalued, with expected (ex-ante) alpha = 10 − 9 = +1% (measured after the fact, the same comparison is Jensen's alpha, 9.3). Buy. A forecast below 9% would plot below the SML (overvalued).
> **Portfolio:** 50% Northfield (β 1.2), 30% a utility (β 0.8), 20% T-bills (β 0).
> βₚ = 0.5(1.2) + 0.3(0.8) + 0.2(0) = 0.84; E(Rₚ) = 3 + 0.84 × 5 = **7.2%**.

A portfolio's beta is its measure of market risk: β = 0.84 means the portfolio should move about 84% as much as the market. To raise expected return, the manager must accept more beta (more market risk). CAPM rewards only systematic risk, because unsystematic risk can be diversified away for free.

> **Exam trap:** Multiply beta by the *premium* (E(Rₘ) − R_f), not by the market return itself, and remember to add back R_f.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.9 The efficient markets hypothesis

The EMH holds that prices reflect available information, so consistently beating the market after costs and risk adjustment is not possible with that information.

| Form | Prices reflect | Strategy that cannot earn excess returns |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | All past price and volume data | Technical analysis |
| Semi-strong | All publicly available information (includes weak) | Fundamental analysis of public data |
| Strong | All information, public and private (includes both) | Even insider information |

**Implications for portfolio management.** The more efficient the market, the stronger the case for **passive, low-cost indexing**, since active research is unlikely to pay for itself. The manager's value then lies in setting the asset mix, managing risk, controlling costs and taxes, and fitting the portfolio to the client. Large, heavily followed markets are generally viewed as close to semi-strong efficient; less-followed segments (small caps, some bonds) may leave more room for active managers.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.10 Active equity management techniques

| Technique | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top-down | Economy → sectors → companies | Captures macro and sector trends | Good companies in "wrong" sectors are missed |
| Bottom-up | Company fundamentals first, little regard to the economy | Finds individual mispricings | Sector or macro risk can be overlooked |
| Sector rotation | Overweight sectors expected to lead at the next business-cycle phase | Can add value at turning points | Depends on forecasting the cycle; high turnover |
| Growth investing | Firms with above-average earnings growth, often high P/E and low dividends | Large gains when growth delivers | Sharp falls on disappointing earnings; pays a premium |
| Value investing | Firms cheap on fundamentals: low P/E, low price-to-book, high dividend yield | Margin of safety; lower expectations built in | "Value traps"; can lag for long periods |
| Market timing | Shift between equities and cash/bonds on market forecasts | Avoids some declines if right | Must be right twice (exit and re-entry); missing a few strong days hurts badly |

A common textbook rotation pattern (actual leadership varies): cyclicals such as consumer discretionary and financials in early expansion; energy and materials late; defensives (staples, utilities, health care) in contraction (sector traits and the cycle: 4.12, 4.14).

**Analyze-level comparison.** Top-down and sector rotation suit a manager with a strong macro view. Bottom-up suits a stock-picker. Growth and value are *styles* and can be run top-down or bottom-up. Market timing is the purest forecasting bet.

> **Exam trap:** A manager who picks stocks for low P/B and high yield regardless of sector is a *bottom-up value* manager, not a sector rotator.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.11 Passive equity management techniques

**Buy and hold:** select a portfolio and keep it, with minimal trading. Costs and taxable turnover are low, but the mix drifts and is not rebalanced.

**Indexing (tracking):** replicate a benchmark such as the S&P/TSX Composite. A manager can do this by:

- **full replication:** hold every index constituent at its index weight;
- **sampling:** hold a representative subset;
- **optimization:** use a model to minimize deviation from the index.

The gap between the fund's and the index's returns is the **tracking difference**; **tracking error** is the variability (standard deviation) of that gap. Both arise from fees, cash drag, sampling and rebalancing costs. Index funds and ETFs are the common vehicles (index fund and ETF styles: 5.6).

**Advantages:** low fees and turnover, tax efficiency, broad diversification, transparency, and little risk of underperforming because of poor security selection (underperformance is limited to costs and tracking error). **Disadvantages:** the index return less costs is the ceiling; there is no protection in a falling market; and cap-weighted indices concentrate in the largest (sometimes overvalued) stocks or sectors.

> **Exam trap:** Index funds are not risk-free. They carry full market (systematic) risk. Passive management largely removes *manager (security-selection)* risk, not market risk.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 6.12 Fixed-income portfolio management techniques

**Passive techniques**

- **Buy and hold:** buy bonds and hold them to maturity, often in a ladder of staggered maturities. Low cost and predictable cash flows; reinvestment risk remains.
- **Index matching:** replicate a bond index, usually by sampling across duration, sector and credit quality, because the index holds too many issues to buy them all.
- **Immunization:** lock in a return over a known horizon by setting the portfolio's (Macaulay) duration (2.7–2.8) equal to the investment horizon. If rates rise, lower prices are offset by higher reinvestment income, and vice versa. Duration drifts over time, so the portfolio needs periodic rebalancing.

**Active techniques**

- **Duration management (interest rate anticipation):** lengthen duration when rates are expected to fall, to gain from rising prices; shorten it when rates are expected to rise.
- **Bond swaps:** sell one bond and buy another. Common types:
  - *substitution swap:* a near-identical bond at a better yield;
  - *intermarket (yield-spread) swap:* move between sectors when a spread looks too wide or too narrow;
  - *rate anticipation swap:* change maturity or duration to match a rate forecast;
  - *tax-loss swap:* realize a capital loss while keeping similar exposure; the replacement must not be identical property, or the superficial loss rule denies the loss (see 7.9).
- **Sector rotation:** shift between government, provincial, corporate and high-yield bonds as credit spreads are expected to narrow (move toward corporates) or widen (move toward governments).

> **Exam trap:** Immunization matches *duration*, not maturity, to the horizon. Expecting rates to rise → *shorten* duration.

*Sources: CRA superficial loss page; standard finance concept (verified)*
