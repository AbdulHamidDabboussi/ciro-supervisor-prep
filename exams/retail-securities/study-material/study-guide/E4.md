# Element 4: Securities analysis

This element covers the information that a Registered Representative (RR) uses to form a view on an investment. It has four parts:
- disclosure documents and the four financial statements (4.1–4.5);
- financial ratios and value ratios (4.6, 4.7);
- market data, trading restrictions, indices and benchmarks (4.8–4.10);
- analysis methods, sectors, the economy and time horizons (4.11–4.14).

The syllabus allots it **14 of 120 questions** (indicative). Expect short calculations on a basic calculator:
- a listed financial or value ratio (4.6, 4.7);
- a portfolio's return against its benchmark (4.10).

Expect also Analyze-level questions on a trend or a gap against peers.

**Terms.** "Company" means the business that you analyze; the rules say "issuer". "Dealer" means the RR's Investment Dealer. "Client" means the RR's client. The syllabus's "earnings statement" (4.2) is the statement of comprehensive income.

## 4.1 Company analysis and sources of information

Company analysis asks whether a company is a good investment at its current price. **Quantitative** analysis uses numbers: statements, ratios and trends (4.2–4.7). **Qualitative** analysis judges the quality of management and of the business. You need both, because strong numbers from a weak business often do not last.

A **top-down** analyst starts with the economy and the industry (4.13, 4.12). A **bottom-up** analyst starts with the company.

### All relevant documents and sources of information

**Why disclosure rules exist.** "It is fundamental that everyone investing in securities have equal access to information that may affect their investment decisions" (NP 51-201 s. 1.1). So an analyst starts with the public record: SEDAR+ is "the official site to access public documents filed by issuers with Canadian securities regulators" (NI 51-102 s. 4.5.1).

| Document | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Annual audited financial statements | Position, performance and cash flows, with notes |
| Interim financial report | The same statements for each quarter |
| MD&A | Management's discussion of results; filed with each set of statements |
| Annual Information Form (AIF) | Annual overview; required of non-venture issuers |
| News releases, material change reports | Significant developments |
| Information circular, prospectus | Matters for a shareholder vote; a new issue |

**Material change.** A material change is "a change in the business, operations or capital of the reporting issuer that would reasonably be expected to have a significant effect on the market price or value" of its securities (NI 51-102 s. 1.1). It also covers a decision to make such a change, by the board or by senior management who believe that board confirmation is probable.

> **Key rule:** "the reporting issuer must (a) immediately issue and file a news release authorized by an executive officer disclosing the nature and substance of the change" (NI 51-102 s. 7.1(1)).

The company must also file a material change report "as soon as practicable, and in any event within 10 days of the date on which the change occurs" (s. 7.1(1)(b)).

**Confidential filing.** Subsection 7.1(1) does not apply in either of these cases (s. 7.1(2)):
- in the company's reasonable opinion, disclosure "would be unduly detrimental to the interests of the reporting issuer";
- the change is a senior-management decision, and both of these apply:
  - management believes that the board will probably confirm it;
  - management has no reason to believe that anyone used knowledge of it to trade.

In both cases, the company "immediately files the report required under paragraph (1)(b) marked so as to indicate that it is confidential, together with written reasons for non-disclosure" (s. 7.1(2)). Then two conditions continue:
- every 10 days, the company must tell the regulator in writing if it believes that the report should stay confidential. This lasts until the company discloses the change, or the board rejects the decision (s. 7.1(5));
- if it knows or reasonably believes that people trade with knowledge of the change, it "must promptly generally disclose the material change" (s. 7.1(7)).

"Unfavourable news must be disclosed just as promptly and completely as favourable news" (NP 51-201 s. 2.1(2)). Selective disclosure gives material nonpublic information to one or more persons, not to the public. It "can create opportunities for insider trading" (NP 51-201 s. 1.1; for tipping, see 8.2). So a private "tip" is not a valid source.

**Other sources.** Research, credit ratings and industry data are opinions, not filings. A dealer's research report "must disclose any matter which might reasonably indicate an existing or potential conflict of interest for the Dealer Member or the analyst" (IDPC 3608(1)). A conflict does not make the analysis wrong, but check it against other sources (4.11).

### Clear explanations to retail clients

A client can decide well only on information that the client understands. So explain the analysis in plain language, and link it to the client's objectives. Separate facts from opinions and forecasts, and state the main risks. A dealer must not "issue, participate in or knowingly allow the use of its name in" an advertisement, sales literature or correspondence that has either of these defects (IDPC 3602(1)):
- it "contains any opinion or forecast of future events which is not clearly labeled as such" (3602(1)(iv));
- it "fails to fairly present the potential risks to the client" (3602(1)(v)).

"Correspondence" includes "written or electronic communication, prepared for distribution to a single current or prospective client" (IDPC 1201(2); communications: 9.4). So an email to one client is correspondence.

### Collaboration with internal and external experts

If a question goes beyond your knowledge, ask an expert before you answer. A guess can cause the client to decide on wrong information. Internal experts at the dealer include:
- research analysts;
- compliance staff;
- supervisors.

External experts include tax advisers and lawyers. Record the advice, and tell the client which view comes from which source. Information barriers limit some contacts, for example with investment banking staff (1.13).

> **Exam trap:** The MD&A and AIF are management documents. The auditor's opinion covers the financial statements, not management's commentary.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ingeborg Prylutska hears a rumour that Tamsworth Minerals Corp. will announce a large discovery. The shares rose 12% today on heavy volume, with no news release. She emails her RR, Tobias Rennick, to ask whether she should buy.
>
> **Question.** What can Tobias tell Ingeborg, and which sources should he use?
>
> **Resolution.** Tobias checks SEDAR+ and finds no news release. A rumour is not a public source. The unusual price and volume suggest a leak. If Tamsworth filed confidentially, a leak with trading on it requires prompt disclosure (NI 51-102 s. 7.1(7)). Tamsworth should also ask the exchange for a trading halt until its news release (NP 51-201 s. 2.3; 4.8).
>
> So Tobias does not recommend a trade on the rumour. His email reply gives public facts and clearly labels his opinion (IDPC 3602(1)(iv)).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ferdinand Mbanefo asks RR Magdalena Elsherif about the dealer's "buy" report on Brightwater Pharma Inc., which depends on a drug trial. The report discloses that the dealer underwrote a Brightwater share issue for a fee eight months ago.
>
> **Question.** How should Magdalena handle the request?
>
> **Resolution.** Magdalena identifies the conflict. The report must disclose paid investment banking services from the dealer to the company in the 12 months before the report (IDPC 3608(2)(iv)). She asks the dealer's analyst to explain the trial risk, so that she does not guess. Then she explains the recommendation in plain language, with the risk that the trial fails.

*Sources: NI 51-102 ss. 1.1, 4.5.1, 5.1, 6.1, 7.1; NP 51-201 ss. 1.1, 2.1(2), 2.3; IDPC 1201(2), 3602(1), 3608(1), (2)(iv); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.2 Statement of financial position and statement of changes in equity

### Format, items and classification

The **statement of financial position** (balance sheet) is a snapshot at one date. It shows what the company owns and how the company finances those assets.

```
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity
```

Creditors or owners finance every asset, so the two sides always balance. Equity is the owners' residual claim after all liabilities.

The statement classifies items as **current** or **non-current**. Current items are items that the company expects to realize or settle within 12 months or one operating cycle. This split lets you test liquidity (4.6).

| Section | Typical items |
|---|---|
| Current assets | Cash, marketable securities, accounts receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses |
| Non-current assets | Property, plant and equipment (net of depreciation), intangibles, goodwill, long-term investments |
| Current liabilities | Accounts payable, accrued liabilities, short-term borrowings, current portion of long-term debt |
| Non-current liabilities | Long-term debt, lease liabilities, deferred tax liabilities |
| Equity | Share capital (preferred and common), contributed surplus, retained earnings, accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI) |

Many long-term assets, such as plant and equipment, stay at historical cost less depreciation. The company often does not record internally built intangibles, such as a brand, at all. So **book value is not market value**. For example, Maple Ridge Ltd. (4.6) has book equity of $750M, but a market capitalization of $3.15 billion (4.8).

### Statement of changes in equity

The **statement of changes in equity** reconciles each equity component from its opening balance to its closing balance:
- add net income and other comprehensive income (OCI);
- deduct dividends;
- add share issues and deduct share buybacks.

It links the other statements. Net income flows into retained earnings, and the closing totals equal balance-sheet equity. For example, Maple Ridge had opening retained earnings of $500M. It adds net income of $210M and deducts dividends of $84M, so closing retained earnings are $626M.

Dividends give the payout ratio (4.6). Share issues and buybacks change the share count for EPS.

> **Key rule:** "a reporting issuer must file annual financial statements that include (a) a statement of comprehensive income, a statement of changes in equity, and a statement of cash flows" (NI 51-102 s. 4.1(1)).

The annual statements must also include a statement of financial position and notes (s. 4.1(1)(b), (e)).

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Wilhelmina Brask sees $626M of retained earnings on the Maple Ridge balance sheet. Cash is only $50M. She asks why the company borrows if it has $626M available.
>
> **Question.** What does retained earnings represent?
>
> **Resolution.** Retained earnings is the total of past profits that the company kept and did not pay as dividends. It is a source of financing inside equity, not an amount of cash. The company already used that money to buy assets such as plant and inventory.

*Sources: NI 51-102 s. 4.1(1); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.3 Statement of comprehensive income

### Purpose and content

This statement measures performance **over a period**. It starts with revenue and deducts expenses to arrive at **net income (profit)**. Then it adds **other comprehensive income (OCI)** to arrive at **total comprehensive income**.

```
Revenue − Cost of goods sold (COGS)                     = Gross profit
Gross profit − Operating expenses (SG&A, depreciation)  = Operating income
Operating income ± Other income                         = EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes)
EBIT − Finance costs (interest)                         = Earnings before tax (EBT)
EBT − Income tax                                        = Net income
Net income + OCI                                        = Total comprehensive income
```

With no other income, operating income equals EBIT (as for Maple Ridge in 4.6). Each subtotal shows one level of cost:
- **gross profit** shows how efficiently the company makes or buys what it sells;
- **operating income** shows the result of the core business;
- **EBIT** shows the result before the effect of debt financing and tax;
- **net income** shows the profit that belongs to the owners.

The margins in 4.6 divide these subtotals by revenue. Compare each subtotal with the same period of earlier years. If expenses grow faster than revenue, margins shrink, even when revenue rises. A company may show profit or loss in a separate income statement. If so, that statement "must be displayed immediately before the statement of comprehensive income" (NI 51-102 s. 4.1(3)).

### Sources of income

- **Operating revenue:** the core business, such as sales of goods, services, fees and royalties.
- **Other income:** for example interest and dividends earned, gains on asset sales, and a share of the earnings of associates.
- **Discontinued operations:** a major line of business that the company sold or closed. The company shows its result on a separate line, after profit from continuing operations.
- **OCI items:** the company records these outside profit. Examples are foreign-currency translation differences, fair-value changes on certain investments, and pension remeasurements. Most are unrealized.

Recurring operating income is usually a better guide to future earnings than one-time gains. Future earnings drive value (4.11). Other income can be large in one year and absent in the next. So analysts often separate it from operating revenue when they forecast earnings. A company that depends on other income has a weaker core business.

> **Exam trap:** The company presents OCI outside profit and generally accumulates it in AOCI. It may transfer some items, such as pension remeasurements, to retained earnings. EPS uses profit attributable to common shareholders, not total comprehensive income.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Ptarmigan Foods Inc. reports that net income doubled this year, and client Caspian Dube wants to buy. The notes show a large gain on the sale of a division. Operating income did not change.
>
> **Question.** Does the profit increase show a stronger business?
>
> **Resolution.** No. The increase comes from a one-time gain on the sale (other income or discontinued operations), not from the core business. The gain will not recur, and the division's own earnings also stop. So next year's net income will probably fall. A P/E on this year's EPS will look low for the wrong reason (4.7).

*Sources: NI 51-102 s. 4.1(3); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.4 Statement of cash flows

### Purpose

Profit is not cash. Accrual accounting records revenue when the company earns it, and expenses when the company incurs them. Cash can enter or leave at a different time. The cash flow statement explains the change in cash over the period, in three sections:

| Section | Inflows | Outflows |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating** | Cash from customers | Payments to suppliers, employees, taxes |
| **Investing** | Proceeds from selling assets or investments | Capital expenditures (capex), acquisitions, buying investments |
| **Financing** | Issuing shares or debt | Repaying debt, buying back shares, paying dividends |

### Cash flow from operating activities

Most companies use the **indirect method** for operating cash flow:
1. Start with net income.
2. Add back non-cash charges, such as depreciation and amortization.
3. Adjust for changes in working capital.

An increase in receivables or inventory uses cash, so you subtract it. An increase in payables keeps cash in the company, so you add it. For example, Maple Ridge (4.6) had net income of $210M and depreciation of $80M. Receivables increased by $20M, inventory by $30M and payables by $40M. So operating cash flow is $210M + $80M − $20M − $30M + $40M = $280M.

### Cash flow from investing activities

This section shows spending on long-term assets and investments, and the proceeds from their sale. A negative number usually means that the company reinvests to grow or to replace worn-out assets. Capex from this section is part of free cash flow to equity (4.6).

### Cash flow from financing activities

This section shows cash from lenders and shareholders, and cash paid to them (debt repayments, dividends and share buybacks). A positive number means that the company raised more cash than it paid to lenders and shareholders. A negative number means that it paid more cash to lenders and shareholders than it raised. The sign of this section often depends on the company's stage:
- a young company that grows fast often shows inflows, because it raises debt or equity;
- a mature company often shows outflows, because it repays debt, pays dividends or buys back shares;
- a weak company may show inflows, because it borrows to cover operating losses.

So a financing inflow is not good or bad alone. Check what the cash pays for:
- in a young company, an inflow is normal if the cash goes into investing activities that can produce future operating cash flow;
- in a mature company, an outflow is usually healthy, because operating cash flow is large enough to pay lenders and shareholders;
- in any company, an inflow that pays for operating losses or negative operating cash flow does not fix the business. It only delays the problem. If this continues year after year, it is a warning sign.

The three sections together explain the change in cash. Maple Ridge ($ millions):

```
Operating                                      +280
Investing: capex −150, buy securities −30      −180
Financing: net borrowing +20, dividends −84     −64
Change in cash                                  +36   (cash rises from 14 to 50)
```

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Quarrytown Apparel Inc. reports higher net income for three years. But its operating cash flow is negative each year. Receivables and inventory grow faster than sales. The company borrows more each year to pay its bills.
>
> **Question.** What do the cash flows tell an analyst?
>
> **Resolution.** The profit does not become cash. Customers pay slowly, and unsold inventory increases. New debt pays for the gap, so the financing section is positive. This pattern cannot continue. Check receivables turnover and inventory turnover (4.6), and the debt terms in the notes (4.5).

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.5 Notes to the financial statements and the auditor's report

### Notes to the financial statements

The **notes** are part of the statements. They explain the assumptions and details behind the totals. They disclose:
- accounting policies and key estimates;
- debt terms (rates, maturities, covenants);
- leases and other commitments;
- contingencies, such as lawsuits;
- segment results;
- related-party transactions;
- share capital and options;
- events after the reporting date.

Many risks never appear as a balance-sheet number, but they appear in the notes. For example, debt covenants help you judge the risk ratios (4.6), and share options show possible dilution of EPS.

### Auditor's report

> **Key rule:** "Annual financial statements filed under subsection (1) must be audited." (NI 51-102 s. 4.1(2))

An audit gives users an independent opinion on management's statements. The **auditor's report** states whether the statements present fairly, in all material respects, under the applicable financial reporting framework (e.g., IFRS). There are four opinion types:
- **unmodified** (clean);
- **qualified** (fair except for a specific matter);
- **adverse** (not fairly presented);
- **disclaimer** (the auditor could not obtain enough evidence).

Even when the opinion is unmodified, a separate "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" section is a warning sign. It means that the company's ability to continue in business is in doubt.

Auditors usually do not audit interim reports. If no auditor reviewed an interim report, it must carry "a notice indicating that the interim financial report has not been reviewed by an auditor" (NI 51-102 s. 4.3(3)). NI 51-102 also sets filing deadlines (ss. 4.2, 4.4). For a "specified default", such as a late filing, the CSA regulators usually choose one of two responses (NP 11-207 s. 6):
- a failure-to-file CTO, which a regulator can issue "at any time" during the default (4.8);
- a management cease trade order, if the company applies under NP 12-203 and shows that it can comply.

> **Exam trap:** An audit is reasonable assurance, not a guarantee against fraud or error.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Imogen Castellanos owns shares of Wexcombe Biotech Ltd. The auditor gave an unmodified opinion. But the report has a "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" section. A note says that Wexcombe has cash for nine months and needs new financing.
>
> **Question.** Is the "clean" opinion a sign that the shares are safe?
>
> **Resolution.** No. The unmodified opinion means only that the statements are fairly presented. The going-concern section warns that the company may not continue without new money. A new share issue could dilute Imogen's holding, or the company could fail.

*Sources: NI 51-102 ss. 4.1(2), 4.2, 4.3(3), 4.4; NP 11-207 s. 6; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.6 Financial ratios: liquidity, risk, profitability, efficiency, equity

A single ratio means little alone, so compare it with the company's history and its peers (4.7). All ratios below use **Maple Ridge Ltd.** ($ millions; 100M common shares; no preferred shares; tax rate 25%).

| Balance sheet | | Income and cash flow | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cash | 50 | Revenue | 2,000 |
| Marketable securities | 30 | COGS | 1,200 |
| Receivables | 120 | Operating expenses | 500 |
| Inventory | 200 | EBIT | 300 |
| Net PP&E | 1,000 | Interest expense | 20 |
| **Total assets** | **1,400** | Earnings before tax | 280 |
| Accounts payable | 150 | Net income | 210 |
| Short-term bank loan | 50 | Dividends | 84 |
| Other current liabilities | 50 | Operating cash flow | 280 |
| Long-term debt | 300 | Capex | 150 |
| Deferred taxes | 100 | Net new borrowing | 20 |
| **Equity** | **750** | Purchase of marketable securities | 30 |

**Convention.** For ROA, ROIC and the turnovers, this guide uses year-end balances. If a question gives opening and closing balances, use their average.

### Liquidity ratios

Liquidity ratios ask one question: can the company pay the bills that fall due within a year? Current liabilities = 250.

```
Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities          = 400 ÷ 250 = 1.60
Quick ratio   = (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liab.   = 200 ÷ 250 = 0.80
Cash ratio    = (Cash + Marketable securities) ÷ Current liab. = 80 ÷ 250  = 0.32
```

Each ratio is stricter than the one before it. The quick ratio removes inventory, which can be slow to sell. A quick ratio below 1 means that Maple Ridge relies on inventory sales to meet current liabilities.

### Risk analysis ratios

Risk ratios measure how much the company depends on debt. Interest is a fixed cost in bad years as well as good ones. In this guide, debt means interest-bearing debt (bank loan + long-term debt = 350). Some texts use total liabilities, so read the question's definition.

```
Debt-to-equity    = Total debt ÷ Equity        = 350 ÷ 750   = 0.47
Debt-to-assets    = Total debt ÷ Total assets  = 350 ÷ 1,400 = 0.25
Interest coverage = EBIT ÷ Interest expense    = 300 ÷ 20    = 15.0×
```

Higher debt ratios mean more risk. Interest coverage shows how many times operating profit covers the interest. If profit falls, low coverage warns of a possible default.

### Profitability ratios

Profitability ratios show the profit from sales and from capital.

```
Gross margin     = Gross profit ÷ Revenue      = 800 ÷ 2,000 = 40.0%
Pre-tax margin   = EBT ÷ Revenue               = 280 ÷ 2,000 = 14.0%
Net margin       = Net income ÷ Revenue        = 210 ÷ 2,000 = 10.5%
Return on assets = Net income ÷ Total assets   = 210 ÷ 1,400 = 15.0%
ROIC = EBIT × (1 − t) ÷ (Total debt + Equity)  = 225 ÷ 1,100 = 20.5%
```

A lower gross margin points to price pressure or higher input costs. A lower net margin with a stable gross margin points to overhead, interest or tax.

### Efficiency ratios

Efficiency ratios show how well the company uses its assets. As a convention, inventory turnover and payables turnover use COGS; the others use revenue.

```
Fixed asset turnover     = Revenue ÷ Net PP&E      = 2,000 ÷ 1,000 = 2.0×
Inventory turnover       = COGS ÷ Inventory        = 1,200 ÷ 200   = 6.0×
Receivables turnover     = Revenue ÷ Receivables   = 2,000 ÷ 120   = 16.7×
Payables turnover        = COGS ÷ Accounts payable = 1,200 ÷ 150   = 8.0×
Working capital turnover = Revenue ÷ (CA − CL)     = 2,000 ÷ 150   = 13.3×
```

A higher turnover usually means better use of assets. Maple Ridge collects from customers (16.7×) faster than it pays suppliers (8.0×), which helps liquidity. Lower inventory turnover over several periods is a sign of weaker demand or obsolete inventory.

### Equity ratios

```
EPS = (Net income − Preferred dividends) ÷ Weighted avg. common shares = 210 ÷ 100 = $2.10
Dividend payout = Dividends ÷ Net income = 84 ÷ 210 = 40%
Retention rate  = 1 − Payout             = 60%
Book value per share = (Equity − Preferred equity) ÷ Common shares = 750 ÷ 100 = $7.50
FCFE = Operating cash flow − Capex + Net borrowing = 280 − 150 + 20 = $150M
```

The FCFE formula assumes that operating cash flow includes interest paid. FCFE is the cash left for common shareholders after reinvestment and debt flows. It shows whether the company can afford its dividend.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Harrow Robotics Ltd. has current assets of $600M, of which $450M is inventory. Its current liabilities are $250M. Client Beatrix Kowalchuk says that a current ratio above 2 proves that Harrow is very liquid.
>
> **Question.** Is she right?
>
> **Resolution.** No. The current ratio is 600 ÷ 250 = 2.4, but the quick ratio is (600 − 450) ÷ 250 = 0.6. Without inventory, Harrow can cover only 60% of its current liabilities. If the inventory sells slowly, Harrow may need to borrow.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Beatrix asks about Maple Ridge in a recession. Assume that EBIT falls from $300M to $120M. Interest stays at $20M, and the dividend stays at $84M ($0.84 a share).
>
> **Question.** Can the company still pay its interest and its dividend?
>
> **Resolution.**
> - Interest: coverage falls from 15.0× to 120 ÷ 20 = 6.0×. The interest is still safe.
> - Earnings: EBT = 120 − 20 = 100. Tax at 25% is 25, so net income = 75 and EPS = $0.75.
> - Dividend: payout = 84 ÷ 75 = 112%, and cover = 0.75 ÷ 0.84 = 0.89×.
> - Earnings no longer cover the dividend (4.7). If the recession lasts, a dividend cut is a real risk.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.7 Value ratios, trend analysis and external comparisons

### Value ratios

Value ratios link company data to the **market price**. Maple Ridge trades at **$31.50**, and its dividends per share (DPS) are $0.84.

```
P/E ratio               = Price ÷ EPS                   = 31.50 ÷ 2.10 = 15.0×
Dividend yield          = DPS ÷ Price                   = 0.84 ÷ 31.50 = 2.67%
Dividend cover          = EPS ÷ DPS                     = 2.10 ÷ 0.84  = 2.5×
Dividend payout         = DPS ÷ EPS                     = 0.84 ÷ 2.10  = 40%   (cover = 1 ÷ payout)
Equity per common share = Common equity ÷ Common shares = 750 ÷ 100    = $7.50
```

**Earnings per share (EPS).** EPS is the base of most value ratios (formula: 4.6). Investors watch its trend, because the market values future earnings. Diluted EPS also counts the shares that options and convertible securities could add (notes: 4.5). So diluted EPS is equal to or lower than basic EPS.

A higher **P/E** means that investors pay more for each dollar of earnings. Usually this reflects expected growth. But it can also reflect overvaluation or temporarily low earnings. **Cover** below 1.0× means that the dividend is larger than earnings, so the company may cut it. **Equity per common share** is book value per share. The market price is often above it (4.2).

### Trend analysis

**Trend analysis** compares a company with itself over several periods, ideally 3–5 years or a full cycle. Higher margins and turnover point to better operations. Higher debt-to-equity with lower coverage points to more risk. One year can mislead, because of one-time items (4.3) or the economic cycle (4.14). Check the rate of change too. For example, EPS of $1.60, $1.85 and $2.10 rises each year, but by a smaller percentage each year.

### External comparisons

**External comparisons** set the ratios against peers and industry averages in the same sector, because normal levels differ widely. For example, a utility's normal leverage would alarm you in a software company (4.12). Compare like with like:
- the same fiscal period;
- the same accounting basis;
- a similar business mix.

> **Exam trap:** A high dividend yield is often the result of a falling share price. Before you treat it as a bargain, check the cover.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Cobalt Bay Mining Corp. shares fell from $40 to $20. The company pays DPS of $1.60, and EPS is $1.20. Client Corwin Grieve wants to buy for the high yield.
>
> **Question.** Is the yield as attractive as it looks?
>
> **Resolution.**
> - Dividend yield = 1.60 ÷ 20 = 8.0%.
> - Dividend cover = 1.20 ÷ 1.60 = 0.75×. Payout = 1.60 ÷ 1.20 = 133%.
> - The company pays out more than it earns. The high yield comes from the price fall, not from a stronger business.
> - A dividend cut is a real risk, and a cut could push the price down again.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Maple Ridge trades at a P/E of 15×, and its peers at an average of 20×. Its margins are close to the peer average, and its debt is lower.
>
> **Question.** Is Maple Ridge cheap?
>
> **Resolution.** Possibly, but find the reason for the gap first. The market may expect slower growth for Maple Ridge, which would justify a lower P/E. So compare its EPS trend with the peers' trends, on the same fiscal periods. If growth and risk are similar, the lower P/E suggests relative value.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.8 Market information from exchanges and regulators; trading restrictions

### Price, volume, yields and market capitalization

**Equity data.** Exchanges and data vendors publish these data:
- last price, bid and ask;
- open, high, low, close and net change;
- **volume** (shares traded), which shows how significant a price move is (4.11);
- 52-week range, P/E and dividend yield.

**Market capitalization** measures company size:

```
Market capitalization = Share price × Common shares outstanding
Maple Ridge: $31.50 × 100M = $3.15 billion
```

Market capitalization decides a stock's weight in a cap-weighted index. Most broad indices count only the public float (4.9).

**Fixed-income data.** Bond data includes:
- the price (per $100 of face value), coupon and maturity;
- the **yield to maturity**;
- the spread over a comparable Government of Canada bond;
- the credit rating.

### Restrictions, including cease trade orders (CTOs)

A **cease trade order (CTO)** is an order under securities legislation "that one or more persons or companies must not trade in securities of a reporting issuer, whether directly or indirectly" (NP 11-207 s. 3).

**Statutory power.** Securities Acts let regulators order, in the public interest, that trading stop (e.g., Ontario Securities Act s. 127(1), para. 2). In Ontario, if a company "fails to file a record required to be filed under this Act", the Commission can order this without a hearing (s. 127(4.1)). After the filing, it must revoke the order "as soon as practicable" (s. 127(4.3)). Exception: if the order is more than 90 days old, the Commission can first ask for more records or information. It can do so only if it considers this in the public interest (s. 127(4.6)).

**Why failure-to-file CTOs exist.** Disclosure rules exist "so that there is information in the marketplace to enable investors and prospective investors to make an informed investment decision" (NP 11-207 s. 1). For "specified defaults", "the CSA regulators generally respond by issuing a failure-to-file cease trade order" (s. 7). The cost: "existing investors may be unable to sell their securities and prospective investors are unable to purchase securities of the issuer" (s. 7).

**UMIR trading ban.** Under UMIR, "No order for the purchase or sale of a security or a derivative shall be executed on a marketplace or over-the-counter" while any of these applies (UMIR 9.1(1)):
- "an order of a securities regulatory authority to cease trading in the security, derivative, related security or related derivative remains in effect" (9.1(1)(a));
- for a listed security, the listing exchange's Market Regulator "has halted or suspended trading" (9.1(1)(b));
- for a quoted security, the QTRS Market Regulator has halted or suspended trading (9.1(1)(c));
- for any other security, a Market Regulator of an ATS has halted trading for public dissemination of material information (9.1(1)(d)).

A parallel ban applies during a **regulatory delay** (9.1(2)). If a CSA regulator issues a CTO for a company whose securities trade on a marketplace, the market regulator acts. It "imposes a regulatory halt on trading of those securities on all marketplaces" for which it is the regulation services provider (NP 11-207 s. 12, which names IIROC, now CIRO).

**Non-regulatory stops.** In these cases, an order can still be entered and trade on a marketplace (9.1(3)):
- the Exchange or QTRS suspended trading "by reason only that the issuer" failed listing or quotation requirements, or did not pay listing fees (9.1(3)(a));
- the Exchange or QTRS delayed or halted trading because of "technical problems affecting only the trading system of the Exchange or QTRS", or under a Marketplace Rule (9.1(3)(b)).

**CTO versus halt.** A CTO comes from a securities regulator. A **regulatory halt** comes from the market regulator (CIRO). An exchange can also halt or delay trading for technical or marketplace reasons, and that stop is not regulatory (9.1(3)). If confidential material news leaks, the company should ask the exchange "that trading be halted pending the issuance of a news release" (NP 51-201 s. 2.3).

> **Exam trap:** A CTO does not always stop every sale. A Participant may "execute a sale in the security on a foreign organized regulated market" (UMIR 9.1(4)(b)). The sale must meet the CTO's conditions and securities legislation.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Pinecrest Energy Corp. did not file its annual audited statements on time. A securities regulator issued a failure-to-file CTO. Client Phoebe Mbeki asks her RR to sell her Pinecrest shares on the TSX today.
>
> **Question.** Can the RR sell the shares?
>
> **Resolution.** No. While the CTO is in effect, nobody can execute an order on a marketplace or over-the-counter (UMIR 9.1(1)(a)). A sale on a foreign organized regulated market is possible only under 9.1(4)(b). The CTO stays until the regulator revokes it. In Ontario, revocation follows the filing of the missing record, subject to the 90-day exception.

*Sources: UMIR 9.1(1)–(4); NP 11-207 ss. 1, 3, 7, 12; Ontario Securities Act s. 127(1), (4.1), (4.3), (4.6); NP 51-201 s. 2.3; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.9 Market indices: construction, weighting and types

An index gives one number for the value of a whole market or market segment. Investors use indices for these purposes:
- to summarize how a market moved over a period (4.10);
- to measure a portfolio's performance against a benchmark (4.10);
- as the basis of index funds and index ETFs;
- to measure a portfolio's market risk, for example its beta against an index.

### How index values are constructed

An **index** tracks the value of a basket of securities relative to a **base period**. The provider starts the index at a round base value, such as 100 or 1,000. For a cap-weighted index, the idea is:

```
Index level = Current market value of the basket ÷ Base-period market value × Base value
```

So a level of 1,250 on a base of 1,000 means that the basket's value rose 25% since the base period. In practice, the provider divides the current market value by a **divisor**. It adjusts the divisor when constituents change, or after a corporate action such as a share issue. Then only changes in market prices move the index level.

### Index versus average versus multi-factor

- **Index:** a relative number against a base value, as above.
- **Average:** it starts as a simple mean of prices. After divisor adjustments for splits and substitutions, analysts quote its level in points, and higher-priced stocks dominate.
- **Multi-factor index:** also called a factor or "smart beta" index. It weights or selects constituents by characteristics such as value, size, momentum, quality or low volatility, not by market cap alone.

### Market-value-weighted versus price-weighted versus equal-weighted

| Weighting | Weight of each stock | Bias |
|---|---|---|
| Price-weighted | Proportional to share price | High-priced stocks dominate, regardless of company size |
| Market-value (cap)-weighted | Proportional to market capitalization | Largest companies dominate; most broad indices, e.g., S&P/TSX Composite |
| Equal-weighted | Same for every stock (needs rebalancing) | Tilts toward smaller companies |

In a price-weighted index, a $1 move in a $100 stock moves the index as much as a $1 move in a $10 stock. But for the $100 stock, $1 is a far smaller percentage change.

Most broad indices, including the S&P/TSX Composite, are **float-adjusted**. They count only shares available to the public, not blocks held by insiders or strategic holders.

### Price return versus total return

**Price return** counts price changes only. **Total return** also reinvests dividends and interest. For example, a $50 stock falls to $45 and pays a $1 dividend. Its price return is −10%, but its total return is (45 + 1 − 50) ÷ 50 = −8%. Use total return to compare an investment that pays income with one that does not.

### Asset class, sector, country, international

Indices cover four kinds of market:
- **asset classes:** equity, and bonds (e.g., a Canadian universe bond index);
- **sectors:** e.g., energy, financials (4.12);
- **countries:** e.g., the S&P/TSX Composite for Canada;
- **international regions:** e.g., MSCI EAFE, MSCI World.

> **Exam trap:** A rise in a price-weighted index can come from a single high-priced stock, while the cap-weighted index of the same stocks falls.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.10 Using indices for market summaries and benchmarks

### Market summaries

A **market summary** uses indices to describe what happened, for example: "The S&P/TSX Composite rose 1.2% this week, led by energy, while bond indices fell as yields rose." Quote the correct index for each market and give the period. Say whether you mean price return or total return. If you know the main cause of the move, give it, for example a rate decision (4.13). Keep facts separate from your opinion about what comes next (4.1).

### Performance versus a benchmark

A benchmark shows whether the RR or the portfolio manager added value. A good **benchmark** has these properties (full criteria: 5.9):
- **appropriate:** the same asset class, region, style and currency as the mandate (a U.S. index in U.S. dollars does not fit a Canadian mandate);
- **investable:** the client could buy it through an index fund, so it is a real alternative;
- **measurable:** its return is published for the same periods;
- **specified in advance:** the client and the RR choose it before the period starts, so nobody picks an easy benchmark after the results.

The benchmark stands for the passive choice that the client had. If the client could not invest in the benchmark, a gap against it does not show a real gain or loss. If someone changes the benchmark after the period, the comparison loses its meaning. A positive relative return shows outperformance, and a negative one shows underperformance.

For a balanced portfolio, use a **blended benchmark**, weighted like the target asset mix.

```
Relative (active) return = Portfolio return − Benchmark return
Blended benchmark = Σ (Weight × Index return)
```

Note: "excess return" often means return above the risk-free rate (9.3). Check the question's definition.

> **Worked example**
> A 60/40 balanced portfolio returned 7.2%. Over the same year, the Canadian equity total-return index gained 8.5%, and the bond universe total-return index gained 3.0%.
> Blended benchmark = 0.60 × 8.5% + 0.40 × 3.0% = 5.1% + 1.2% = **6.3%**.
> Relative return = 7.2% − 6.3% = **+0.9 percentage points** (outperformed).
> A comparison with the equity index alone (−1.3 points) would wrongly suggest underperformance.

> **Exam trap:** If you compare a portfolio's total return with a price-return index, the portfolio looks better by about the index's dividend yield. Use a total-return benchmark.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** RR Dmitri Arkwright recommends the holdings in a Canadian dividend stock portfolio for his client Gwendolyn Osei. Its total return in the year was 4.0%. The S&P/TSX Composite price return was 3.0%, and its total return was 5.5%.
>
> **Question.** Did the portfolio outperform, and which index is the right comparison?
>
> **Resolution.**
> - Against the price return: 4.0% − 3.0% = +1.0 percentage point. Against the total return: 4.0% − 5.5% = −1.5 percentage points.
> - The portfolio's 4.0% includes dividends, so the total-return version is fairer than the price-return version.
> - But the broad Composite does not match a dividend style. A Canadian dividend total-return index matches the mandate better (5.9).

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.11 Methods of assessing stock market behaviour

### The importance of different sources

No single source is complete:
- filings can be late;
- research can have conflicts (4.1);
- market data shows only what investors do now.

So analysts combine all three, and cross-check their conclusions. If two methods disagree, find the reason before you act.

### Fundamental, quantitative and technical analysis

**Fundamental analysis** estimates a company's **intrinsic value** from its statements, management, industry and the economy. It assumes that the price moves toward intrinsic value over time. It suits long-term investors. Its main limit: the value depends on forecasts, and the price can differ from it for a long time.

**Quantitative analysis** uses statistical models on large data sets. Common tools:
- factor screens, for example a value screen or a momentum screen;
- regression;
- beta and correlation.

For example, a momentum screen ranks stocks by their recent returns. It assumes that relationships in past data continue. Its main limit: a relationship can stop.

**Technical (statistical) analysis** studies price and volume history. It assumes that prices adjust gradually to information, so they move in trends and patterns repeat. Common tools:
- a **moving average** smooths prices, for example over 200 days; a price above it suggests an uptrend;
- **support and resistance** are price levels where earlier moves stopped;
- **breadth** (advance-decline) shows whether many stocks join a market move.

Technical analysis helps with timing. Its main limit: a trend can reverse suddenly.

### Assumptions and valuation approaches

Fundamental **valuation approaches** include:
- **Discounted cash flow:** value is the present value of expected future cash flows. So it depends heavily on the forecasts and on the discount rate.
- **Dividend discount model:** value is the present value of expected dividends. The constant-growth version assumes steady dividend growth below the required return. So it suits mature dividend payers (calculation: 3.10).
- **Relative valuation:** P/E or price-to-book against peers (4.7). It assumes that the peer group is fairly priced.

If a dealer "sets a price target in a research report", it "must disclose, in that research report, the valuation method used" (IDPC 3616(1)). The reader can then test the assumptions behind the target.

The **efficient markets hypothesis** questions whether technical and fundamental analysis can earn returns above what their risk justifies (abnormal returns). See 6.9.

> **Exam trap:** Technical analysts do not need financial statements. Quantitative analysts may use fundamental data, but they process it statistically.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Three analysts examine Glenhollow Rail Corp. A fundamental analyst says the shares are 20% below intrinsic value. A technical analyst sees a downtrend on higher volume. A quantitative screen ranks the stock low on momentum.
>
> **Question.** How should an RR use these opposite views for a client?
>
> **Resolution.** The views answer different questions. The fundamental view is about long-term value. The other two say that the market does not agree yet. The RR checks the forecasts behind the intrinsic value, and searches for news behind the downtrend. Then the RR explains the timing risk to the client.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A dealer's report sets a $60 price target on Larchmont Utilities Inc., which trades at $48. Client Sunniva Okafor asks whether the target is reliable.
>
> **Question.** How should her RR assess the target?
>
> **Resolution.** The RR finds the valuation method, which the report must disclose (IDPC 3616(1)). It is a dividend discount model with 5% annual dividend growth. A utility's dividends usually grow slowly, so 5% may be high. With lower growth, the model gives a lower value. So the RR explains that the target depends on that assumption.

*Sources: IDPC 3616(1); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.12 Industry and sector classification

Classification systems group similar companies, so that you can compare them. The S&P/TSX indices use the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), which has 11 sectors. The four syllabus groupings are broad and do not cover every GICS sector. **Cyclical** earnings follow the economy. **Defensive** earnings fall less in recessions.

**Consumer products.** Consumer staples (food, household goods) sell in good and bad times, so their earnings are defensive. Consumer discretionary (autos, retail, travel) follows income and confidence, so its earnings are cyclical. Investors often pay a steady P/E for staples.

**Manufacturing industries.** This grouping covers the industrials sector (including capital goods) and the materials sector. These companies have high fixed costs, so a small change in sales gives a large change in profit. Analysts often value them on average earnings over a cycle, because one year's P/E can mislead. At the top of the cycle, a low P/E can be a trap.

**Service industries.** This grouping includes these sectors:
- financials;
- utilities;
- communication services;
- health care services.

Many service companies are regulated, with recurring revenue. Utilities carry high debt and pay high dividends, so investors compare their yields with bond yields (4.13). Analysts often value banks on price-to-book, because their assets are mainly financial.

**Technologies.** Software, hardware and semiconductor companies spend heavily on research. Many pay low or no dividends. Investors pay high P/E and price-to-book multiples for expected growth. If growth slows, these multiples can fall fast.

**Other sectors.** Energy and real estate are also GICS sectors. Energy earnings follow commodity prices, and analysts often group energy with materials as "resources". Real estate pays regular income and is sensitive to interest rates. The Canadian market is concentrated in financials, energy and materials.

**How classification affects valuation.** A P/E of 25× may be normal for technology but expensive for a bank. So compare within the sector (4.7). An industry's **life-cycle** stage also drives its expected growth, payout and multiple:
- **introduction:** little or no profit;
- **growth:** fast growth, low payout, high multiple;
- **maturity:** steady growth, higher payout, moderate multiple;
- **decline:** low growth, low multiple.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Gareth Ashdown compares a software company at a P/E of 30× with a utility at 16×. He concludes that the software company is overpriced.
>
> **Question.** Is the comparison valid?
>
> **Resolution.** No. The two companies are in different sectors with different growth and risk. Investors pay more for the software company's expected growth. The utility has regulated, stable revenue and a high payout. Compare each one with its own sector peers instead.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.13 Macroeconomic factors

Markets price **expectations**. So it is a surprise against the consensus forecast that moves prices, not the news alone. The four factors act mainly through two channels: the discount rate that investors apply, and the earnings that companies expect.

So for each news item, ask two questions:
- Does it change the expected path of interest rates?
- Does it change the earnings that companies expect?

| Factor | Typical effect on bonds | Typical effect on equities |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest rates** rise | Prices fall (inverse relationship) | Valuations fall; rate-sensitive sectors (utilities, real estate) fall most |
| **Inflation** rises | Yields rise as investors expect tighter policy | Input costs rise; companies with pricing power cope best |
| **Employment** strengthens | Can push yields up if wage pressure builds | Supports consumer spending and earnings |
| **Productivity** rises | Less inflation pressure supports prices | Supports margins and earnings |

**Interest rates.** A higher rate makes future cash flows worth less today (4.11). Long-term bonds and stocks with distant earnings react most. Higher rates also raise the interest cost of companies with high debt. Consumer loans also cost more, so consumer spending slows.

**Inflation.** Investors care about **real** returns: real return ≈ nominal return − inflation. So a 6% yield with 2.5% inflation is about a 3.5% real return. When inflation rises, bond investors demand higher yields. Inflation also lowers the real value of fixed bond coupons. Companies that can raise their prices protect their margins better than others.

**Employment.** Strong employment raises household income, so spending and earnings rise. But if wages rise faster than output, inflation pressure rises, and the central bank may raise rates. Unemployment is a lagging indicator. It often continues to rise after a recovery starts.

**Productivity.** Productivity is output per hour of work. If it rises, unit labour costs can fall even when wages rise. So margins improve without higher prices. The economy can grow with less inflation, and the central bank has more room to hold rates.

> **Exam trap:** If good economic news makes rate hikes more likely, it can push stocks down.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Philippa Arundel holds utility shares and a long-term bond fund. The central bank raises its policy rate by more than economists expected.
>
> **Question.** What is the likely effect on her holdings?
>
> **Resolution.** The surprise matters, because the market expected a smaller increase. Short-term yields rise most. Long-term yields usually rise too, and the fund's long duration magnifies the price effect. So the fund probably falls. Utilities carry high debt and compete with bonds for income investors, so her utility shares probably fall too.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** A monthly report shows much stronger job growth than economists expected, and wages rise fast. The same day, stock indices fall.
>
> **Question.** Why do stocks fall on good economic news?
>
> **Resolution.** Strong jobs support consumer spending and earnings. But fast wage growth adds to inflation pressure. Investors now expect the central bank to raise rates, which lowers valuations. The rate effect outweighs the earnings effect on that day.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.14 Other factors affecting performance expectations over time horizons

The right expectation for a client depends on the client's time horizon.

**Asset class.** Cash offers low, stable returns. Bonds offer moderate returns. Equities offer the highest expected return with the widest range of outcomes. A longer horizon gives time to recover from equity declines.

**Volatility.** Standard deviation measures volatility. One-year equity outcomes are widely spread, including large losses. Over long horizons, the spread of **average annual** returns narrows. But the range of final dollar wealth does not narrow. So a short-horizon investor cannot expect the long-run average return of equities.

**Market sector.** Over months, sector leadership rotates through the cycle. Cyclical sectors lead in recoveries, and defensive sectors lose less in downturns (4.12). Over years, a sector's return depends more on its growth and on its valuation at purchase. A portfolio concentrated in one sector can differ widely from the broad market in the short term.

**Economic cycle.** The cycle has four phases: expansion, peak, contraction and trough. Leading indicators, such as stock prices and building permits, turn before the economy. Lagging indicators, such as unemployment, turn after it.

**Benchmarks.** A benchmark sets a realistic expectation for each asset class (4.10). Choose one that fits the horizon and the mandate. Over a quarter, a portfolio can differ from its benchmark by chance. Over a full cycle, the difference shows skill and costs more clearly. So judge performance over a full cycle.

> **Exam trap:** A long time horizon reduces the uncertainty of the average annual return. It does not reduce the risk of loss in any single year. Money needed within a year or two usually belongs in cash or short-term bonds.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Hamish Pettigrew needs $80,000 for a home down payment in 18 months. He wants to hold it in equities, because "stocks average about 9% a year".
>
> **Question.** Is the long-run average a reasonable expectation for his horizon?
>
> **Resolution.** No. Over 18 months, equities can show a large loss, and Hamish has no time to recover. His goal needs capital preservation, so cash or short-term bonds suit him better.

> **Scenario**
>
> **Facts.** Maelis Kalnins holds a manufacturing-sector fund. In one quarter of an economic contraction, it fell much more than the broad market. Its sector index fell about as much. She wants to sell.
>
> **Question.** Is one quarter enough to judge the fund?
>
> **Resolution.** No. Manufacturing is cyclical, so contractions hurt the whole sector. Against its sector benchmark, the fund did as expected. Judge it over a full cycle, against that benchmark.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*
