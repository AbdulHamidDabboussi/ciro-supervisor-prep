# Element 4: Securities analysis

This element covers the information available on companies, markets and the economy, and how a Registered Representative turns it into a view on an investment: disclosure documents, the four financial statements, ratios, market data and trading restrictions, indices and benchmarks, analysis methods, sectors and macroeconomic drivers. The syllabus allots it **14 of 120 questions** (indicative).

Expect short calculations (a listed financial or value ratio, a portfolio's return versus its benchmark) done step by step on a basic calculator, and Analyze-level interpretation of trends or peer gaps. Learn each formula with what a high or low value means.

## 4.1 Company analysis and sources of information

Company analysis asks whether a business is financially sound, well run and competitively positioned, and reasonably priced. It blends **quantitative** analysis (statements, ratios, trends) with **qualitative** judgement (management, competitive advantages, industry outlook, governance). A **top-down** analyst starts with the economy, then the industry, then the company; a **bottom-up** analyst starts with the company and works outward.

The main public disclosure documents of a Canadian reporting issuer:

| Document | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Annual audited financial statements | Position, performance and cash flows for the year, with comparatives and notes |
| Interim financial report (quarterly) | Same statements for the interim period |
| MD&A | Management's own discussion of results and financial condition; filed with the annual statements and each interim report |
| Annual Information Form (AIF) | An annual overview of the company and its business; required of non-venture issuers |
| News releases and material change reports | Significant developments as they occur |
| Information circular, prospectus | Background for matters shareholders vote on; disclosure for a new issue |

A **material change** is "a change in the business, operations or capital of the reporting issuer that would reasonably be expected to have a significant effect on the market price or value" of its securities. It also covers a decision to implement such a change made by the board, or by senior management who believe board confirmation is probable (NI 51-102 s. 1.1).

> **Key rule:** The issuer must "immediately issue and file a news release authorized by an executive officer disclosing the nature and substance of the change" and file a material change report "as soon as practicable, and in any event within 10 days of the date on which the change occurs" (NI 51-102 s. 7.1(1)).

Exception: if the issuer reasonably believes disclosure would be "unduly detrimental" to it, it may instead file the report marked confidential, with written reasons (s. 7.1(2)).

Filings are on SEDAR+, "the official site to access public documents filed by issuers with Canadian securities regulators" (NI 51-102 s. 4.5.1). Analyst research, credit ratings and industry data add context but are opinions, not filings.

**Explaining to retail clients:** use plain language, tie findings to the client's objectives, and separate fact from opinion and forecast. **Consulting experts:** draw on the firm's research and due-diligence staff, and refer specialist tax, legal or technical questions to qualified internal or external experts.

> **Exam trap:** The MD&A and AIF are management documents. The auditor's opinion covers the financial statements, not management's commentary.

*Sources: NI 51-102 ss. 1.1, 5.1, 6.1, 7.1, 4.5.1; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.2 Statement of financial position and statement of changes in equity

The **statement of financial position** (balance sheet) is a snapshot at one date of what the company owns and how it is financed:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity
```

Items are classified as **current** (expected to be realized or settled within 12 months or one operating cycle) or **non-current**.

| Section | Typical items |
|---|---|
| Current assets | Cash, marketable securities, accounts receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses |
| Non-current assets | Property, plant and equipment (net of depreciation), intangibles, goodwill, long-term investments |
| Current liabilities | Accounts payable, accrued liabilities, short-term borrowings, current portion of long-term debt |
| Non-current liabilities | Long-term debt, lease liabilities, deferred tax liabilities |
| Equity | Share capital (preferred and common), contributed surplus, retained earnings, accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI) |

Most assets are carried at historical cost less depreciation, so **book value is not market value**.

The **statement of changes in equity** reconciles each equity component from opening to closing balance: net income and other comprehensive income (OCI) added, dividends deducted, share issues and buybacks. It is the bridge: net income from the statement of comprehensive income flows into retained earnings, and closing totals equal balance-sheet equity. For example, for Maple Ridge Ltd. (the company used in 4.6), opening retained earnings of $500M plus net income of $210M less dividends of $84M gives closing retained earnings of $626M, the figure shown in balance-sheet equity.

> **Key rule:** Annual statements filed by a reporting issuer must include "a statement of comprehensive income, a statement of changes in equity, and a statement of cash flows" (NI 51-102 s. 4.1(1)(a)), together with a statement of financial position and notes (s. 4.1(1)(b), (e)).

*Sources: NI 51-102 s. 4.1(1); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.3 Statement of comprehensive income

This statement measures performance **over a period**. It runs from revenue down through expenses to **net income (profit)**, then adds **other comprehensive income (OCI)** to arrive at **total comprehensive income**.

```
Revenue − Cost of goods sold (COGS)                     = Gross profit
Gross profit − Operating expenses (SG&A, depreciation)  = Operating income
Operating income ± Other income                         = EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes)
EBIT − Finance costs (interest)                         = Earnings before tax (EBT)
EBT − Income tax                                        = Net income
Net income + OCI                                        = Total comprehensive income
```

With no other income, operating income equals EBIT (as for Maple Ridge in 4.6).

**Sources of income:** core **operating revenue** (sales of goods, services, fees, royalties); **other income** (interest and dividends earned, gains on asset sales, share of earnings of associates); and **OCI** items, which are recorded outside profit (e.g., foreign-currency translation differences, fair-value changes on certain investments, pension remeasurements) and are mostly unrealized. Analysts weight recurring operating income more heavily than one-time gains.

An issuer may show profit or loss in a separate income statement; if so, it "must be displayed immediately before the statement of comprehensive income" (NI 51-102 s. 4.1(3)).

> **Exam trap:** OCI is presented outside profit and is generally accumulated in AOCI (some items, such as pension remeasurements, may be transferred to retained earnings). EPS uses profit attributable to common shareholders, not total comprehensive income.

*Sources: NI 51-102 s. 4.1(3); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.4 Statement of cash flows

Profit is not cash. The cash flow statement explains the change in cash over the period in three sections:

| Section | Inflows | Outflows |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating** | Cash from customers | Payments to suppliers, employees, taxes |
| **Investing** | Proceeds from selling assets or investments | Capital expenditures (capex), acquisitions, buying investments |
| **Financing** | Issuing shares or debt | Repaying debt, buying back shares, paying dividends |

Most companies use the **indirect method** for operating cash flow: start with net income, add back non-cash charges (depreciation, amortization), then adjust for working-capital changes. An increase in receivables or inventory uses cash (subtract); an increase in payables conserves cash (add). For Maple Ridge (4.6), net income of $210M plus depreciation of $80M, less increases in receivables ($20M) and inventory ($30M), plus an increase in payables ($40M), gives operating cash flow of $280M.

A mature, healthy company usually shows positive operating cash flow, negative investing (it reinvests) and negative financing (it repays debt and pays dividends). Persistent negative operating cash flow funded by financing inflows is a warning sign.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.5 Notes to the financial statements and the auditor's report

The **notes** are part of the statements. They disclose accounting policies and key estimates, debt terms (rates, maturities, covenants), leases and other commitments, contingencies such as lawsuits, segment results, related-party transactions, share capital and options, and events after the reporting date. Many risks that never appear as a balance-sheet number surface here.

> **Key rule:** "Annual financial statements filed under subsection (1) must be audited." (NI 51-102 s. 4.1(2))

The **auditor's report** gives an independent opinion on whether the statements present fairly, in all material respects, under the applicable financial reporting framework (e.g., IFRS). Opinion types: **unmodified** (clean), **qualified** (fair except for a specific matter), **adverse** (not fairly presented), and **disclaimer** (auditor could not obtain enough evidence). A separate "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" section in the report is a red flag even when the opinion is unmodified.

Interim reports are usually not audited. If no auditor has reviewed one, it must carry "a notice indicating that the interim financial report has not been reviewed by an auditor" (NI 51-102 s. 4.3(3)). Annual statements "must be approved by the board of directors before the statements are filed" (s. 4.5(1)).

> **Remember:** Annual audited statements are due within 90 days of year-end (120 for venture issuers); interim reports within 45 days of the period end (60 for venture issuers), or sooner if filed earlier in a foreign jurisdiction (NI 51-102 ss. 4.2, 4.4).

> **Exam trap:** An audit is reasonable assurance, not a guarantee against fraud or error.

*Sources: NI 51-102 ss. 4.1(2), 4.2, 4.3(3), 4.4, 4.5(1); standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.6 Financial ratios: liquidity, risk, profitability, efficiency, equity

All ratios below use **Maple Ridge Ltd.** ($ millions; 100M common shares; no preferred shares; tax rate 25%).

| Balance sheet | | Income and cash flow | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cash | 50 | Revenue | 2,000 |
| Marketable securities | 30 | COGS | 1,200 |
| Receivables | 120 | Operating expenses | 500 |
| Inventory | 200 | EBIT | 300 |
| Net PP&E | 1,000 | Interest expense | 20 |
| **Total assets** | **1,400** | Earnings before tax | 280 |
| Accounts payable | 150 | Net income | 210 |
| Short-term bank loan | 50 | Dividends | 84 |
| Other current liabilities | 50 | Operating cash flow | 280 |
| Long-term debt | 300 | Capex | 150 |
| Deferred taxes | 100 | Net new borrowing | 20 |
| **Equity** | **750** | | |

Convention: ratios that combine a full year's flow with a balance-sheet amount (ROA, ROIC, the turnovers) use year-end balances here. Many texts use the average of opening and closing balances, so use averages when a question gives both years.

**Liquidity:** can the company pay bills due within a year? Current liabilities = 250.

```
Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities          = 400 ÷ 250 = 1.60
Quick ratio   = (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liab.   = 200 ÷ 250 = 0.80
Cash ratio    = (Cash + Marketable securities) ÷ Current liab. = 80 ÷ 250  = 0.32
```

**Risk (leverage):** debt here means interest-bearing debt (bank loan + long-term debt = 350); some texts use total liabilities, so read the question's definition.

```
Debt-to-equity   = Total debt ÷ Equity        = 350 ÷ 750   = 0.47
Debt-to-assets   = Total debt ÷ Total assets  = 350 ÷ 1,400 = 0.25
Interest coverage = EBIT ÷ Interest expense   = 300 ÷ 20    = 15.0×
```

**Profitability:** convention for ROIC: after-tax operating profit ÷ (debt + equity).

```
Gross margin   = Gross profit ÷ Revenue    = 800 ÷ 2,000 = 40.0%
Pre-tax margin = EBT ÷ Revenue             = 280 ÷ 2,000 = 14.0%
Net margin     = Net income ÷ Revenue      = 210 ÷ 2,000 = 10.5%
Return on assets = Net income ÷ Total assets = 210 ÷ 1,400 = 15.0%
ROIC = EBIT × (1 − t) ÷ (Total debt + Equity) = 225 ÷ 1,100 = 20.5%
```

**Efficiency:** convention: inventory and payables turnover use COGS; the others use revenue.

```
Fixed asset turnover   = Revenue ÷ Net PP&E      = 2,000 ÷ 1,000 = 2.0×
Inventory turnover     = COGS ÷ Inventory        = 1,200 ÷ 200   = 6.0×
Receivables turnover   = Revenue ÷ Receivables   = 2,000 ÷ 120   = 16.7×
Payables turnover      = COGS ÷ Accounts payable = 1,200 ÷ 150   = 8.0×
Working capital turnover = Revenue ÷ (CA − CL)   = 2,000 ÷ 150   = 13.3×
```

**Equity ratios:**

```
EPS = (Net income − Preferred dividends) ÷ Weighted avg. common shares = 210 ÷ 100 = $2.10
Dividend payout = Dividends ÷ Net income = 84 ÷ 210 = 40%
Retention rate  = 1 − Payout             = 60%
Book value per share = (Equity − Preferred equity) ÷ Common shares = 750 ÷ 100 = $7.50
FCFE = Operating cash flow − Capex + Net borrowing = 280 − 150 + 20 = $150M
```

The FCFE formula assumes interest paid is included in operating cash flow; if a company reports interest paid in financing, subtract the interest paid from operating cash flow first.

Interpretation: Maple Ridge turns over its receivables about 16.7 times a year but its payables only 8 times, so it collects from customers faster than it pays suppliers, which helps liquidity. A quick ratio below 1 means it relies on inventory sales to meet current liabilities; 15× coverage signals low default risk.

> **Exam trap:** A very high current ratio is not automatically good; it can mean idle cash or slow-moving inventory. Falling inventory turnover over several periods is a classic sign of weakening demand or obsolete stock.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.7 Value ratios, trend analysis and external comparisons

Value ratios link company data to the **market price**. Maple Ridge trades at **$31.50**; dividends per share (DPS) are $0.84.

```
P/E ratio        = Price ÷ EPS             = 31.50 ÷ 2.10 = 15.0×
Dividend yield   = DPS ÷ Price             = 0.84 ÷ 31.50 = 2.67%
Dividend cover   = EPS ÷ DPS               = 2.10 ÷ 0.84  = 2.5×
Dividend payout  = DPS ÷ EPS               = 0.84 ÷ 2.10  = 40%   (cover = 1 ÷ payout)
Equity per common share = Common equity ÷ Common shares = 750 ÷ 100 = $7.50
```

A higher P/E means investors pay more per dollar of earnings, usually for expected growth, but it can also reflect overvaluation or temporarily depressed earnings. Cover below 1.0× means the dividend exceeds earnings and may be cut. Equity per common share is book value, which is usually well below the market price.

**Trend analysis** compares a company with itself over several periods (ideally 3–5 years, or a full cycle). Rising margins and turnover point to improving operations; rising debt-to-equity with falling coverage points to growing risk.

> **Worked example**
> EPS was $1.60, $1.85 and $2.10 over three years.
> Year 2 growth = 1.85 ÷ 1.60 − 1 = **15.6%**. Year 3 growth = 2.10 ÷ 1.85 − 1 = **13.5%**.
> Earnings are still growing strongly, but the growth rate is decelerating.

**External comparisons** set ratios against peers and industry averages in the same sector, because norms differ widely (a utility's normal leverage would alarm you in a software firm). Compare like with like: same fiscal period, accounting basis and business mix.

> **Exam trap:** A high dividend yield is often produced by a falling share price; check the cover before treating it as a bargain.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.8 Market information from exchanges and regulators; trading restrictions

**Equity data** published by exchanges and data vendors: last price, bid and ask, open/high/low/close, net change, **volume** (shares traded; technical analysts treat heavy volume as confirming the significance of a price move), 52-week range, P/E and dividend yield. **Market capitalization** measures company size:

```
Market capitalization = Share price × Common shares outstanding
Maple Ridge: $31.50 × 100M = $3.15 billion
```

**Fixed-income data:** price (per $100 of face value), coupon, maturity, **yield to maturity**, the spread over a comparable Government of Canada bond, and credit rating. Bond prices and yields move inversely.

**Regulators** provide filings (SEDAR+) and trading restrictions. A **cease trade order (CTO)** is an order under securities legislation "that one or more persons or companies must not trade in securities of a reporting issuer, whether directly or indirectly" (NP 11-207 s. 3). Under UMIR, "No order for the purchase or sale of a security or a derivative shall be executed on a marketplace or over-the-counter" while "an order of a securities regulatory authority to cease trading in the security, derivative, related security or related derivative remains in effect" (UMIR 9.1(1)). The same ban applies while the listing exchange's Market Regulator "has halted or suspended trading" (9.1(1)(b)), with parallel bans for quoted securities and regulatory delays (9.1(1)(c), 9.1(2)). The ban does not cover non-regulatory stops, such as a suspension "by reason only that the issuer" failed to meet listing requirements or pay listing fees, or a halt for "technical problems affecting only the trading system of the Exchange or QTRS" (9.1(3)).

A CTO comes from a securities regulator, whereas a **halt** is imposed by the market regulator. If confidential material news leaks, NP 51-201 says the company's response should include "contacting the relevant exchange and asking that trading be halted pending the issuance of a news release" (s. 2.3). CTOs also enforce disclosure: for certain continuous disclosure defaults ("specified defaults"), "the CSA regulators generally respond by issuing a failure-to-file cease trade order" (NP 11-207 ss. 1, 7). Contravening the prospectus requirement through advertising or marketing "could result in a cease trade order in respect of the preliminary prospectus" (41-101CP s. 6.8).

> **Exam trap:** During a regulatory halt, delay or suspension, a Participant may "execute a trade in the security, if permitted by applicable securities legislation, outside of Canada on a foreign organized regulated market" (UMIR 9.1(4)(a)). During a CTO it may only "execute a sale in the security on a foreign organized regulated market", and only if the order's conditions are met and the sale complies with securities legislation (9.1(4)(b)).

*Sources: UMIR 9.1(1)–(4); NP 11-207 ss. 1, 3, 7; NP 51-201 s. 2.3; 41-101CP s. 6.8; standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.9 Market indices: construction, weighting and types

An **index** tracks the value of a basket relative to a **base period** set to a round number (100 or 1,000), so its level shows cumulative change. An **average** starts as a simple mean of prices, but after divisor adjustments for splits and substitutions its level is quoted in points, and higher-priced stocks dominate. A **multi-factor** (factor or "smart beta") index weights or selects constituents by characteristics such as value, size, momentum, quality or low volatility instead of by market cap alone.

| Weighting | Weight of each stock | Bias |
|---|---|---|
| Price-weighted | Proportional to share price | High-priced stocks dominate, regardless of company size |
| Market-value (cap)-weighted | Proportional to market capitalization | Largest companies dominate; most broad indices, e.g., S&P/TSX Composite |
| Equal-weighted | Same for every stock (needs rebalancing) | Tilts toward smaller companies |

In a price-weighted index, a $1 move in a $100 stock moves the index as much as a $1 move in a $10 stock, even though it is a far smaller percentage change. In a cap-weighted index, a 1% move in the largest company outweighs a 1% move in a small one. Most broad indices, including the S&P/TSX Composite, are **float-adjusted**: market value counts only shares available to the public, not blocks held by insiders or strategic holders.

**Price return** counts price changes only; **total return** also reinvests dividends and interest. A $50 stock that falls to $45 and pays a $1 dividend has a price return of −10% but a total return of (45 + 1 − 50) ÷ 50 = −8%.

Indices cover **asset classes** (equity; bonds, e.g., a Canadian universe bond index), **sectors** (e.g., energy, financials), **countries** (S&P/TSX Composite for Canada) and **international** regions (e.g., MSCI EAFE, MSCI World).

> **Exam trap:** A rise in a price-weighted index can be driven by a single high-priced stock, while the cap-weighted index of the same stocks falls. The weighting method, not just the constituents, decides the result.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.10 Using indices for market summaries and benchmarks

A **market summary** uses indices to describe what happened: "The S&P/TSX Composite rose 1.2% this week, led by energy, while bond indices fell as yields rose." Quote the right index for each market and say whether you mean price or total return.

A good **benchmark** is relevant (same asset class, region, style and currency as the mandate), investable, measurable and agreed in advance (full criteria: 5.9). For a balanced portfolio, use a **blended benchmark** weighted like the target asset mix.

```
Relative (active) return = Portfolio return − Benchmark return
Blended benchmark = Σ (Weight × Index return)
```

Note: "excess return" often means return above the risk-free rate (9.3); check the question's definition.

> **Worked example**
> A 60/40 balanced portfolio returned 7.2%. Over the same year, the Canadian equity total-return index gained 8.5% and the bond universe total-return index 3.0%.
> Blended benchmark = 0.60 × 8.5% + 0.40 × 3.0% = 5.1% + 1.2% = **6.3%**.
> Relative return = 7.2% − 6.3% = **+0.9 percentage points** (outperformed).
> Comparing with the equity index alone (−1.3 points) would wrongly suggest underperformance.

> **Exam trap:** Comparing a portfolio's total return with a price-return index flatters the portfolio by the index's dividend yield. Use a total-return benchmark.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.11 Methods of assessing stock market behaviour

No single source or method is complete, so analysts combine filings, independent research and market data and cross-check conclusions.

| Method | What it studies | Core assumption |
|---|---|---|
| **Fundamental** | Financial statements, management, industry and economy to estimate **intrinsic value** | Price will move toward intrinsic value over time |
| **Quantitative** | Statistical models: factor screens (value, momentum, quality), regression, beta and correlation | Measurable relationships in data persist enough to be exploited |
| **Technical / statistical** | Price and volume history: trends, support and resistance, moving averages, chart patterns, breadth (advance-decline), sentiment | Prices discount known information but adjust gradually, so they move in trends, and patterns recur |

Fundamental **valuation approaches** include:

- **Discounted cash flow:** value is the present value of expected future cash flows, so it depends heavily on the forecasts and the discount rate chosen.
- **Dividend discount model:** value is the present value of expected dividends. The constant-growth version assumes dividends grow at a steady rate below the required return, so it suits mature dividend payers (see 3.10 for the calculation).
- **Relative valuation:** P/E or price-to-book versus peers, which assumes the peer group is fairly priced.

The **efficient markets hypothesis** questions whether technical and fundamental analysis can earn excess returns; see 6.9 for the three forms and their portfolio implications.

> **Exam trap:** Technical analysts do not need financial statements; quantitative analysts may use fundamental data but process it statistically.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.12 Industry and sector classification

Classification systems group companies with similar businesses so they can be compared. The S&P/TSX indices use the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), which has 11 sectors. The syllabus groupings map to them as follows:

| Grouping | Examples | Valuation traits |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumer products** | Staples (food, household goods); discretionary (autos, retail) | Staples are defensive with stable earnings; discretionary is cyclical |
| **Manufacturing** | Industrials, materials, capital goods | Capital-intensive, cyclical, high operating leverage; valued on cycle-adjusted earnings |
| **Service industries** | Financials, utilities, telecom, health services | Often regulated, recurring revenue; utilities carry high debt and yields |
| **Technologies** | Software, hardware, semiconductors | High growth and R&D, low or no dividends, high P/E and price-to-book |

A P/E of 25× may be normal for technology yet expensive for a bank, so compare within the sector. **Cyclical** sectors' earnings track the economy; **defensive** sectors hold up in recessions. An industry's **life-cycle** stage (introduction, growth, maturity, decline) drives expected growth, payout and multiple. The Canadian market is concentrated in financials, energy and materials.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.13 Macroeconomic factors

Markets price **expectations**, so it is surprises relative to the consensus that move prices.

| Factor | Typical effect on bonds | Typical effect on equities |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest rates** rise | Prices fall (inverse relationship) | Higher discount rate lowers valuations; borrowing costs rise; rate-sensitive sectors (utilities, real estate) hit hardest |
| **Inflation** rises | Real return eroded; expectation of central-bank tightening pushes yields up | Input costs rise; firms with pricing power cope best |
| **Employment** strengthens | Can push yields up if wage pressure builds | Supports consumer spending and earnings |
| **Productivity** rises | Eases inflation pressure, so supports bond prices and lower yields | Higher output per hour supports margins and earnings growth |

Investors care about **real** returns: real return ≈ nominal return − inflation, so a 6% yield with 2.5% inflation is roughly a 3.5% real return. When inflation rises, a fixed coupon buys less, which is why bond investors demand higher yields.

> **Exam trap:** Good economic news can push stocks down if it makes rate hikes more likely. Unemployment is a lagging indicator; it often keeps rising after a recovery begins.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*

## 4.14 Other factors affecting performance expectations over time horizons

- **Asset class:** cash offers low, stable returns; bonds offer moderate returns; equities offer the highest expected return with the widest range of outcomes. A longer horizon gives time to recover from equity declines.
- **Volatility:** measured by standard deviation. One-year equity outcomes are widely dispersed, including large losses. Over long horizons the dispersion of **average annual** returns narrows, although the range of ending dollar wealth does not. A short-horizon investor therefore cannot count on equities' long-run average.
- **Market sector:** cyclicals lead in recoveries; defensives hold up in downturns.
- **Economic cycle:** expansion, peak, contraction, trough. Leading indicators (stock prices, building permits) turn before the economy; lagging ones (unemployment) after. Sector leadership rotates through the cycle.
- **Benchmarks:** set realistic expectations for each asset class and judge performance over a full cycle, not one quarter.

> **Exam trap:** A long time horizon reduces the uncertainty of the average annual return, not the risk of loss in any single year. Money needed within a year or two is usually better held in cash or short-term bonds, whatever equities have averaged historically.

*Sources: standard finance concept (verified)*
